# Chapter 3: The feed is built to keep you

Sana did not think she had a problem with her phone. She had a problem with time, which seemed to vanish in the evenings, and with concentration, which seemed to have gotten worse since she started university. But the phone? Everyone used their phone.

Then her screen-time report arrived one Sunday morning. Her daily average for the past week was six hours and fifty-two minutes. Three hours and ten minutes of it was in a single short-video app. She had picked the phone up 143 times a day.

Her first reaction was that the number must be wrong. Her second was to do the arithmetic. If she was awake for about sixteen hours, she was picking up the phone roughly every seven minutes. She had never once, in that week, decided to spend three hours watching videos. She'd only ever decided to watch one.

## You're not imagining it

If you find it hard to put your phone down, it's worth knowing that you're up against something designed by skilled people to be hard to put down. That's not a conspiracy theory. It's the business model.

Most social media apps and many video and news platforms make money from advertising. Advertisers pay for attention. The more time you spend in the app, the more ads you see and the more the company earns. So these companies employ large teams of engineers, designers and data scientists whose job, measured in hard numbers, is to increase how long and how often you use the product. They test changes on millions of people and keep whatever works.

What works, it turns out, are some old and well-understood features of human psychology.

## The slot machine in your pocket

In the middle of the twentieth century, the psychologist B. F. Skinner studied how animals learn from rewards. One of his findings was that rewards which arrive unpredictably, sometimes yes and sometimes no, produce more persistent behavior than rewards that arrive every time. A pigeon that gets food every time it pecks a key will peck when it's hungry. A pigeon that gets food on some pecks, at random, will peck and peck and peck.

Slot machines are built on this principle. So, whether or not anyone planned it that way, are feeds.

When you pull down to refresh, you don't know what you'll get. Maybe a message from someone you like. Maybe a funny video. Maybe nothing. When you scroll, the next post might be boring or it might be the best thing you've seen all week. That uncertainty is exactly what keeps your thumb moving. You're not scrolling for what you just saw. You're scrolling for what might come next.

## No bottoms, no edges

Older media had natural stopping points. A newspaper ran out of pages. A television episode ended and you had to decide whether to watch another. A magazine had a back cover.

Modern feeds removed those edges. Infinite scroll means there's always more below. Autoplay means the next video starts before you've decided whether you want it. Stories disappear after a day, which creates a small pressure to check before you miss them. Streaks reward you for opening an app every single day, and punish you, mildly, for missing one.

Aza Raskin, the designer usually credited with inventing infinite scroll, has spoken publicly about regretting how it's been used. He meant it as a convenience. It turned out to remove the one moment, at the bottom of the page, where people used to stop and ask themselves whether they wanted to continue.

Every time you finish something and have to choose to start the next thing, you get a small chance to stop. A lot of good design for focus, as you'll see in Chapter 6, is about putting those stopping points back.

## The cost of a buzz

Notifications are a separate problem. They don't just tempt you when you're already on your phone. They reach out and pull you in while you're doing something else.

In a 2015 study at Florida State University, Cary Stothart and his colleagues gave people a task that required sustained attention. While they worked, some received phone calls or text notifications on their own phones. They didn't answer them or even look at them. The researchers found that just receiving the notifications was enough to significantly hurt performance on the task, to a degree comparable to actually using a phone.

It makes sense when you think about it. A buzz in your pocket doesn't just make a noise. It starts a chain of thoughts: *Who's that? Is it important? Should I check? I'll check later. But what if it's...?* Even if you never pick up the phone, part of your attention has left the work.

## The checking habit

Notice how often you pick up your phone without any reason. Not to reply to something, not to look something up. You just find it in your hand, unlocked, with your thumb hovering over an app.

This is habit, and it's powerful because it's automatic. Somewhere along the way, your brain learned that small moments of boredom, discomfort or waiting can be relieved by the phone. Standing in a queue: phone. A pause in conversation: phone. A hard paragraph in your textbook: phone. The phone became the answer to every small gap in the day, and so now every gap triggers a reach.

Gloria Mark, whose interruption research you met in Chapter 1, has tracked how long people stay on one screen before switching. In her 2023 book *Attention Span*, she reports that in her more recent studies the average was around forty-seven seconds. Some of those switches are caused by notifications. Many of them aren't. People interrupt themselves, all day long, out of habit.

Many people also experience "phantom vibrations", the feeling that their phone has buzzed when it hasn't. It's a small, strange sign of how closely tuned we've become to our devices.

## Comparison and the highlight reel

There's another cost that has less to do with time and more to do with how you feel.

Social media shows you a curated selection of other people's lives: the holidays, the promotions, the engagements, the perfect bodies, the beautifully lit desks of people who claim to wake at five and do everything right. You compare this highlight reel with your own ordinary, unfiltered day. You almost always lose.

For a student like Sana, that comparison often lands at exactly the wrong moment. She opens an app for a quick break from studying, sees a classmate posting about how much she's already revised, and comes back to her notes feeling anxious and behind. The break meant to refresh her has left her worse off.

## What happens when people cut back

If social media is costly, what happens when people use less of it? A few well-designed studies give some useful answers.

In 2018, Melissa Hunt and her colleagues at the University of Pennsylvania asked a group of students to limit their use of Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to ten minutes per platform per day, thirty minutes in total, for three weeks. A comparison group carried on as usual. The students who cut back reported significant reductions in loneliness and depression compared with the other group, and the effects were largest for those who had started out more depressed.

In a much larger study, published in 2020, the economists Hunt Allcott, Luca Braghieri, Sarah Eichmeyer and Matthew Gentzkow paid some participants to deactivate their Facebook accounts for four weeks. Those who deactivated freed up about an hour a day. They spent more time offline, including with friends and family, and reported small but real improvements in happiness and life satisfaction. When the experiment ended, many of them used Facebook less than before, by their own choice. About eight in ten said the break had been good for them.

These findings don't say that social media is poison. The same Allcott study found that people valued Facebook a great deal; most of them had to be paid a meaningful sum to give it up. Social media lets you keep in touch with family abroad, find communities, learn things, run a business. Amira gets most of her customers through it.

What the studies do say is that, for many people, less is better than more, and that we tend to underestimate how much better our lives can feel with a little less.

## Whose job is it?

Here's the balance this book tries to strike.

It's not a personal failing that your phone is hard to resist. You're a human being facing a product designed by experts to hold human attention, and it's doing what it was built to do.

But understanding that doesn't get you off the hook. Nobody is going to redesign these apps to protect your study time. The companies won't, and the government probably won't, at least not soon. If you want your attention back, you have to take it back yourself.

The good news is that you don't need superhuman willpower to do it. You need to work with the same psychology that's working against you. If unpredictable rewards make apps sticky, you can remove the apps from your home screen. If notifications break your attention, you can turn them off. If the phone is the answer to every boring moment, you can give boredom a different answer. If the feed has no bottom, you can put a stopping point back with a timer or a time window.

Chapter 6 takes you through all of this, step by step. For now, the most important thing is to see clearly what's happening.

## What Sana noticed

After the shock of her screen-time report, Sana didn't delete anything. She did something smaller. For three days, every time she picked up her phone, she said the reason under her breath: "reply to Mum", "check the bus", "bored", "avoiding the essay".

By the end of the first day she was embarrassed by how often the reason was "bored" or "no reason". By the end of the third, she'd started catching her hand on the way to the phone and putting it back down before she'd unlocked it.

"I didn't know I was doing it," she said. "That was the scary part. I thought I was choosing to use my phone. Most of the time, I wasn't choosing anything."

## Try this today

Do a screen-time audit. It takes about fifteen minutes.

1. Open the screen-time or digital well-being section in your phone's settings. (On most iPhones it's under Settings, then Screen Time. On most Android phones it's under Settings, then Digital Wellbeing.)
2. Write down three numbers: your average daily screen time over the past week, the number of times you picked up or unlocked the phone each day, and the number of notifications you received each day.
3. List the top five apps by time.
4. For each of those five, write one honest sentence: *What does this app give me, and what does it cost me?*
5. Circle the app that costs you the most compared with what it gives you. You'll come back to it in Chapter 6.

Then, for the rest of today, try Sana's experiment. Each time you pick up your phone, say (or think) the reason before you unlock it.

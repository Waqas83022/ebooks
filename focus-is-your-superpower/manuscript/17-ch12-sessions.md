# Chapter 12: Work sessions and breaks

In the late 1980s, an Italian university student named Francesco Cirillo was struggling to study. He was distracted, he procrastinated, and he felt he was getting nowhere. So he made a small bet with himself: could he study, really study, for just ten minutes?

To keep himself honest, he used a kitchen timer shaped like a tomato. *Pomodoro* is Italian for tomato. Over time, the ten minutes became twenty-five, followed by a short break, and the system he developed around that timer became one of the best-known productivity methods in the world: the Pomodoro Technique.

The method's success isn't really about tomatoes, or even about twenty-five minutes. It's about something simpler. Work goes better when it comes in sessions with clear edges, separated by real breaks.

## The Pomodoro Technique

In its classic form, the technique works like this:

1. Choose a task.
2. Set a timer for 25 minutes. That's one "pomodoro".
3. Work on the task, and only that task, until the timer rings.
4. Take a 5-minute break.
5. After four pomodoros, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

Cirillo added a few rules that are easy to overlook but make a big difference. A pomodoro can't be split. If you're interrupted and have to stop, it doesn't count, and you start a fresh one later. If a distracting thought or task comes up, you write it down and return to work, as you learned in Chapter 4. And at the end of each day, you look back at how many pomodoros you completed and what got in the way.

The technique works well for many people for reasons you've already met in this book. The task is clear. The time is short enough that starting doesn't feel threatening. The timer gives you a boundary, so resisting distractions is easier because a break is always close. And the record of completed pomodoros gives you a simple measure of real, focused work.

## Not everyone's number is 25

Twenty-five minutes is a good starting point, but it isn't magic. Some people find it too short. Just as they're getting into the work, the timer rings. Others find it too long, especially at first. And different kinds of work need different rhythms.

Here are some common patterns. Try a few and see which fits you.

**25 and 5.** The classic. Good for getting started, for tasks you tend to avoid, for revision and admin, and for days when your focus is weak.

**50 and 10.** Popular with people who find 25 minutes too short. Good for writing, coding, design and other work that needs time to warm up.

**75 to 90, then 15 to 20.** For deep work by people who have built up their focus (rung five on the focus ladder in Chapter 5). Good for complex problems and creative work. Not usually sustainable more than two or three times a day.

**Work until a natural stopping point.** Some people prefer to work without a fixed timer and take a break when they reach a natural pause or notice their focus fading, then note how long the session was. This suits people who are already good at focusing and dislike being interrupted by an alarm.

You can mix them, too. Many people use short sessions in the morning to get going and longer ones once they've warmed up, or longer sessions for creative work and short ones for admin.

## Breaks are part of the work

People who are trying to get more done often skip breaks, or feel guilty for taking them. That's a mistake.

Attention is tiring. If you work for hours without stopping, your focus doesn't stay level. It sags, often without your noticing. You read the same line three times. You make more errors. You start drifting to your phone, which is your mind taking the break you didn't give it, only in a less restful form.

A planned break lets you recover before you reach that point. In 2022, Patricia Albulescu and her colleagues at the West University of Timișoara published a review of 22 experimental studies on micro-breaks, short breaks of ten minutes or less between work tasks. They found that micro-breaks reliably increased people's energy and reduced fatigue, though the effects were modest. Their effect on performance was less clear overall; it showed up mainly for less demanding tasks, and the longer the break, the larger the boost tended to be. Their conclusion was that recovering from very demanding work may need breaks of more than ten minutes.

So breaks help you feel better and keep going, and for hard thinking, a proper break is better than a tiny one.

## What makes a good break

Not all breaks are equal. Some leave you refreshed. Others leave you more tired, or pull you away from work for far longer than you meant.

**Good breaks** tend to give your mind a rest from the kind of effort the work demanded. For most desk work, that means:

- **Moving your body.** Stand up, stretch, walk around the room or the building, climb a flight of stairs.
- **Looking into the distance.** Out of a window, ideally at something green. Your eyes have been focused on a screen or a page a short distance away; let them relax.
- **Getting outside.** Even a few minutes of fresh air and daylight help many people. In a 2008 study, Marc Berman, John Jonides and Stephen Kaplan at the University of Michigan found that people who walked for just under an hour in a park-like arboretum did better on a test of attention afterward than people who walked the same length of time along busy city streets.
- **Drinking water, having a light snack, making tea.**
- **Talking to someone**, briefly, about something unrelated to work.
- **Resting your eyes**, or a few minutes of slow breathing.

**Poor breaks** tend to be ones that keep your mind busy in the same way, or that are hard to stop:

- **Scrolling social media or watching short videos.** These are hard to stop after five minutes, they keep your eyes on a screen, and they often leave you with new things to think about (attention residue again) or feeling worse (the comparison problem from Chapter 3).
- **Checking email or work messages.** This isn't a break; it's switching to a different kind of work.
- **Reading the news**, which often leaves people anxious or distracted.

You don't have to be strict about this. A few minutes on your phone is sometimes exactly what you want. But if you notice that your five-minute breaks keep turning into thirty, or that you come back from them feeling worse, try a break with no screen at all.

## Protect the break's ending

A break that doesn't end isn't a break. It's the end of the work session.

Set a timer for your breaks as well as your work. When the break timer rings, go back, even if you'd prefer not to. If you find this hard, try deciding the target for the next session before you start the break. Then, when the break ends, you know exactly what to return to, which makes returning easier.

## Longer breaks: lunch and the end of the day

Beyond short breaks, two longer ones matter.

**Lunch.** Many people eat at their desks, often while reading email or scrolling. Taking a real lunch break, away from the desk and preferably away from screens, gives your mind and body a proper rest in the middle of the day. Even twenty or thirty minutes can make the afternoon noticeably better.

**The end of the workday.** Chapter 17 covers this in detail, but the short version is: stop at a defined time, and do something to mark the end. Without a clear end, work spreads into the evening and you never fully rest.

## Choosing your rhythm

To choose the right session length and break pattern, ask four questions.

*How long can I currently focus?* Use your number from Chapter 5. Start your sessions slightly below it.

*What kind of work is this?* Creative, complex work tends to benefit from longer sessions. Repetitive or unpleasant tasks often go better in shorter bursts.

*How's my energy today?* On a tired or stressed day, shorten your sessions. On a strong day, lengthen them.

*What does my schedule allow?* If you only have forty minutes between classes, one forty-minute session may be better than trying to fit in two short ones.

Daniel found that 50 and 10 suited him best for planning and writing, but that 25 and 5 worked better for the dull admin he tended to avoid. Sana used 25 and 5 for the first fortnight of exam revision, then moved to 45 and 10 as her focus improved. Tariq, coding in the evenings after a full working day, stuck with 25 and 5: two pomodoros on weeknights, four on Saturdays.

## Count sessions, not hours

One useful side effect of working in sessions is that it gives you a better way to measure your work.

Hours at a desk are a poor measure. You can sit for three hours and do twenty minutes of real work. Completed sessions are a much better measure, because each one is a period of genuine focus on one thing.

At the end of each day, count your completed sessions. Over a week, you'll see a pattern. Most people are surprised at how few truly focused sessions they used to do, and encouraged by how quickly the number grows once they start paying attention to it. Four good sessions a day, five days a week, is twenty sessions, or around eight to sixteen hours of real focused work depending on their length. For most people, that's more than enough to make serious progress on what matters.

## Try this today

Run a rhythm experiment.

1. Choose an important task you'll work on today.
2. Do two sessions of 25 minutes, each followed by a 5-minute break with no screen.
3. Later, or tomorrow, do one session of 50 minutes, followed by a 10-minute break with no screen.
4. After each, note in your log: How focused did I feel? How much did I get done? How did I feel afterward?
5. Compare the two. Which rhythm suited this task better?

During each break, try one of the good-break ideas: a short walk, stretching, looking out of a window, stepping outside.

### Reflection

- What do I usually do on my breaks, and how do I feel afterward?
- Which session length feels right for my most important kind of work?
- How many truly focused sessions do I do on a normal day now? What would I like that number to be?

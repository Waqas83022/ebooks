# Chapter 18: Focus for students

For most of her first year, Sana studied the way almost everyone around her studied. She read her lecture notes. She reread them. She highlighted the important parts in yellow, then the really important parts in pink. The night before an exam, she read everything one more time, and it all looked familiar.

Then she sat down in the exam hall, turned over the paper, and found she couldn't answer half the questions. She recognized the topics. She just couldn't pull the details out of her head.

"I'd spent hours on it," she said. "I really thought I knew it. It was like the information was in there somewhere, but I couldn't reach it."

She'd done what millions of students do: confused familiarity with knowledge. And she'd spent many of her focused hours on study methods that feel productive but don't work very well.

This chapter applies the whole focus system to studying. But first, it's worth making sure your focused hours go into methods that work.

## Methods that work, and methods that feel like they work

In 2013, John Dunlosky and four colleagues published a long review of ten popular study techniques, looking at the evidence for each across many studies, ages and subjects. They rated each technique as high, moderate or low in usefulness.

Two techniques came out on top.

**Practice testing.** Testing yourself on the material, through flashcards, practice questions, past papers, or simply closing the book and trying to write down what you remember. This is sometimes called retrieval practice, because you're practicing pulling information out of your memory, which is exactly what an exam asks you to do.

**Distributed practice.** Spreading your study of a topic over several sessions, with gaps between them, instead of doing it all in one go. This is often called spacing.

Several popular techniques came out low, including highlighting, rereading and summarizing (as many students do it). These aren't useless, but they produce much less learning for the time they take. They also feel effective, because the material starts to look familiar. That familiarity is exactly the trap Sana fell into.

## Why testing beats rereading

In a 2006 study, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke gave students short passages to learn. Some students studied the passage several times. Others studied it once and then took practice tests in which they tried to recall what they'd read. When tested five minutes later, the students who'd restudied did slightly better. But when tested a week later, the students who'd practiced recall remembered much more.

Rereading makes material feel easy, which is satisfying in the moment. Testing yourself is harder and often frustrating, because you discover what you don't know. But that difficulty is a sign of learning. Each time you pull something out of memory, you make it easier to pull out next time.

For focus, this has a useful side effect. Practice testing is active. It gives your attention a clear job, which makes it much harder to drift than passive reading.

## Spacing: little and often

The second high-value technique, spacing, fits perfectly with the focus sessions you learned in Part Three.

Instead of studying one topic for six hours on one day, study it for an hour on six different days, spread over a couple of weeks. You'll remember more, for longer. In 2006, Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues combined the results of hundreds of experiments on spacing and found that spreading study sessions out consistently produced better long-term retention than cramming the same amount of study into one block.

This is also why cramming doesn't work well. It may get you through tomorrow's test, but much of what you crammed is gone within days, and you have to learn it all again for the final exam.

## A study session that works

Put the two techniques together with your focus skills, and a good study session looks like this:

1. **Set a target.** "Pharmacology: anticoagulants. Answer the 15 practice questions at the end of the chapter, then make flashcards for anything I got wrong."
2. **Protect the session.** Phone in another room, unnecessary tabs closed, timer set (Chapters 6, 7 and 12).
3. **Start with retrieval.** Before you look at your notes, spend five minutes writing down everything you remember about the topic. It's a quick warm-up that tells you where the gaps are.
4. **Study actively.** Read or review a section, then close the book and explain it in your own words, out loud or in writing. Ask yourself why and how questions: *Why does this drug interact with that one? How would this change if the patient had kidney problems?*
5. **Test yourself.** Practice questions, past papers, flashcards.
6. **Check and correct.** Look up what you got wrong and make sure you understand it.
7. **Schedule the next review.** Put this topic back in your plan in a few days, and again in a week or two.

## The Feynman technique

The physicist Richard Feynman was famous for his ability to explain complicated ideas simply. A study method based on his approach is now widely used, and it's another form of active recall.

1. Choose a concept you're learning.
2. Explain it, on paper or out loud, as if you were teaching it to a twelve-year-old. Use simple words. Avoid jargon.
3. When you get stuck or find yourself waving your hands, that's a gap in your understanding. Go back to your notes and fill it.
4. Simplify your explanation again.

If you can explain something simply, you understand it. If you can't, you've found exactly what to study next.

## Planning for exams

Use the planning tools from Part Three, working backward from the exam date.

1. **List every topic** that might be examined.
2. **Rate each topic** by how well you know it now (1 to 5) and how important it is in the exam.
3. **Count the study sessions you realistically have** between now and the exam, using your normal weekly schedule, with buffer days (Chapter 10).
4. **Allocate sessions to topics**, giving more to weak, important topics. Make sure every topic gets at least two or three sessions spread over time, not one big block.
5. **Leave the last few days for mixed practice**: past papers and practice questions from all topics together, under timed conditions.
6. **Protect your sleep.** Especially in the last week. Pulling all-nighters before exams usually hurts more than it helps, because sleep is when much of the day's learning gets consolidated in memory.

## Lectures and online classes

Lectures are a classic place for attention to wander, whether you're in a hall or watching on a screen.

A few things help:

- **Close everything except your notes.** In a 2013 study, Faria Sana, Tina Weston and Nicholas Cepeda found that students who multitasked on laptops during a lecture understood less of it. So did students sitting nearby who could see the multitaskers' screens. If you take notes on a laptop, use full-screen mode with nothing else open.
- **Consider handwritten notes**, at least for some classes. Many students find that writing by hand forces them to summarize and think, instead of transcribing.
- **Prepare questions in advance.** Skim the reading or slides before the lecture and write down two or three questions you want answered. You'll listen differently.
- **For recorded lectures**, resist the urge to have them on in the background while doing something else. Watch them like a live class, with notes, and pause to test yourself.

## Long assignments

Essays, dissertations and big projects are where procrastination does its worst damage, because the deadline is far away and the task is large and vague.

Use everything from Chapters 9 to 11:

- **Break it down** into small steps (choose topic, find sources, outline, draft section 1, and so on).
- **Set your own deadlines** for each step, spaced out, and tell someone about them (Chapter 14).
- **Schedule the first step** for this week, not "when I have time".
- **Write an ugly first draft** quickly, then improve it.
- **Stop in the middle** each day so tomorrow's start is easy.

## Studying with others

Group study can help or hurt focus, depending on how it's run. It helps when it's active and structured: quizzing each other, explaining concepts in turn, working through problems together. It hurts when it's mostly chatting with books open.

If you study with friends, agree in advance on what you'll cover and for how long. Use focus sessions together: everyone works in silence for 25 or 50 minutes, then you discuss or test each other during the break. Many students also find "body doubling", working silently alongside someone else, in person or on a video call, makes it easier to stay on task.

## Sana's exam season

For her second-year exams, Sana built a plan on these principles. She listed her topics and rated them. She planned two or three fifty-minute study sessions a day, with Sunday afternoons as buffer. Each session started with five minutes of writing down what she remembered, then active study, then practice questions. Each topic came back at least three times over the six weeks. The last week was past papers.

She stopped highlighting. She still reread her notes sometimes, but only after she'd tested herself.

"It was harder," she said. "Testing myself was horrible at first, because I kept finding out how much I didn't know. Rereading felt so much nicer. But in the exam, I could actually remember things. That had never happened before."

Her marks went up in every module. More than that, she studied fewer hours than the year before, and she slept every night of exam season.

## Try this today

Upgrade one study session.

1. Choose one topic you need to learn or revise.
2. Before you look at any notes, spend five minutes writing down everything you remember about it.
3. Compare with your notes. Mark the gaps.
4. Spend 25 minutes studying the gaps actively: explain each one in your own words, or use the Feynman technique.
5. Test yourself with practice questions or flashcards for 10 minutes.
6. Put two more review sessions for this topic in your calendar: one in three days, one in about a week.

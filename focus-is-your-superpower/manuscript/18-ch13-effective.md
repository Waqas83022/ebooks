# Chapter 13: Do the right things

Amira was proud of how fast she worked. She could turn around a social media graphic in twenty minutes, answer a customer message in thirty seconds, and pack an order in under three minutes. She had keyboard shortcuts for everything. She had once timed herself wrapping prints and shaved a minute off the process.

What she couldn't work out was why, despite all that speed, she was earning less than she had a year ago and working more hours.

When she finally sat down and looked at where her time went, the answer was uncomfortable. She was very efficient at a lot of work that didn't pay. She spent eight hours a week making free graphics for the online shop's social media, which brought in a handful of sales. She spent five hours a week answering questions from customers that were already answered on her website. She spent hours redesigning things that were already fine. Meanwhile, her best-paying work, brand projects for small businesses, got whatever time was left over.

She was doing things right. She just wasn't always doing the right things.

## Doing things right versus doing the right things

The management writer Peter Drucker drew this distinction in his 1967 book *The Effective Executive*. Efficiency, he said, is doing things right. Effectiveness is doing the right things.

Both matter. But effectiveness comes first. There's no point doing something efficiently if it shouldn't be done at all. A faster way to do useless work is still useless work.

Most productivity advice is about efficiency: shortcuts, apps, faster methods. This chapter is about effectiveness: making sure the work you're doing is worth doing, and then doing it in the simplest way that works.

## Four questions for every recurring task

Look at the tasks that fill your week, especially the ones that come back again and again. For each, ask four questions, in this order.

**1. Can I eliminate it?** What would happen if you simply stopped? Sometimes the honest answer is "nothing much". Reports that nobody reads. Meetings that don't decide anything. Social media posts that don't bring customers. Tidying that nobody notices. Before you make a task faster, check whether it needs to exist.

**2. Can I reduce it?** If you can't eliminate it, can you do less of it? Post three times a week instead of daily. Hold the meeting every two weeks instead of weekly. Check email three times a day instead of thirty. Write a one-page summary instead of a ten-page report.

**3. Can I hand it over?** Can someone else do it, perhaps better or more cheaply? A colleague whose job it really is. A family member. A paid service. A piece of software. Delegating can feel uncomfortable, especially if you like control, but your time is limited, and every hour you spend on something someone else could do is an hour you can't spend on what only you can do.

**4. Can I simplify or systematize it?** For what's left, find the simplest reliable way. Use a template. Write a checklist. Automate the repetitive parts. Batch it with similar tasks.

Amira went through these questions for every regular task in her week. She stopped making daily social graphics and posted three strong pieces of work a week instead. She rewrote her website's frequently asked questions and added a link to them in her automatic message reply, which cut her customer questions by more than half. She hired a student to pack orders two afternoons a week. And she made a proposal template, which turned a four-hour task into a ninety-minute one. In a month, she'd freed up around twelve hours a week, and she put most of them into brand projects.

## Templates and checklists

Any task you do more than a few times is worth a template or a checklist.

A **template** is a ready-made starting point: a document, email or file with the structure already in place. Amira's proposal template had her standard sections, her terms and her pricing table, so each new proposal only needed the details for that client. Daniel made templates for his weekly status report and for meeting agendas. Sana made a template for her revision notes: key idea, how it works, examples, common exam questions.

A **checklist** is a list of steps you follow every time, so you don't have to remember them. The surgeon Atul Gawande, in *The Checklist Manifesto*, describes how simple checklists reduced errors in operating theaters and other complex settings. You don't need to be doing surgery for checklists to help. A checklist for starting your workday, for packing your bag before an exam, for sending an invoice, or for closing your shop at night removes small decisions and stops small things being forgotten.

Templates and checklists also help with focus, because they reduce the friction of starting (Chapter 11). When the structure is already there, it's much easier to begin.

## Batch similar tasks

You met batching in Chapter 8, as a way to deal with email and messages. It works for many other tasks too.

Make all your phone calls in one block. Run all your errands in one trip. Pay all your bills on the same day each month. Cook several meals at once at the weekend. Write several social media posts in one session instead of one a day.

Each time you switch between different kinds of tasks, there's a cost in time and attention. Batching keeps you in one mode for longer and shrinks that cost.

## Use technology on purpose

Technology can make you much more effective, or it can eat your time without your noticing.

Useful technology tends to remove repetitive work: calendar reminders, automatic bill payments, email filters that sort messages into folders, text shortcuts for phrases you type often, a password manager, a shared family calendar.

AI assistants are now part of this picture too. They can help you draft a first version of an email, summarize long documents, suggest how to break down a project, or explain a concept you're stuck on. Used well, that can save real time. But they bring their own trap. It's easy to spend an hour asking a chatbot to make you the perfect study plan, the perfect morning routine, the perfect productivity system, and never start the actual work. Asking an assistant to plan can be another form of the productive procrastination you met in Chapter 2. Use these tools for the parts of a task they help with, check what they give you, and then close them and do the work.

As a rule, before adopting any new app or tool, ask: *What specific problem will this solve, and is that problem worth the time it takes to set up and learn?* Many people have more productivity apps than productive hours.

## Good enough

Some tasks deserve your best. Most don't.

An important client proposal, an exam answer, a difficult conversation, your most important creative work: these deserve real care. But a routine email to a colleague, the formatting of an internal document, the color of a folder, the wording of a reminder message: these need to be clear and correct, and that's all.

Perfectionists, like Amira, often spend large amounts of time on small improvements that nobody else will notice. One way to break this habit is to decide in advance, for each task, which level it deserves:

- *Excellent* for work that is important, visible and high-stakes. Give it time and care.
- *Good* for work that needs to be done properly but doesn't need to be outstanding.
- *Done* for work that just needs to exist and work.

Most of your tasks are "good" or "done". Treating them all as "excellent" is how hours disappear.

## Know your best hours

Effectiveness isn't only about which tasks you do. It's also about when you do them.

Most people have times of day when they think most clearly, and times when their energy dips. These patterns differ a lot from person to person. Some people are sharpest early in the morning; others don't really wake up until mid-morning; some do their best thinking late at night. Many people notice a dip in the early afternoon.

For one week, check your energy and focus every two hours or so, on a scale of 1 to 5. Note the time and what you were doing. By the end of the week you'll have a rough map of your own day.

Then use it. Put your most demanding work, your MIT and your deep work, in your high-energy times. Put shallow work, meetings and admin in your low-energy times. Don't waste your sharpest hour on email.

Tariq discovered that his worst time for coding was straight after dinner, when he usually tried to do it. His best was early morning, before his family woke up. He moved two of his weekly sessions to 6:00 a.m. on weekdays and found he got more done in forty minutes than he used to in ninety.

## Rest is part of effectiveness

One more point, which Part Four develops in detail. Working longer hours doesn't always mean getting more done. Beyond a certain point, extra hours often produce less, because tiredness slows you down, increases mistakes and makes everything feel harder.

Effective people protect their rest for the same reason they protect their deep work: because both are necessary for good results. A tired mind isn't an efficient one.

## Try this today

Do an effectiveness review of your week.

1. List the tasks that take up most of your time in a normal week, especially the recurring ones.
2. For each, ask the four questions in order: Can I eliminate it? Reduce it? Hand it over? Simplify or systematize it?
3. Choose one task to eliminate or reduce this week.
4. Choose one recurring task and make a template or checklist for it.
5. For your three most common tasks, decide whether each deserves "excellent", "good" or "done".
6. Start an energy map: for the rest of this week, note your energy level every couple of hours.

### Reflection

- Which task in my week am I doing very efficiently, even though it may not need doing at all?
- What do I keep doing myself that someone or something else could do?
- When are my best hours, and what am I currently doing with them?

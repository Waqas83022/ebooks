# Chapter 20: Your focus system for life

By now you have a lot of tools. Focus sessions and the focus ladder. A digital reset. A workspace designed for work. Deep work blocks and batched shallow work. The urgent-important matrix and the MIT. Time blocking with buffers. Two-minute starts and if-then plans. Work rhythms and real breaks. Templates and checklists. Process goals and WOOP. Self-compassion and the reset routine. Habit stacks and trackers. Sleep, movement and a shutdown ritual.

You don't need to use all of them all the time. Nobody does. What you need is a simple, repeatable way to keep the system working, notice when it isn't, and fix it without starting over every time.

That's what this chapter is for. It brings everything together into a rhythm of daily, weekly and monthly routines that you can keep for years.

## The daily rhythm

Your daily routine should be short enough to survive a bad day. For most people, it has three parts.

**The start (5 to 10 minutes).** Your starting routine from Chapter 16: phone to its home, focus mode on, read your MIT, set the target for your first session, start the timer.

**The work (your sessions).** One to three focused sessions on important work, at your best-energy times. Shallow work batched into one or two windows. Real breaks.

**The shutdown (10 to 15 minutes).** Your shutdown ritual from Chapter 17: capture loose ends, look at tomorrow, choose tomorrow's MIT, leave a note on unfinished work, tidy up, mark the end.

That's the core. On a good day you'll do more. On a bad day, do the minimum: choose an MIT, do one session (even a short one), and shut down properly.

## The weekly review

The weekly review is the single most important routine for keeping your system alive. It's where you step back from the daily rush, look at the bigger picture and adjust.

Choose a fixed time each week, and give it thirty to forty-five minutes. Many people use Friday afternoon (to close the week) or Sunday evening (to prepare for the next). Put it in your calendar as a recurring appointment.

Here's a structure you can follow. Use the weekly review page in the toolkit at the back of this book if it helps.

**1. Clear (10 minutes).**

- Empty your capture list, inbox and notes into one place. Decide what to do with each item: do, schedule, delegate or delete.
- Clear your desk and your computer desktop.
- Close any open loops: replies you owe, small tasks you forgot.

**2. Review (10 minutes).**

- Look back at the past week's calendar. What happened? What got done?
- Look at your lead measures (Chapter 14). How many focus sessions did you complete? Did you keep your key habits?
- Look at your screen time numbers. Up or down?
- Ask three questions and write short answers:
  - *What went well this week?*
  - *What got in the way?*
  - *What will I do differently next week?*

**3. Plan (15 minutes).**

- Look at your goals and milestones. Are you on track?
- Choose your big rocks for next week: the one to three most important outcomes.
- Look at next week's calendar. Block time for your big rocks, your deep work and your breaks. Add buffer.
- Note any obstacles you can see coming, and make an if-then plan for each.

**4. Close (2 minutes).**

- Choose Monday's MIT.
- Say your shutdown phrase. The week is done.

The first few weekly reviews may feel slow and awkward. After a month, most people find it's the half hour that makes the rest of the week work. Daniel described his Friday review as "the meeting that makes all the other meetings bearable".

## The FOCUS check

When something isn't working, use the FOCUS method from the start of the book to find out where the problem is. During your weekly review, ask one question for each letter.

**F: Find.** *Do I know what matters most right now?* If you're busy but not making progress, or you feel scattered, go back to Chapter 9. Re-sort your list, choose your big rocks, write your MIT.

**O: Organize.** *Does my important work have a place in my calendar?* If important tasks keep getting pushed aside, or your plans keep collapsing, go back to Chapter 10. Block time, add buffer, use the 50 percent rule.

**C: Control.** *Am I protecting my attention from distraction?* If you're losing time to your phone, interruptions or a cluttered environment, go back to Chapters 6 and 7. Check your notifications, your phone's home, your workspace, your agreements with others.

**U: Use.** *Am I working with my energy?* If you're tired, slow or burnt out, go back to Chapters 12, 13 and 17. Check your sleep, your breaks, your best hours, your boundaries.

**S: Sustain.** *Are my habits holding?* If you start well and fade, or keep abandoning your routines after a slip, go back to Chapters 15 and 16. Shrink your habits, use the reset routine, track your progress, never miss twice.

Most weeks, one letter will stand out. Fix that one. Don't try to fix everything.

## The monthly reset

Once a month, spend an hour on a deeper review. A good time is the first weekend of the month, which also takes advantage of the fresh start effect (Chapter 15).

In the monthly reset:

- **Review your goals.** Are they still the right ones? Has anything changed in your life that means they need adjusting? Is there a goal you should drop or move to the "later" list?
- **Review your milestones.** Did you reach this month's? If not, why not? Adjust the timeline if needed. Being realistic is not the same as giving up.
- **Review your habits.** Which ones are now automatic? Which are still a struggle? Is it time to add a new one?
- **Review your digital life.** Check your screen time trends. Look at your notifications again. Have any apps crept back onto your home screen? Has a new app become a problem?
- **Review your commitments.** Is there anything you've agreed to that you should now step back from?
- **Choose one experiment for next month.** One new technique to try, one habit to build, or one change to make. Write it down.

## When things fall apart

At some point, probably more than once, your system will collapse. A family crisis, a new job, illness, a move, a new baby, a breakup, a long holiday, or simply a slow slide over several weeks. You'll look up one day and realize you haven't done a focus session in a month, your phone time has doubled, and your weekly review is a distant memory.

This is normal. It happens to everyone who tries to build a system like this. The question isn't whether it'll happen, but how you'll respond.

Here's how to rebuild:

1. **Don't try to restart everything at once.** That's the all-or-nothing trap again (Chapter 15).
2. **Use the reset routine.** Name what happened, be kind, look for causes, decide what to change.
3. **Start with the smallest core.** One MIT a day and one short focus session. That's all, for the first week.
4. **Add the weekly review back** in the second week.
5. **Add other pieces gradually**, one per week, in the order that matters most for you: often the digital reset first, then the workspace, then the habits.
6. **Choose a fresh start date** if it helps, and tell someone.

Most people find that rebuilding is much faster than building the first time. The habits haven't disappeared. They're just dormant, and they wake up quickly.

## A year from now

Imagine yourself a year from today.

Imagine that, for most of that year, you've done one or two focused sessions on your most important goal on most days. You've kept your phone out of your bedroom. You've done a weekly review most weeks. You've slept reasonably well, moved a bit every day, and had a real day off each week. You've had bad weeks, and you've recovered from them.

What would be different?

Here's roughly where the four people in this book got to, a year after they began.

**Sana** finished her second year with the best marks she'd ever had, and started her third year with a study routine she no longer had to think about. Her screen time settled at a little over two hours a day, most of it messages and music. She still loves short videos. She watches them on her laptop on Saturday evenings, and stops when the evening ends.

**Daniel** delivered his project on time, and his director asked him to lead the next one. He still works from his bedroom corner, but behind a folding screen, with a clean desk and a ten-minute walk to end each day. He's home for dinner most nights. "I'm doing less," he said, "but what I do actually matters now."

**Amira** raised her prices twice, works with fewer, better clients, and takes Fridays off from client work to think about her business. She still struggles with perfectionism. She just has a timer now, and a rule: "good" is good enough for almost everything.

**Tariq** finished his course, built three small apps, and, after eleven applications and two disappointing interviews, accepted a junior developer role at a company that builds software for accountants, where his old career turned out to be an advantage. He codes in the mornings, takes Sundays off and still eats dinner with his phone in the hallway basket.

None of them became different people. None of them achieved perfect focus. They all still have distracted days, lost evenings and weeks where the system wobbles. What changed was that focused, meaningful work became a normal part of their lives, instead of something that happened only under the pressure of a deadline.

That's what's possible for you, too.

## Try this today

Schedule your rhythm.

1. Put a recurring weekly review in your calendar, at a time you're likely to keep, for 30 to 45 minutes.
2. Put a recurring monthly reset in your calendar, on the first weekend of each month, for one hour.
3. Write down your daily minimum: the smallest version of your start, your work and your shutdown that you'll do even on a bad day.
4. Do a quick FOCUS check now. Which letter needs the most attention this week? Write down one action for it.

- Which part do I keep dropping, and how could I make it smaller or easier?
- What do I want my focus to make possible a year from now?

# Chapter 19: Focus at work, in business and at home

Each of the four people in this book faces a different kind of pressure on their attention. Sana has lectures, exams and a phone full of friends. Daniel has a manager, a team, an inbox and a calendar that other people can write in. Amira has clients, customers and a business that depends partly on the same social media that distracts her. Tariq has a full-time job, two teenagers, a household and a dream that has to fit into what's left.

The principles are the same for all of them: find what matters, organize time for it, control distractions, use energy wisely and sustain progress. But the details differ. This chapter looks at how the system works in three settings: the workplace, running your own business, and home life with family.

## At work: when other people own your calendar

Daniel's biggest problem wasn't his phone. It was other people. His calendar was visible to his whole team, so anyone could book a meeting into any empty slot. His chat was expected to be open all day. His manager sent messages at all hours and seemed to expect fast replies.

If your job is like this, you can't simply switch everything off. But you usually have more room than you think.

### Block your calendar first

The simplest defense is to fill your calendar before others do. At the start of each week, block your deep work time (Chapter 8) as if it were a meeting. Give it a clear, professional name, like "Project planning: do not book" or "Focus time". Most colleagues will book around it.

Daniel blocked 8:00 to 9:30 every weekday and two afternoons a week from 2:00 to 3:30. A few people booked over the blocks anyway at first. He politely suggested other times, and after a few weeks the blocks were mostly respected.

### Make meetings earn their place

Meetings are often the biggest drain on focus at work, because they break the day into fragments too short for serious work.

Before accepting a meeting, ask:

- **Is there a clear purpose or agenda?** If not, ask for one. "Happy to join. Could you send a quick agenda so I can prepare?" Often this is enough to make the meeting better, or to reveal that it isn't needed.
- **Do I need to be there?** Could someone else attend, or could you read the notes afterward?
- **Could it be shorter?** Many meetings are scheduled for an hour because that's the calendar's default. Try 25 or 45 minutes.
- **Could it be an email or a message instead?**

When you do run meetings, end them with clear decisions and actions: who's doing what, by when. A meeting that ends without decisions usually leads to another meeting.

It also helps to cluster meetings together. Two meetings back to back in the afternoon leave the morning free for deep work. The same two meetings at 10:00 and 2:00 break the whole day.

### Agree on what's urgent

Much of the pressure to reply instantly comes from unspoken assumptions. Have the conversation out loud.

Daniel asked his manager for fifteen minutes and said something like this: "I want to make sure I'm getting the project plan and the big pieces done, not just reacting to messages all day. I'd like to block two focus periods a day when I'm not checking chat. If something's really urgent, could you call me? Otherwise I'll reply within a couple of hours."

His manager agreed, and was actually relieved. She'd been worried about the project plan too.

Not every manager will respond so well. But many will, especially if you frame the request around getting important work done, and especially if you then deliver.

### Handle interruptions in person

In an office, interruptions often come from colleagues stopping by. A few strategies:

- **Use a visible signal.** Headphones on, or a small sign, means you're focusing.
- **Offer a later time.** "I'm in the middle of something. Can I come and find you at 11?"
- **Hold office hours.** If people often need your help, set regular times when you're available for questions, and encourage them to save non-urgent questions for those times.
- **Find a hiding place.** If your office has quiet rooms or a library, use them for deep work.

### When your job is reactive

Some jobs, such as customer service, IT support, healthcare, retail and reception, are reactive by nature. You can't schedule hours of uninterrupted focus, because responding is the job.

In these roles, the system adapts. Focus on doing one thing at a time, fully, instead of juggling several. Use quieter moments for small chunks of planned work, with notes so you can resume quickly (Chapter 8). Protect your attention outside work even more carefully, so you arrive each day rested. And if you have any important non-reactive work, such as a project or a qualification, try to schedule it before or after the reactive part of your day, or on a quieter day.

## In business: when you're the boss

Amira had a different problem. Nobody told her what to do. That was the appeal of working for herself, and also the danger. Without a manager, a schedule or colleagues, every day was hers to plan, and every day she could choose not to.

If you run your own business or work freelance, a few extra principles help.

### Give yourself a boss

Self-employed people need structure even more than employees, because nobody else is providing it. Create the structure deliberately:

- **Set working hours**, and keep to them on most days. Start and finish at roughly the same time.
- **Hold a weekly planning meeting with yourself** (Chapter 20), as seriously as you'd attend one with a client.
- **Find accountability.** A business friend you check in with weekly. A co-working space. A coach or mentor. An online group of other freelancers.

### Separate working on the business from working in it

Most small business owners spend almost all their time working *in* the business: doing the work clients pay for, answering messages, packing orders, handling problems. Very little time goes to working *on* the business: improving prices, finding better clients, building systems, planning for the future.

The second kind of work is almost always important and not urgent (Chapter 9), which means it gets pushed aside. Give it a fixed block every week. Amira chose Friday mornings: no client work, no messages, just thinking about the business. In that block, she raised her prices, built her proposal template, and planned how to find more brand clients. It was the most valuable time in her week.

### Use social media without being used by it

For many business owners, social media is both a necessary tool and a constant distraction. The trick is to separate creating and publishing from consuming.

- **Batch your content.** Create several posts in one session, then schedule them to publish automatically.
- **Reply in windows.** Check comments and messages at set times, not continuously.
- **Use a computer, not your phone**, for business social media where possible. It's easier to do what you came to do and leave.
- **Unfollow accounts that drain you**, especially competitors whose highlight reels make you anxious.
- **Measure what matters.** Track whether social media actually brings customers. Amira discovered that her daily posts brought in very few sales, while three strong posts a week did almost as well. That freed hours.

### Set boundaries with clients

Clients will often take as much of your time and attention as you give them. Clear boundaries protect both your focus and the quality of your work.

- **State your response times** in your contracts, your email signature or an automatic reply.
- **Define the scope of each project** in writing, and agree in advance how changes will be handled and charged.
- **Don't answer business messages late at night** unless you've agreed to and are being paid for it.
- **Batch client calls** on particular days or afternoons, where possible.

When Amira introduced her twice-a-day reply schedule, she worried she'd lose clients. She didn't lose any. Two told her it made her seem more professional.

## At home: focus with family

Tariq's challenge was the one many people with families face. His time wasn't really his own. Work took the day. Family took the evening, and rightly so. His goal had to fit into the cracks.

### Find your real hours

The first step is honesty about how much time you actually have. Look at a typical week and mark the hours that are truly available for focused work on your goal, after work, sleep, meals, family and essential household tasks.

For many parents and carers, the honest answer is small: perhaps five to ten hours a week, often in early mornings, late evenings, nap times or weekends. That can feel discouraging. But five focused hours a week is 260 hours a year, enough to learn a lot, build a lot, or finish a major project. The key is to use those hours well, not to pretend you have more.

### Make agreements, not demands

You met Tariq's family agreement in Chapter 7. The principles are worth repeating, because they apply to almost every household:

- Explain what you're doing and why it matters.
- Ask for specific, limited times.
- Agree on a signal and on what counts as urgent.
- Keep your side of the deal by being fully present outside those times.

With a partner, it often helps to trade focus time. One adult takes the children for an hour on Saturday morning while the other works, then they swap.

### Work in short bursts

If you can't get long blocks, make the most of short ones. Twenty-five minutes during a nap. Forty minutes before the house wakes up. Fifteen minutes on the train. With a clear target, a ready-to-resume note from last time (Chapter 8) and your phone away, a short session can achieve far more than you'd expect.

The two-minute start and the minimum version (Chapter 11) are especially useful here. On days when there's no time at all, do the minimum. Keep the chain going.

### Be present when you're present

Focus at home isn't only about your own goals. It's also about the quality of your attention for the people you live with.

Children notice when a parent's eyes keep going to a phone. Partners notice when conversations are half-heard. The same skills that protect your work, single-tasking, putting the phone away, being fully where you are, also protect your relationships.

Tariq made a rule that dinner was phone-free for everyone, including him. At first his teenagers complained. After a few weeks, dinners got longer. "We actually talk now," he said. "My daughter told me about a problem at school that she'd never have mentioned if we'd all been on our phones. That's worth more than any amount of coding."

### Seasons of life

Finally, it's worth accepting that focus looks different at different times of life. A parent with a newborn, a person caring for a sick relative, someone going through grief or illness, a student in exam season, an employee in a crisis at work: each of these is a season with its own limits.

In a demanding season, lower your expectations without abandoning your goals. Shrink your process goals. Focus on the minimum. Protect sleep. Ask for help. The season will pass, and when it does, the habits you've kept alive, even in their smallest form, will be ready to grow again.

## Try this today

Choose the section of this chapter that fits your life best, and take one action from it.

- **At work:** block two focus periods in your calendar for next week, and decline or shorten one meeting.
- **In business:** schedule a weekly block for working on your business, and set a response-time policy for client or customer messages.
- **At home:** list your real available hours for a typical week, and have a conversation with your family about one protected focus time.

Then choose one place in your life where you'll practice being fully present, such as dinner, the walk to school or a weekly call with a parent, and keep your phone away for it.

### Reflection

- Who or what has the most control over my time, and how much of that control is real?
- What's one conversation I need to have to protect my focus?
- What season of life am I in, and what does a realistic level of progress look like right now?

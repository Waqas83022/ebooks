# Chapter 14: Goals you can act on

Every January for five years, Tariq had written the same goal at the front of a new notebook: *Change careers. Become a developer.*

Every year it stayed there, underlined, while the year filled up with other things. It wasn't that he didn't want it. He wanted it badly. But the goal was so big and so far away that it never told him what to do on a Tuesday evening. It was a destination with no road.

This year, the course had given him a road of sorts. But he noticed that he still swung between two moods: a rush of excitement whenever he imagined the finish line, and a slump whenever he realized how far away it was. What he needed was a way to turn the big goal into something he could act on every week.

## Why big goals stall

Big goals are important. They give your life direction and make the effort feel worthwhile. But on their own, they have three weaknesses.

They're too far away. A goal that's two years off doesn't create any pressure today. The reward is distant, and as you saw in Chapter 2, distant rewards are weak motivators.

They're too vague. "Get fit", "be more productive", "start a business", "do well at university" sound like plans, but they don't say what to do.

They're out of your direct control. Whether you get the job, win the client or pass the exam depends partly on things you can't control: other candidates, the economy, the examiner. If your only goal is the outcome, you can do everything right and still feel like a failure.

The answer is to keep the big goal and build a structure beneath it.

## Outcome goals and process goals

It helps to separate two kinds of goals.

An **outcome goal** is the result you want. "Get a job as a junior developer." "Pass my pharmacology exam with a good grade." "Earn twice as much from brand projects this year." "Lose ten kilos."

A **process goal** is what you'll do regularly to make that result more likely. "Code for five sessions a week." "Do fifteen practice questions every weekday." "Send three proposals to potential clients every month." "Walk for thirty minutes every day and cook at home five nights a week."

Outcome goals give you direction. Process goals give you something to do today. And process goals are under your control in a way outcomes aren't. You can't guarantee you'll get the job. You can guarantee you'll code five times this week.

James Clear makes a similar point in *Atomic Habits*: winners and losers often have the same goals. What differs is the system they follow. You don't rise to the level of your goals; you fall to the level of your systems.

Keep both. Write the outcome goal down, so you remember where you're going. Then put most of your attention on the process.

## Make it specific

You've probably heard of SMART goals. The idea is usually traced to a 1981 article by George Doran in the journal *Management Review*, and the letters have been used in several slightly different ways since then. A common version is that a good goal is:

- **Specific**: clear about what you'll do or achieve.
- **Measurable**: you can tell whether you've done it.
- **Achievable**: realistic given your time and abilities.
- **Relevant**: connected to something you care about.
- **Time-bound**: it has a deadline or a timeframe.

"Get better at coding" becomes "complete modules five and six of my course, including both projects, by the end of March". "Study more" becomes "do two fifty-minute study sessions every weekday for the next four weeks".

SMART isn't perfect. Applied too rigidly, it can push people toward safe, small goals and away from ambitious ones. But it's a useful check, especially the M and the T. If you can't measure your goal and it has no timeframe, you'll never know whether you're on track.

## Milestones: the steps between here and there

Between a big outcome and your daily process, add milestones: checkpoints that break the journey into stages of a few weeks to a few months each.

Tariq's milestones for the next year looked like this:

- **End of March:** finish the course and both projects.
- **End of May:** build two small apps of my own and put them online.
- **End of July:** make a simple portfolio website and ask two developers I know for feedback.
- **End of September:** apply for at least ten junior roles or apprenticeships.
- **End of December:** have a developer job offer, or a clear plan for what to do differently next year.

Each milestone was far enough away to matter and close enough to feel real. Each one told him what to focus on now. And reaching each one gave him a reason to celebrate on the way, instead of waiting two years for a single moment of success.

## Deadlines that help

Deadlines are strange. Nobody likes them, and yet many people find they only do serious work when one is close.

In a pair of studies published in 2002, Dan Ariely and Klaus Wertenbroch looked at what happens when people set their own deadlines. In the first, students on a course had to write three papers. In one class they could choose their own due dates; in another the dates were fixed and evenly spaced across the term. Many students who could choose did set themselves early deadlines, which shows they knew they needed them. But the students with fixed, evenly spaced deadlines got better grades. In the second study, people were paid to proofread three long texts over three weeks. Those given evenly spaced deadlines found the most errors and handed in the most work on time, those who set their own deadlines came next, and those with a single deadline at the end did worst.

The lesson: deadlines help, and closer, more regular deadlines help more. If your goal only has a deadline at the end, create your own along the way, and if you can, make them binding by involving someone else. Tell a friend you'll send them your first draft by Friday. Book a mock exam. Schedule a meeting to show your progress.

## WOOP: dream, then plan for the obstacle

In Chapter 4 you met Gabriele Oettingen's finding that positive fantasies about the future can reduce effort. Oettingen didn't stop there. She went on to develop and test a method that combines dreaming with realism, which she calls mental contrasting with implementation intentions, or WOOP for short.

WOOP stands for:

- **Wish**: What do you want to achieve? Make it challenging but possible.
- **Outcome**: What's the best result of achieving it? How would it feel? Picture it vividly for a minute.
- **Obstacle**: What's the main thing inside you that might stop you? Not the outside world, but your own habits, feelings or beliefs. Picture it vividly too.
- **Plan**: What will you do when the obstacle appears? Write it as an if-then plan (Chapter 11).

Here's Sana's WOOP for her exam revision:

- **Wish:** Do two focused study sessions every weekday until the exam.
- **Outcome:** I'll walk into the exam feeling prepared, not panicked. I'll actually understand the material, and I'll have my evenings back the week before.
- **Obstacle:** When I get home tired, I lie on my bed "for a minute" with my phone and lose the evening.
- **Plan:** If I get home after 5 p.m., then I'll go straight to the desk, put my phone in the hallway basket, and start the first session before I sit or lie anywhere else.

The obstacle step is the important one. Dreaming alone gives you a pleasant feeling. Dreaming followed by a hard look at what's in the way, and a plan for it, gives you a reason and a method to act. Oettingen and her colleagues have tested WOOP in many studies with students, patients and working adults, and generally found that it helps people make more progress on their goals than positive thinking alone.

A WOOP takes about five minutes. You can do one for a big goal, or for a single day.

## Lead and lag measures

Here's a way of tracking progress that keeps your attention on what you can control.

A **lag measure** tells you about the result, after it's happened: your exam grade, your weight, your monthly income, whether you got the job. It's important, but by the time you see it, it's too late to change.

A **lead measure** tracks the actions that drive the result, while you can still do something about them: number of focused study sessions this week, number of practice questions done, number of proposals sent, number of days you walked, number of coding sessions completed.

Track your lead measures weekly. If they're on target, trust the process even when the lag measures are slow to move. If they're off target, you know exactly what to fix.

## Fewer goals, done properly

One of the most common mistakes is having too many goals at once. Learn a language, get fit, start a business, read fifty books, meditate daily, learn guitar, get promoted, save more money. Each one is worthwhile. All of them at once is a recipe for doing none of them well.

Your attention and energy are limited. Every goal competes for them. Choose one to three major goals at a time, at most. Put the others on a "later" list. When one goal is achieved or becomes a settled habit, you can add another.

## Should you tell people?

You may have heard that telling people about your goals makes you more likely to achieve them, or the opposite, that it makes you less likely.

The evidence is mixed, and it seems to depend on how you tell them. A 2009 study led by Peter Gollwitzer found that when people announced goals that were tied to their identity ("I'm going to become a lawyer"), and others noticed, they sometimes put in less effort afterward, as if being seen as someone with that goal gave them some of the satisfaction already. On the other hand, telling someone specific about a specific plan, and asking them to check on your progress, can create helpful accountability.

A good rule of thumb: don't announce your dreams for applause. Share your plans with one or two people who'll ask you how they're going.

## Try this today

Turn one big goal into a plan you can act on.

1. Write down one important outcome goal.
2. Break it into three to five milestones, each with a date.
3. For the first milestone, write one or two process goals: what you'll do every day or every week.
4. Choose one lead measure you'll track weekly.
5. Do a WOOP for this week: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan.
6. Tell one person your plan for the first milestone, and ask them to check on you in two weeks.

### Reflection

- What goal have I been carrying around for years without a road beneath it?
- What's the main obstacle inside me, not outside, that stops me making progress?
- Which goals should I put on a "later" list so I can focus on the one or two that matter most now?

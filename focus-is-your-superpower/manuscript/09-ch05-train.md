# Chapter 5: Train your focus

When Sana decided to fix her studying, she did what most people do. She planned a heroic Saturday: eight hours in the library, phone switched off, no breaks except lunch. She lasted fifty minutes. After that she was so restless she went for a coffee, checked her phone "just for the time" and didn't come back to the library at all.

She came home convinced she had no attention span left. "Maybe my brain is just broken," she told her sister.

Her brain wasn't broken. She had tried to run a marathon without ever having jogged around the block.

## Attention is a skill

It's tempting to think of focus as something you either have or don't, like eye color. In fact, it behaves much more like a skill or a level of fitness. It responds to practice. It weakens without use. It's better on some days than others, depending on sleep, stress and what you've been doing. And like fitness, it improves fastest when you start where you are, not where you wish you were.

If you've spent the past few years switching tasks every few minutes and filling every gap with your phone, your attention has had a lot of practice at switching and very little at staying. That's not a moral failing. It's training, just training in the wrong direction. The good news is that the same process works the other way.

## Start where you actually are

The first step is honest measurement. How long can you currently work on something demanding before your attention breaks?

For many people the answer is somewhere between ten and twenty minutes. For some it's less. That's fine. If your real number is twelve minutes, then a session of fifteen is a stretch and a session of ninety is a fantasy. Start at fifteen.

The aim in the early weeks isn't to work for long periods. It's to build a track record of sessions where you set out to focus for a certain time and actually did. Every successful session teaches your brain that this is something you do. Every failed heroic attempt teaches it the opposite.

## The focus ladder

Here's a simple progression you can follow over four to six weeks. Move up a rung only when the current one feels comfortable on most days.

1. **Rung one: 10 to 15 minutes** of focused work, then a 5-minute break. One or two sessions a day.
2. **Rung two: 20 to 25 minutes**, then a 5-minute break. Two or three sessions a day.
3. **Rung three: 35 to 45 minutes**, then a 10-minute break. Two or three sessions a day.
4. **Rung four: 50 to 60 minutes**, then a 10-to-15-minute break. Two or three sessions a day.
5. **Rung five: 75 to 90 minutes**, then a longer break of 20 minutes or so. One or two of these a day is plenty for most people.

Most people never need to go beyond rung four or five. Ninety minutes of truly focused work is a lot. Two or three of those blocks a day, done regularly, will put you ahead of almost everyone who sits at a desk for ten hours with their phone buzzing.

If you have a bad day, drop down a rung. That's not failure. Athletes vary their training load every day.

## What counts as a focus session

A focus session has four features. Without them, it's just time spent near your work.

**One clear target.** Before you start, write down what you want to finish or reach by the end of the session. "Read pages 40 to 55 and write a five-line summary." "Draft the introduction." "Fix the bug in the login form." As you learned in Chapter 1, a clear finish line is what your attention steers by.

**One task.** Only the target task, and whatever is directly needed for it. If you need a reference, you can look it up. If you suddenly want to check the weather, you can't.

**A boundary.** A timer, so you know when the session ends. This matters more than it seems. Knowing there's a break coming in twenty minutes makes it much easier to resist the urge to take one now.

**Protection.** Phone out of reach, notifications off, unnecessary tabs closed, a door closed if you have one. You'll learn more ways to do this in the next two chapters.

## What to do when your mind drifts

It will drift. That's guaranteed, especially in the first few weeks. The question isn't whether your attention leaves, but what you do when you notice.

Use the method from Chapter 4. Notice, name it in a word, and return. If a useful thought appears, write it on your parking page and return. If a strong urge appears, to check your phone or open a new tab, try what some therapists call "urge surfing". Notice the urge as a physical sensation: a restlessness in your hands, a tightness in your chest, a sort of itch. Don't act on it and don't fight it. Just watch it for a minute or two, the way you'd watch a wave rise and fall. Urges usually peak and fade within a few minutes if you don't feed them.

Nir Eyal, who writes about distraction, suggests a related rule he calls the ten-minute rule: when you feel the urge to give in to a distraction, tell yourself you can, but only after ten minutes. Often, by then, the urge has passed. If it hasn't, at least you've practiced waiting.

Every time you bring your attention back, you've done one repetition of the most important exercise in this book. Some sessions will have dozens of repetitions. That's not a bad session. That's a workout.

## Tolerating boredom

One reason focus is hard now is that we've almost forgotten how to be bored. Every queue, every bus ride, every lull in a lecture can be filled instantly with something more interesting. So when a task gets dull, or difficult, or slow, our minds are out of practice at staying with it.

Deep focus almost always includes some boredom and some discomfort. Learning a new topic means sitting with confusion for a while. Writing means facing a blank page. Solving a problem means not knowing the answer, sometimes for a long time.

You can practice tolerating this outside of work. Try these, a few times a week:

- Wait in a queue without your phone.
- Take a short walk without headphones.
- Eat one meal a day without a screen.
- Sit for five minutes doing nothing at all, and notice what happens in your mind.

These sound trivial. They're not. Each one teaches your brain that a moment without stimulation is survivable, and that makes it easier to stay with a slow task when it matters.

## A simple attention exercise

If you'd like a direct way to train attention, try this short daily practice. It's a basic form of breath-focused meditation, and you don't need any special beliefs or equipment.

1. Sit comfortably. Set a timer for five minutes.
2. Close your eyes or let your gaze rest on a spot in front of you.
3. Pay attention to the feeling of your breath, at the nose, chest or belly, wherever it's easiest to notice.
4. When your mind wanders, which it will within seconds, notice where it went, and gently bring it back to the breath.
5. Repeat until the timer ends.

That's it. The goal isn't to empty your mind or to feel calm, although you may. The goal is to practice noticing and returning, over and over, in a setting with no pressure. It's the same skill you need at your desk, practiced in the simplest possible conditions. Many people find five minutes a day makes a noticeable difference within a few weeks. If you enjoy it, you can extend it to ten or fifteen.

## The role of interest

It's much easier to focus on something you find interesting. That's obvious, but it has a useful consequence: you can often make a task easier to focus on by making it more interesting.

Ask a question before you read. Instead of "read Chapter 6", try "find out why this drug interacts with grapefruit juice". A question gives your attention something to hunt for. Turn a task into a challenge: can you finish the first draft in forty minutes? Explain what you're learning out loud, as if to a friend. Connect it to something you care about: how will this skill help you in the job you want?

None of this turns every task into fun. But a task with a question, a challenge or a reason attached is far easier to stay with than one you're simply getting through.

## Tracking your progress

Keep a simple focus log. After each session, write down the date, how long it was, what the target was, whether you reached it, and roughly how many times your attention wandered. Once a week, look back.

After a few weeks, you'll see things you couldn't see before. Maybe your best sessions are in the morning. Maybe they're worse after you've been on social media. Maybe you're more focused in the library than at home. Maybe your wandering count is slowly dropping. The log turns vague impressions ("I'm terrible at concentrating") into evidence you can act on.

## What happened to Sana

Sana started again, this time at rung one. Fifteen minutes, then a break. She did two sessions on the first day and felt almost silly at how short they were.

But she finished both. The next day she did three. After a week she moved to twenty-five minutes, and after three weeks to forty. By the end of the month she could reliably do two forty-five-minute sessions in the evening, with a proper break in between, and she was getting through more pharmacology in those ninety minutes than she used to in a whole distracted evening.

"The first week felt like I was doing nothing," she said. "It was only when I looked at my log that I saw I'd done more focused studying that week than the whole month before."

## Try this today

Find your starting rung.

1. Choose a task that needs concentration and write down a clear target for it.
2. Put your phone in another room and close everything you don't need.
3. Start a stopwatch (counting up, not down) and work until you notice your focus has truly broken: you've drifted several times in a row, or you find yourself reaching for something else.
4. Stop the stopwatch and write down the time.
5. Your starting session length is about that number, rounded down to the nearest five minutes. If it was 18 minutes, start at 15.
6. Write down tomorrow's plan: how many sessions, how long each, and when.

Then, if you can, do one five-minute breathing practice before bed.

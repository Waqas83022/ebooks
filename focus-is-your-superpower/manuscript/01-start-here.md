# Start here: how to use this book {.unnumbered}

You do not have to read this book from the first page to the last, although you can. It was written for people who are short on time and attention, which means it should be easy to dip into.

Every chapter does four things. It explains one problem in plain language. It shows the problem in a real-feeling life: a student, a manager, a freelancer or a father learning a new trade at night. It gives you one or more techniques to try. And it ends with a short section called "Try this today", which you can do in less than half an hour, usually much less.

If you read a chapter and skip the exercise, you will understand your habits a little better. If you do the exercise, you will start to change them. The second is the point.

## The people you will meet

Four people appear throughout the book. They are composites, built from the stories that come up whenever people talk honestly about distraction.

**Sana** is twenty and in her second year of a pharmacy degree. She is bright and she cares about her grades. She also loses two or three hours a day to short videos without ever deciding to, and she does most of her studying in a panic the week before each exam.

**Daniel** is thirty-four and manages projects for a logistics company. Since his team went remote, he works from a desk in the corner of his bedroom. He is busy from eight in the morning until seven at night, and he cannot remember the last day he finished the thing he meant to finish.

**Amira** is twenty-eight and works for herself as a graphic designer. She also sells prints through a small online shop. She is talented and a perfectionist, and she puts off any task where she might be judged: proposals, pricing emails, posting her work.

**Tariq** is forty-five, an accountant with two teenagers, and he is teaching himself to code so he can change careers. He has the motivation. What he lacks is energy after nine hours at work, and a way to stop imagining the new life long enough to build it.

You may see yourself in one of them, or in pieces of all four.

## The FOCUS method

The techniques in this book fit into five steps. Their first letters spell FOCUS, which makes them easy to remember on a bad day.

**F: Find what matters.** Decide which goals and tasks deserve your best attention, and which ones can wait or be dropped.

**O: Organize your time.** Give important work a place in your calendar, sized realistically, with room for the things that will go wrong.

**C: Control distractions.** Change your phone, your room and your routines so that the easy thing and the right thing are more often the same thing.

**U: Use your energy wisely.** Work with your body and mind, with sleep, breaks and the hours when you think best, instead of against them.

**S: Sustain your progress.** Build habits, review your week and recover quickly when things slip, because they will.

FOCUS is a way of organizing well-known methods, not a new scientific theory. Its value is that it tells you where to look when something is not working. If your days are full but nothing important moves, the problem is usually F or O. If you know what to do and keep drifting to your phone, start with C. If you start strong and fade by mid-week, look at U and S.

## A two-minute focus check

Answer these three questions quickly. Pick the answer that is most often true for you.

**1. What most often stops you from finishing important work?**

a. I keep checking my phone or drifting to websites and videos.\
b. I worry, overthink, or put off starting.\
c. I have too many tasks and no clear priorities.\
d. I start well but run out of energy or consistency.

**2. What usually happens when you sit down to work?**

a. Within minutes, I'm on something else.\
b. The task feels too big, too vague or too uncomfortable.\
c. I'm not sure what to do first, so I do something small and easy.\
d. I work, but I can't keep the routine going for more than a few days.

**3. Which change would help you most?**

a. Controlling my screen time.\
b. Facing difficult tasks instead of avoiding them.\
c. A realistic daily plan.\
d. Steady habits and enough rest.

Count your letters. This is not a clinical test. It is a way to find a sensible starting point.

**Mostly a: digital distraction.** Read the introduction, then go to Chapters 3, 6 and 7.

**Mostly b: avoidance and overthinking.** Start with Chapters 2, 4 and 11.

**Mostly c: planning and priorities.** Start with Chapters 9, 10 and 14.

**Mostly d: energy and consistency.** Start with Chapters 12, 15, 16 and 17.

If your answers are spread out, read the book in order. It was built to work that way.

## The 30-day challenge

At the back of the book is a 30-day challenge: one small exercise a day for a month, grouped into four weeks. Each day refers to the chapter that explains it. Some readers prefer to read the whole book first and then do the challenge. Others start the challenge on the day they open the book and read the chapters as they go. Both work.

You will also find a toolkit of planning pages and worksheets. Copy them into a notebook, or use them on paper if you have the paperback. They are deliberately simple. A plan you will use is better than a beautiful one you won't.

## One request

Keep a notebook, paper or digital, while you read. Many exercises ask you to write something down, and writing is part of how they work. A thought you only think is easy to forget or argue with. A sentence you write down stays put and can be checked tomorrow.

# Chapter 16: Habits that carry you

When Daniel first started his forty-five-minute block before email, every morning was a small fight. His hand drifted toward the email tab. His mind listed all the messages that might be waiting. Some mornings he won the fight and some mornings he lost.

Two months later, something had changed. He'd sit down, make his coffee, open the project file and start working, and only afterward notice that he hadn't thought about email at all. The fight had stopped. Not because he'd become more disciplined, but because the new routine had become a habit.

"It's like brushing my teeth now," he said. "I don't decide to do it. I just do it."

That's the goal of this chapter: to turn the most important parts of your focus system into habits, so they stop costing you willpower every day.

## What a habit is

A habit is a behavior that has become automatic in a particular situation. When the situation comes up, you do the behavior without having to decide. You get in the car and put on your seatbelt. You walk into the kitchen in the morning and switch on the kettle. You sit down on the train and take out your phone.

Habits are built through repetition in a stable context. Each time you do the same thing in the same situation, the link between them grows a little stronger, until the situation alone is enough to trigger the behavior.

This is good news and bad news. The bad news is that your current habits, including the unhelpful ones, are well established, because you've repeated them hundreds or thousands of times. The good news is that the same process can build new ones.

## How long it takes

You may have heard that it takes 21 days to form a habit. That number has no good scientific basis.

The best-known study on habit formation was published in 2010 by Phillippa Lally and her colleagues at University College London. They asked 96 people to choose a new daily behavior, such as drinking a glass of water after breakfast or going for a run before dinner, and to report each day for twelve weeks how automatic it felt. For the people whose data could be analyzed this way, the median time to reach a plateau of automaticity was 66 days. But the range was huge: from 18 days to 254. Simple behaviors, like drinking water, became automatic faster than complex ones, like doing fifty sit-ups.

Two lessons follow. First, expect habits to take weeks to months, not days. Don't give up after a fortnight because a new routine still feels effortful. Second, the study found that missing a single day didn't seriously damage the process. That's another reason for the "never miss twice" rule from Chapter 15.

## The parts of a habit

Writers such as Charles Duhigg and James Clear describe habits as having a few parts. A simple version:

**The cue** is what triggers the habit: a time, a place, a feeling, a preceding action, or other people.

**The routine** is the behavior itself.

**The reward** is what you get from it: pleasure, relief, a sense of completion. The reward is what teaches your brain that the routine is worth repeating.

To build a good habit, you make the cue clear, the routine easy and the reward satisfying. To break a bad one, you do the opposite: hide the cue, make the routine harder and remove or replace the reward.

You've already met many of these ideas. Putting your phone in another room hides a cue. Grayscale weakens a reward. Leaving your notes open on the desk makes a cue obvious. The two-minute start makes a routine easy. This chapter brings them together.

## Habit stacking

One of the easiest ways to create a clear cue for a new habit is to attach it to something you already do every day.

The behavior scientist BJ Fogg, in his book *Tiny Habits*, uses a simple formula: "After I [existing habit], I will [new habit]." James Clear calls this habit stacking.

For example:

- After I pour my morning coffee, I'll open my most important task.
- After I sit down on the train, I'll read ten pages of my book.
- After I finish dinner, I'll put my phone in the hallway basket.
- After I close my laptop at the end of work, I'll write tomorrow's MIT on a card.
- After I brush my teeth at night, I'll write three lines in my journal.

The existing habit is already automatic and reliable, so it makes a dependable cue. Choose an anchor that happens at the right time and place for the new habit, and make the new habit small, especially at first. Fogg's advice is to start tiny: floss one tooth, do two push-ups, read one page. Once the habit is established, it will naturally grow.

## Make it satisfying

Habits stick when they feel rewarding. The trouble with many good habits is that their rewards are delayed. You study now and see the results in an exam months later. You code tonight and get the job next year. Meanwhile, the bad habits have instant rewards: the video is funny right now.

So add some immediate satisfaction to your good habits.

**Track them.** A habit tracker, which can be as simple as a calendar where you cross off each day you do the habit, gives you a small, instant reward: the satisfaction of making the mark. Over time, the growing chain of crosses becomes a reward in itself, and you won't want to break it.

**Celebrate small wins.** BJ Fogg suggests a tiny celebration right after you do a new habit: a fist pump, saying "yes!" or "good job", a smile. It sounds silly. It's meant to create a small burst of good feeling that your brain links with the habit.

**Bundle it with something you enjoy.** In Chapter 2's terms, this raises the value of the task. In a study published in 2014, Katherine Milkman, Julia Minson and Kevin Volpp gave some gym members access to gripping audiobooks they could only listen to at the gym. Those members went to the gym more often than people in a comparison group, at least until a holiday break interrupted the routine. Milkman called this temptation bundling. You might allow yourself a favorite podcast only while cleaning, a special coffee only during your deep work block, or a particular playlist only for study sessions.

## Make bad habits harder

The same tools work in reverse for habits you want to break.

**Hide the cue.** If you always check your phone first thing in the morning, don't keep it by the bed. If you always snack while studying, don't keep snacks on the desk. If a particular app always pulls you in, remove it from your home screen.

**Add friction.** Log out of social media after each use. Put the television remote in a drawer. Unplug the game console and put it in a cupboard. Every extra step gives you a chance to notice what you're doing.

**Replace the routine.** Habits meet needs. As you saw in Chapter 6, if you remove a habit without replacing it, the need remains and the habit usually returns. Keep the cue and the reward, but change the routine. Bored at your desk? Instead of opening a video, stand up and stretch. Want a break from studying? Instead of scrolling, step outside for five minutes.

**Notice and name.** When you catch yourself in a bad habit, simply naming it ("there's the phone habit again") creates a small gap between the cue and the routine. In that gap, you can make a different choice.

## Build a starting routine

One of the most useful habits for focus is a consistent way to start your working day. Without one, many people drift into the day through email, news and messages, and by the time they reach their important work, half their best energy is gone.

A starting routine can be short. Daniel's took about ten minutes:

1. Make coffee.
2. Put phone in the drawer, switch on work focus mode.
3. Read the MIT card from the night before.
4. Open the project file. Close everything else.
5. Write the target for the first block.
6. Start a 45-minute timer.

Tariq's, for his early-morning coding sessions, was even simpler: get up, drink a glass of water, sit at the kitchen table where he'd left the laptop open the night before, start the timer.

The important thing isn't what's in the routine. It's that it's the same every day, so it becomes automatic and carries you into your work without a fight.

## Identity: who you're becoming

James Clear makes a point about habits that many people find powerful: every action you take is a vote for the type of person you want to become.

Each time you sit down for a focus session, you cast a vote for being someone who focuses. Each time you put your phone away during dinner, you cast a vote for being someone who's present with their family. One vote doesn't decide anything. But as the votes add up, your sense of who you are begins to change, and so do your habits.

This is a useful way to think about bad days, too. A missed session is one vote the other way. It doesn't undo the hundred votes you've already cast. Just make sure the next vote goes the right way.

## Don't try to change everything at once

There's a temptation, when you read a chapter like this, to design a perfect new life: wake at 5:30, meditate, exercise, journal, deep work, healthy lunch, no phone, read for an hour, bed at ten.

Most people who try this last a week.

Habits take time and attention to build, and you only have so much of both. Choose one or two new habits at a time. Make them small. Get them to the point where they feel automatic, which may take a couple of months. Then add another.

It feels slow. It's actually the fastest route, because the habits you build this way tend to last.

## Try this today

Design one new focus habit.

1. Choose one habit that would make the biggest difference to your focus. It might be your starting routine, putting your phone away after dinner, writing tomorrow's MIT each evening, or one daily focus session.
2. Write a habit stack: "After I [existing habit], I will [new habit]."
3. Make it tiny. What's the two-minute version?
4. Decide on an immediate reward: a tracker mark, a small celebration, or a temptation bundle.
5. Set up the environment: make the cue obvious and remove any friction.
6. Start a habit tracker. It can be a calendar on your wall, a page in your notebook, or an app. Mark today, if you do it.

Then choose one bad habit to weaken, and change one thing about its cue or friction.

### Reflection

- What habits currently fill my mornings and evenings? Which help my focus, and which hurt it?
- What existing daily habit could I use as an anchor for a new one?
- What kind of person do I want my daily actions to vote for?

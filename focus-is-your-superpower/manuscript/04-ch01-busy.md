# Chapter 1: Busy is not the same as productive

On Wednesday morning, Daniel tried an experiment his sister had suggested. Every time he switched from one thing to another, he made a tally mark on a sticky note beside his keyboard. Email to spreadsheet: one mark. Spreadsheet to a message from a colleague: another. Message to the news site he opened without noticing: another.

By lunch, the sticky note was full. He counted eighty-one switches in just under four hours. That is one every three minutes or so. He had not spent more than ten minutes in a row on any single piece of work all morning, and yet, when his wife asked him over lunch how it was going, he said, truthfully, "Flat out. I haven't stopped."

He hadn't. That was the problem.

## Two people, one project

Picture two people given the same project and the same day.

The first starts early. She opens her email, then a few browser tabs she'll need later, then the team chat. She answers questions as they come in, joins a meeting she wasn't sure she needed to attend, and works on the project in the gaps. She switches often and thinks of herself as responsive. At six o'clock she has worked for ten hours and feels exhausted.

The second starts by asking one question: *What would make today a success?* She decides it is finishing the first full draft of the project's budget. She spends the first ninety minutes on that and nothing else, with her chat closed and her phone in a drawer. Then she answers messages in one batch, attends the one meeting that needs her, and spends another hour on the draft in the afternoon. She finishes at five.

The second person worked fewer hours. She also, very likely, got more of what mattered done.

The difference between them is not intelligence, talent or even motivation. It's where their attention went, and how often it was pulled somewhere else.

## Attention is the resource you actually spend

We talk about managing time, but time is the easy part. Everyone gets the same twenty-four hours, and the clock runs whether you're using them well or not. What you actually have to manage is attention: which of the thousands of things around you, and inside you, gets your mind at any given moment.

Attention decides what you notice, what you learn, what you remember and what you produce. You can own the best laptop in the world and have access to every textbook ever written, but if your attention keeps sliding off the page, none of it helps much.

And attention has limits. Your brain can do many simple things at once (walk, chew gum, hum a song) because those tasks are automatic. It cannot do two demanding, thinking tasks at the same time in the way a computer runs two programs. When you try, it switches between them, back and forth, and each switch costs something.

## The myth of multitasking

Multitasking feels efficient. You reply to a message while reading a report, half-watch a lecture while answering email, listen to a podcast while writing. There's a pleasant sense of keeping several plates spinning.

For simple tasks, combining them is fine. Folding laundry with music on is a perfectly good use of an evening. For demanding work, the research has been fairly consistent for more than twenty years. In a well-known set of experiments published in 2001, Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer and Jeffrey Evans had people switch between different kinds of tasks and measured how long the switches took. Every switch cost time, and the cost grew as the tasks got more complex. The losses were fractions of a second per switch, but they add up when you switch hundreds of times a day, and they don't include the larger cost of losing your train of thought.

In 2009, researchers at Stanford compared people who described themselves as heavy media multitaskers with people who rarely multitasked. You might expect the heavy multitaskers to be better at it, through practice. They weren't. On several tests, they were more easily distracted by irrelevant information and slower to switch between tasks than the light multitaskers.

None of this means you're doomed to fail if you ever do two things at once. It means that when a task needs real thought, doing it alone is not a luxury. It's how the work gets done well.

## What an interruption really costs

Think about writing something that matters: an essay, a proposal, a careful email to a client. You read the paragraph you wrote, you begin to form the next idea, you can almost see where the argument is going. Then a notification appears. You glance at it. It's nothing urgent, so you go back.

But where were you? You reread the paragraph. You try to recall the idea that was forming. Some of it comes back. Some of it doesn't. The interruption lasted five seconds; the recovery took two minutes, and the idea you return to is a little flatter than the one you lost.

Gloria Mark, a researcher at the University of California, Irvine, has studied interruptions at work for decades. In one experiment, people who were interrupted while writing emails finished just as fast as those who weren't, and sometimes faster. They compensated by working quicker. But they reported significantly more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. Interrupted work can look fine from the outside and feel much worse from the inside. Over a long day, that strain is a big part of why you feel drained without knowing what you've done.

There's a second cost too, which you'll meet properly in Chapter 8. When you switch away from an unfinished task, part of your attention stays behind with it. You're answering the message, but some of your mind is still on the essay, and when you return to the essay, some of it is still on the message.

## The three kinds of distraction

To fix a problem, it helps to name it. For the rest of this book, it's useful to sort distractions into three kinds.

**External distractions** come from outside you. Notifications, phone calls, a colleague's question, the television in the next room, a child who needs a snack, a messy desk, a browser full of open tabs.

**Internal distractions** come from inside. Boredom, worry, hunger, tiredness, a sudden memory of something you forgot to do, a daydream about the holiday you're planning, the itch to check whether anyone liked your post, the vague dread of a task that feels too hard.

**Structural distractions** come from how your life and work are arranged. Unclear priorities. A to-do list with forty items on it. Being expected to answer messages within minutes. Too many commitments. A calendar with no empty space. A workspace where the bed, the television and the desk share one corner.

Each kind needs a different fix. External distractions usually need changes to your environment: a quieter place, notifications off, the phone in another room. Internal distractions need emotional skills: noticing what you're feeling, tolerating some discomfort, knowing what to do with a wandering thought. Structural distractions need planning, boundaries and sometimes the courage to drop commitments.

No single app or motivational quote solves all three. That's why a real focus system has to deal with your surroundings, your inner life and the way your work is organized, which is what the rest of this book does.

## Busy is a feeling, productive is a result

Here's a simple test. At the end of a day, ask two questions.

*How busy was I?* That one's about how the day felt. It's measured in effort, rushing and fatigue.

*What did I finish that matters?* That one's about results. It's measured in things that are now done, learned or moved forward.

Most people who struggle with focus score high on the first question and low on the second. They mistake the feeling of busyness for evidence of progress. It's an easy mistake to make, because busyness is loud and progress is often quiet. Answering twenty emails feels like twenty things done. Spending an hour thinking through a hard problem can feel like nothing happened at all, until you notice that the problem is solved.

Learning to tell the difference is the first skill in this book. Once you see it, you will start noticing the moments where you choose busy over productive, and you can begin to choose differently.

## Decide before you begin

Many people sit down to work without deciding what they mean to do. They're going to "study", "work on the project", "do some writing". These intentions sound reasonable, but they're too vague to steer by. A vague task gives your mind dozens of possible directions, and when the work gets hard, it takes the easiest one, which is often away from the work altogether.

Compare these two plans.

"I'll study chemistry tonight."

"From 8:00 to 8:40, I'll do the five practice problems on chemical equilibrium at the end of Chapter 6, check my answers against the key, and mark any I got wrong."

The second plan has a start time, a scope and a finish line. Even if the problems are hard, you always know what the next action is. When your attention drifts, you have something specific to come back to.

This is the first rule of focused work, and you'll see it again throughout the book:

*Before you begin an important work session, decide what finishing that session will look like.*

It takes thirty seconds. It changes the whole session.

## Daniel's first change

Daniel didn't try to fix everything at once. After his sticky-note morning, he made one change. Each evening, before closing his laptop, he wrote a single sentence on a card: the one piece of work that would make tomorrow a good day. The next morning, before opening his email, he spent forty-five minutes on that sentence and nothing else.

The first two days were hard. He kept reaching for the email tab out of habit, and twice he opened it before he'd noticed what his hand was doing. By the end of the first week, he had a complete draft of the project plan that had sat untouched for a month. He had not worked longer hours. He had simply given forty-five minutes a day to one thing.

"It was a bit embarrassing, honestly," he said later. "I'd spent weeks thinking I didn't have time for it. I had the time. I was just spending it on everything else."

## Try this today

Run your first focus experiment. It takes about forty minutes, including the notes.

1. Choose one meaningful task, something that matters and that you've been putting off or doing badly.
2. Write down the smallest useful result you could get in twenty-five minutes. Be specific: "outline the three sections of the report", not "work on the report".
3. Put your phone out of sight and reach, ideally in another room. If you can't, turn it face down and switch on a focus or do-not-disturb mode.
4. Close every browser tab and app you don't need for this task.
5. Set a timer for twenty-five minutes.
6. Work only on the chosen task. If another thought or task pops into your head ("I need to email Sara", "what time is the dentist?"), write it on a piece of paper in three or four words and go straight back to work.
7. When the timer ends, stop and answer four questions in your notebook:
   - Did I reach the result I wrote down?
   - What pulled at my attention?
   - At what point did I most want to switch to something else?
   - What one change would make the next session easier?

This isn't a test of your character. You're not trying to prove you have iron discipline. You're collecting evidence about how your attention behaves and what conditions help it.

### Reflection

Before you go on to the next chapter, write short answers to these:

- Which activity eats most of the time I mean to spend working or studying?
- Which kind of distraction affects me most: external, internal or structural?
- What is one change I can make before my next focused work session?

Don't try to solve everything today. Pick one change and test it. Focus improves when good intentions become small, repeatable actions.

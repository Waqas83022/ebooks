# The 30-day focus challenge {.unnumbered}

This challenge turns the main ideas in this book into one small action a day, for thirty days. Most days take between ten and forty minutes. Each one tells you which chapter to read if you want more detail.

A few guidelines before you start:

- **Do it in order.** The days build on each other. Week one is about noticing, week two about your environment and attention, week three about time and starting, and week four about habits and keeping going.
- **Keep a notebook.** Each day ends with the same four questions, and writing down your answers is part of the challenge.
- **Don't aim for perfect.** If you miss a day, do it the next day and carry on. Never miss twice (Chapter 15). If a whole week goes badly, repeat that week.
- **Adapt freely.** If a day's task doesn't fit your life, change it to something that does. The point is the practice, not the exact instructions.
- **Start on a fresh-start day** if you like, such as a Monday or the first of a month.

## The daily check-in

At the end of each day, write short answers to four questions:

1. What did I try today?
2. What distracted me or got in the way?
3. What worked?
4. What will I adjust tomorrow?

It takes two or three minutes. Over thirty days, these notes become a detailed record of how your attention works.

## Week 1: Awareness

This week you're not trying to change much. You're gathering evidence about how your attention, time and energy actually behave.

**Day 1: Track your distractions.** Carry a small notebook or a note on your phone. Every time you notice yourself drifting from what you meant to do, make a quick note: the time, what you switched to, and what you were feeling just before. Don't judge. Just record. (Chapter 1)

**Day 2: Check your screen time.** Do the screen-time audit from Chapter 3. Write down your daily average, pickups and notifications, and your top five apps. Circle the app that costs you most for what it gives. For the rest of the day, say the reason each time you pick up your phone. (Chapter 3)

**Day 3: Identify your procrastination triggers.** Start the procrastination log from Chapter 2. For each thing you put off today, note the task, what you did instead, the feeling, and which of the seven triggers it hits. (Chapter 2)

**Day 4: Choose your most important goal.** Write down the one goal you most want to make progress on in the next three months. Write a sentence about why it matters to you. Then write the very next physical action you could take toward it. (Chapters 11 and 14)

**Day 5: Try one 25-minute focus session.** Do the first focus experiment from Chapter 1: one task, a clear target, phone in another room, unneeded tabs closed, a 25-minute timer and a parking page. Afterward, answer the four experiment questions. (Chapters 1 and 5)

**Day 6: Remove unnecessary notifications.** Go through every app's notification settings and turn off everything that fails the test: "Does a human being need me, right now, through this app?" (Chapter 6)

**Day 7: Review your baseline.** Read back through your notes from the week. Answer: What are my three biggest distractions? When is my focus best and worst? What feeling most often comes before procrastination? Write a short paragraph describing your starting point. (Chapter 20)

## Week 2: Environment and attention

This week you start reshaping the conditions around your work, and training your attention directly.

**Day 8: Create a dedicated focus space.** Clear your desk or work area completely, then put back only what you need for focused work. Turn your seat away from distractions if you can. Close unneeded tabs and turn off desktop notifications. If you share your space, write down the agreement you'd like to suggest. (Chapter 7)

**Day 9: Set a social media boundary.** Choose one specific rule. For example: no social media before noon; checking windows at lunch and 6 p.m. only; the app you circled on Day 2 deleted from your phone; or grayscale on. Write the rule down and keep it today. (Chapter 6)

**Day 10: Practice single-tasking.** Choose three everyday activities and do each with no other input: one meal without a screen, one conversation with your phone out of sight, one walk without headphones. At work or study, do one task at a time, fully, until it's finished or your session ends. (Chapter 8)

**Day 11: Do two focus sessions.** Use your starting session length from Chapter 5 (or 25 minutes). Do two sessions with a real, screen-free break between them. Log both. (Chapters 5 and 12)

**Day 12: Create an if-then plan for distraction.** Look at your distraction notes from Day 1. Choose your most common distraction and write an if-then plan for it: "If [situation], then I will [action]." Write two more for your next most common distractions. Use them today. (Chapter 11)

**Day 13: Try a phone-free period.** Choose a block of at least two hours, such as an evening or a morning, and leave your phone in another room, switched to sleep mode, for the whole time. Notice how often you reach for it and how you feel. Tonight, charge it outside your bedroom. (Chapter 6)

**Day 14: Review what worked.** Read back through week two's notes. Which changes made the biggest difference? Which were hardest? Choose the two or three you'll keep going forward. Check your screen time and compare it with Day 2. (Chapter 20)

## Week 3: Time and execution

This week is about choosing what matters, planning realistic days and getting started on hard things.

**Day 15: Identify your most important task.** Write a full list of everything you need to do this week. Sort it with the urgent-important matrix. Cross out or delegate what you can. Choose up to three big rocks for the week, and tomorrow's MIT. (Chapter 9)

**Day 16: Create a time-blocked schedule.** Plan tomorrow using the ten-minute routine from Chapter 10: fixed commitments, MIT in your best-energy slot, one or two other important blocks, batched shallow work, breaks and at least one buffer. Add 50 percent to every estimate. (Chapter 10)

**Day 17: Break down an avoided project.** Choose a project you've been avoiding. Spend fifteen minutes breaking it into steps, each small enough for one session. Put them in order. Schedule the first step this week. (Chapter 11)

**Day 18: Practice a two-minute start.** Take the first step from yesterday's list. Set a timer for two minutes and do only that. When the timer ends, decide whether to carry on for ten minutes. (Chapter 11)

**Day 19: Reduce unnecessary commitments.** List your regular tasks and commitments. For each, ask: Can I eliminate it? Reduce it? Hand it over? Simplify it? Act on at least one today, even if it's just sending a message to step back from something. Start a not-to-do list. (Chapters 9 and 13)

**Day 20: Try a longer focus session.** Move up one rung on the focus ladder. If you've been doing 25 minutes, try 40 or 50. Prepare carefully: clear target, phone away, everything closed, water ready. Take a full 10-minute break afterward. (Chapters 5 and 12)

**Day 21: Review progress and adjust.** Do your first full weekly review, using the structure in Chapter 20 and the page in the toolkit: clear, review, plan, close. Do a FOCUS check and choose the letter that needs the most attention next week. (Chapter 20)

## Week 4: Sustainable habits

This week is about making the changes last, and protecting the energy that makes focus possible.

**Day 22: Build a morning starting routine.** Design a starting routine of five to ten minutes that leads straight into your first focus session. Write it as a habit stack: "After I [existing habit], I will [first step]." Do it tomorrow morning, and track it. (Chapter 16)

**Day 23: Define a realistic evening shutdown.** Design your shutdown ritual: capture loose ends, look at tomorrow, choose tomorrow's MIT, leave notes on unfinished work, tidy up, say your shutdown phrase. Do it tonight. (Chapter 17)

**Day 24: Create a weekly review.** Put a recurring weekly review in your calendar, and a monthly reset on the first weekend of each month. Copy the weekly review page from the toolkit into your notebook, or print it. (Chapter 20)

**Day 25: Practice recovering from a setback.** Think of a recent time you fell short. Write down what you said to yourself. Then write what you'd say to a friend in the same situation. Do the six-step reset routine. Write a "never miss twice" plan for your most important habit. (Chapter 15)

**Day 26: Protect time for rest.** Look at your sleep, movement and rest from the past week. Choose one change for sleep and one for movement, and start both today. Plan a real day off, with no work or study, in the next seven days. (Chapter 17)

**Day 27: Complete one meaningful milestone.** Choose a small but real piece of progress on your most important goal, something you can finish today in one to three focus sessions. Finish it. Then celebrate in some small way. (Chapter 14)

**Day 28: Assess your new habits.** Look at your habit tracker and your notes. Which new habits feel close to automatic? Which still take effort? Which have you dropped? For each one you want to keep, decide whether to shrink it, keep it the same or grow it. (Chapter 16)

## The final two days

**Day 29: Design your personal FOCUS system.** On one page, write down the system you'll keep using after the challenge. Use the five headings:

- **Find:** how I'll choose what matters (for example, a weekly review with big rocks, and a daily MIT).
- **Organize:** how I'll plan my time (for example, time blocking each evening, with buffer).
- **Control:** how I'll protect my attention (for example, notifications off, phone in the hallway, checking windows).
- **Use:** how I'll manage my energy (for example, deep work in the morning, 50/10 sessions, bed by 11).
- **Sustain:** how I'll keep it going (for example, starting routine, shutdown ritual, habit tracker, never miss twice).

Keep it short enough to fit on one page. Put it somewhere you'll see it. (Start here and Chapter 20)

**Day 30: Commit to a maintenance plan.** Look back at your Day 7 baseline and compare it with where you are now: screen time, focus sessions, how you feel about your work. Write a short letter to yourself about what has changed. Then make a plan for the next 30 days: which practices you'll keep, which one new experiment you'll try, and when your next monthly reset will be. Tell one person about your plan. (Chapter 20)

## After the challenge

Finishing thirty days doesn't mean your habits are fixed. As you read in Chapter 16, many habits take two months or more to become automatic. Keep going with the practices that worked, keep your weekly reviews, and use your monthly reset to adjust.

If you'd like, repeat the challenge in three or six months. Many people find it works even better the second time, because they know which days matter most for them and can adapt the rest.

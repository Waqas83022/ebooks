# Chapter 4: The daydream and the loop

Tariq had been learning to code for seven months. Most evenings after dinner he sat at the kitchen table with his laptop and an online course, and most evenings, about ten minutes in, something happened that he found hard to describe.

He would be reading an explanation of how a loop works, and somewhere in the middle of a sentence he would be gone. In his mind he was already a software developer. He was walking into a bright office, introducing himself to his new team, explaining to his old manager at the accountancy firm that he was leaving. He was telling his teenage son about the app he'd built. The scene was vivid and pleasant and detailed. It felt almost real.

Then he'd blink, and the page in front of him would still be on the same paragraph about loops, and twenty minutes would have passed.

"It's like I'm watching a film of my life," he said, "instead of living it. Sometimes I think I've seen that film more often than I've written code."

## When your mind leaves the room

Some people call this "hallucinating" about the future, or about their goals. It's a vivid way to put it, and you'll probably recognize the experience: the scenes that play out while you stare at a page, so real that you forget where you are.

Strictly speaking, though, it isn't hallucination. A hallucination, in the medical sense, means seeing, hearing or feeling something that isn't there, as if it were actually happening in the world, such as hearing voices or seeing figures that others can't. That's a health matter, not a productivity one, and if it happens to you, the right step is to talk to a doctor. Nothing in this chapter is a substitute for that.

What Tariq experiences is something almost everyone does every day. Psychologists call it mind-wandering: the attention drifting away from the task in front of you to thoughts, images and stories of its own. Sometimes it's a pleasant daydream. Sometimes it's a worry that loops round and round. Sometimes it's just a blank, where you've been reading without taking anything in.

## How much of the time we're elsewhere

In 2010, two Harvard psychologists, Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert, published a study that used an iPhone app to check in on people at random moments during their day. The app asked what they were doing, whether they were thinking about something other than what they were doing, and how happy they felt.

The results were striking. Across about 2,250 adults, people's minds were wandering in 46.9 percent of the samples. Nearly half of waking life, in other words, was spent thinking about something other than what was happening. And people were generally less happy when their minds were wandering than when they weren't, even when the daydreams were about pleasant things. The title of the paper summed it up: "A wandering mind is an unhappy mind."

That doesn't mean you should try to stop your mind from ever wandering. You can't, and you wouldn't want to. It does mean that if you sometimes look up from a page and realize you've been somewhere else, you're not broken. You're a normal human being, and the skill you need is not to stop wandering but to notice it sooner and come back.

## When daydreaming helps

Not all mind-wandering is a problem. A wandering mind is also how you plan, imagine, solve problems and connect ideas. Many people have their best ideas in the shower, on a walk or while washing dishes, precisely because their mind is free to drift.

In 2012, a team led by Benjamin Baird gave people a creative problem, then a break. During the break, some did a demanding task, some rested, and some did an easy task that left plenty of room for the mind to wander. When they came back to the original problem, the easy-task group improved the most. Letting the mind roam, it seems, helped ideas incubate.

So there's a time for daydreaming. The trouble is when it shows up at the wrong time, in the middle of work that needs your full attention, and when it replaces the action instead of fueling it.

## The trap of the beautiful fantasy

Tariq's daydreams were about his goal. He wasn't escaping into something unrelated; he was imagining success. Surely that's motivating?

Not necessarily. The psychologist Gabriele Oettingen has spent decades studying what happens when people fantasize about their goals. In one early study, published in 1991, she followed women on a weight-loss program. Those who had the most positive fantasies about how slim and confident they'd become lost less weight than those whose thoughts about the future were more mixed or realistic. Later studies found similar patterns with students, job seekers and people recovering from surgery.

Her explanation is that a vivid positive fantasy can give your mind a taste of success before you've earned it. You feel a little of the satisfaction already, and that takes away some of the drive to do the work. The dream becomes a reward in itself.

Oettingen's answer, which you'll use in Chapter 14, isn't to stop dreaming. It's to follow the dream with a hard look at what stands in the way. Dream, then picture the obstacle, then make a plan. That combination turns out to be much more powerful than either positive thinking or plain realism alone.

## The worry loop

The other kind of mind-wandering is less pleasant. You're trying to work, and your mind keeps returning to a worry. The argument you had yesterday. The bill you haven't paid. Whether your manager was annoyed in this morning's meeting. What you'll do if you fail the exam.

Psychologists call repetitive negative thinking like this rumination when it's about the past and worry when it's about the future. Both feel like problem-solving, which is why they're hard to stop. Your mind seems to be working on something important. But real problem-solving moves forward: you understand more, you decide something, you take a step. A loop goes round and round and lands back where it started.

Overthinking also shows up as analysis paralysis. You can't start the assignment until you've chosen the perfect topic. You can't launch the shop until you've compared every website platform. You can't send the message until you've rewritten it eleven times. Each option opens more questions, and the action you need to take gets buried under the thinking about it.

## Perfectionism: the high bar that keeps you on the ground

Perfectionism and overthinking often travel together. If you believe your work must be flawless, every decision becomes high-stakes, and every draft is a potential embarrassment.

Healthy high standards are a good thing. They push you to do work you're proud of. The problem comes when the standard is so high that nothing can meet it, and when your sense of your own worth rides on meeting it. Then starting becomes frightening, finishing becomes impossible (there's always one more thing to fix), and sharing your work feels like exposing yourself to judgment.

The psychologist Barry Schwartz and his colleagues described a related pattern in decision-making. Some people are "maximizers": they want the best possible option and keep searching until they're sure they've found it. Others are "satisficers": they set a standard of good enough and take the first option that meets it. In their studies, maximizers often ended up with slightly better outcomes on paper but felt worse about their choices, with more regret and less satisfaction.

For most everyday tasks, good enough is the right target. Save the maximizing for the few decisions that truly deserve it.

## Bringing your mind back

Here are five ways to work with a wandering mind, from the simplest to the more involved.

**1. Notice and name it.** When you realize your mind has wandered, silently name where it went: "planning", "worrying", "daydreaming", "remembering". Then return to the task. Don't scold yourself. The moment of noticing is the skill you're building, and every return is a small repetition, like a lift at the gym. Over time, you'll start noticing sooner.

**2. Use a parking page.** Keep a sheet of paper beside you while you work. When a thought pops up that seems important ("call the bank", "idea for the website"), write it down in a few words and return to work. Your mind often keeps nagging because it's afraid you'll forget. Writing the thought down tells it you won't.

There's research behind this. In 2011, E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister found that unfinished goals tend to intrude on people's thoughts while they're trying to do something else. But when people made a specific plan for the unfinished goal, the intrusions largely stopped, even though the goal still wasn't done. A plan, it seems, is enough to let the mind put something down.

**3. Schedule your worry.** If the same worries keep returning, give them a time slot. Choose fifteen or twenty minutes each day, not too close to bedtime, as your "worry time". When a worry appears outside that slot, write it down and tell yourself you'll deal with it then. When worry time comes, sit down with the list and think properly: what can you actually do about each item? Many people find that by the time worry time arrives, half the worries don't seem so urgent. Researchers have tested this "worry postponement" approach since the 1980s, with encouraging results for many people.

**4. Give the dream a job.** If, like Tariq, you daydream about your goals, don't fight it. Schedule it. Spend five minutes before you start work picturing where you want to go, then picture the main obstacle, then write down your next small step. After that, the dream has done its job, and when it shows up again in the middle of work you can tell it, gently, "Not now. We've already done this today."

**5. Set a "good enough" standard before you start.** For any task you tend to overthink, decide in advance what good enough looks like and how much time you'll give it. "This email needs to be clear and polite, and I'll spend ten minutes on it." "I'll compare three website platforms, not twenty, and choose by Friday." When you hit the standard or the time limit, you stop.

## When it's more than ordinary

Everyone's mind wanders, and everyone worries. But some people find it much harder than others to keep their attention on a task, and some carry worries that are so heavy and constant that they get in the way of daily life.

If you have always found concentration much harder than the people around you, or if your mind races so much that you can't work, sleep or relax, it's worth speaking to a doctor. Conditions such as ADHD, anxiety disorders and depression can all affect attention, and they're treatable. The techniques in this book can still help alongside proper support, but they're not a replacement for it. And, as mentioned at the start of this chapter, if you ever see or hear things that other people don't, please get medical advice.

## What Tariq changed

Tariq's fix was surprisingly small. Before he opened the course each evening, he spent three minutes on what he called his "film". He let himself picture the new job as vividly as he liked. Then he wrote one sentence about what might stop him that evening ("I'm tired and I'll want to watch the match") and one sentence about what he'd do about it ("Code for twenty-five minutes first, then watch the second half").

When he caught himself drifting during the lesson, he made a small tick on his parking page and went back to the code. The first night he made fourteen ticks. Two weeks later it was usually four or five.

"I still daydream," he said. "I'm not going to stop. But now I watch the film once, at the start, and then I go and do the work that makes it true."

## Try this today

Do a twenty-minute mind-wandering check.

1. Choose a task that needs concentration, and set a timer for twenty minutes.
2. Keep a sheet of paper beside you, divided into two columns: "Wandered" and "Parked".
3. Each time you notice your mind has wandered, put a tick in the first column and silently name where it went (worrying, planning, daydreaming, remembering). Then go back to the task.
4. Each time a useful thought or to-do appears, write it in a few words in the second column and go back to the task.
5. At the end, count the ticks. Don't judge the number. It's a starting point, and you can check again in a couple of weeks to see whether it has changed.

If you're a regular worrier, also choose a fifteen-minute worry time for tomorrow and write it in your calendar.

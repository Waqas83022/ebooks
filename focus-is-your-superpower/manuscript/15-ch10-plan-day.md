# Chapter 10: Plan the day you'll actually live

Sana's study timetable was a thing of beauty. She'd made it on a Sunday evening, color-coded, in a spreadsheet: every hour of every day for three weeks before her exams, each one assigned to a subject. Pharmacology from 8 to 10. Chemistry from 10 to 12. Lunch. Physiology from 1 to 3. And so on, until 10 p.m., every day, including weekends.

By Tuesday it had collapsed. She'd overslept on Monday and was an hour behind before she started. A lab session she'd forgotten about took up Tuesday afternoon. A friend's birthday dinner swallowed Tuesday evening. By Wednesday the timetable was so far out of date that looking at it only made her feel guilty, so she stopped looking at it.

The problem wasn't that Sana planned. The problem was that she planned a day no human being could live.

## Why plans fail

Most daily plans fail for one of four reasons.

**They're too full.** Every hour is assigned, with no room for travel, meals, rest, interruptions or things taking longer than expected.

**They're too optimistic.** Each task gets the time it would take on a perfect day, with no problems, no tiredness and no distractions.

**They're too vague.** "Study" or "work on project" doesn't tell you what to actually do when you sit down.

**They're too rigid.** When one thing goes wrong, the whole plan falls apart, and there's no easy way to adjust.

The planning methods in this chapter are designed to avoid all four.

## The planning fallacy

The second problem, optimism, is so common that psychologists have a name for it. In the 1970s Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky described the planning fallacy: our tendency to underestimate how long tasks will take, even when we know from experience that similar tasks took longer in the past.

In a well-known study published in 1994, Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross asked psychology students to estimate when they'd finish their final-year theses. On average, they predicted about 34 days. The actual average was about 56 days. Most of them finished later than they had predicted, and even when asked for a worst-case estimate, many of them still finished later than that.

We plan for the best case and then live in the normal one. So a good plan builds in that gap from the start.

A rough rule many people find useful: estimate how long a task will take, then add 50 percent. If you think the report will take two hours, give it three. If you finish early, that's a bonus. Over a few weeks, compare your estimates with what actually happened, and adjust.

## Time blocking

Time blocking means giving your tasks a place in your calendar, not just a place on a list. Instead of "draft project plan" floating on a to-do list, you have "9:00 to 10:30: draft project plan, risks section" in your calendar, like a meeting with yourself.

This does several things at once. It forces you to be realistic, because you can see how much time you actually have. It protects your important work from being swallowed by other demands, because the time is already taken. And it removes a lot of deciding during the day, because the plan has already decided what's next.

You don't need to block every minute. In fact, you shouldn't. A good time-blocked day has:

- **One to three blocks for important work**, usually your deep work and your MIT.
- **One or two blocks for shallow work**, such as email, messages and admin, batched together.
- **Fixed commitments**: classes, meetings, appointments.
- **Breaks and meals**, actually written in.
- **Buffer time**: empty blocks of 30 to 60 minutes, spread through the day, for the things that run over or come up unexpectedly.

The buffer is what makes the plan survive. When the meeting runs long or a task takes longer than you thought, you move the next block into the buffer instead of throwing away the whole plan.

## Timeboxing

Timeboxing is a close cousin of time blocking. The difference is emphasis. Time blocking says *when* you'll work on something. Timeboxing says *how long* you're willing to spend on it, and then you stop.

This is useful for tasks that tend to expand. In 1955, the historian Cyril Northcote Parkinson wrote a humorous essay in *The Economist* that began, "It is a commonplace observation that work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." That's now known as Parkinson's law, and you've probably seen it in action. Given a week to write a two-page report, many people will take a week. Given an afternoon, they'll take an afternoon.

So for tasks that don't need to be perfect, give them a box. "I'll spend 30 minutes on these emails." "I'll give the presentation slides 90 minutes." "I'll research website platforms for one hour, then choose." When the time's up, you finish what you're doing and move on.

Timeboxing is especially useful for perfectionists like Amira. It's much easier to stop polishing when the decision has already been made.

## A daily plan in ten minutes

Here's a simple routine for planning a day. Do it either the evening before (many people prefer this, because it means the next morning starts with a clear plan) or first thing in the morning, before you check messages.

1. **Look at your calendar.** Note your fixed commitments for the day.
2. **Choose your MIT** (Chapter 9). What's the one task that would make this a good day?
3. **Choose up to two other important tasks**, if there's realistically room.
4. **Block time for them**, with the 50 percent rule. Put the MIT in your best-energy slot.
5. **Block one or two windows for shallow work**: email, messages, admin.
6. **Add breaks, meals and at least one buffer block.**
7. **Write down a clear target for each important block.** Not "work on essay" but "write the paragraph on side effects, about 300 words".
8. **Check the plan against reality.** Does it leave room to breathe? If not, remove something.

Some people do this on paper, some in a calendar app. It doesn't matter much. What matters is that you do it every day, until it becomes as automatic as brushing your teeth.

## Capture everything else

Planning one day well is much easier when you're not also trying to remember everything else.

David Allen, in his book *Getting Things Done*, makes a point that many people find freeing: your mind is a poor place to store tasks. When you try to remember everything you need to do, your mind keeps reminding you, at random times, usually when you're trying to concentrate on something else. That's the same effect you met in Chapter 4, where unfinished goals intrude on your thoughts until you make a plan for them.

The answer is to capture. Keep one trusted place, a notebook, a notes app or a simple list, where every task, idea and commitment goes the moment it appears. Then, once a day or once a week, go through it and decide what to do with each item: do it, schedule it, hand it to someone else, or delete it.

The key word is trusted. If you only write down some things, or you never look at the list, your mind won't trust it and will keep nagging. If you capture everything and review it regularly, your mind can let go.

## When the day goes wrong

It will. The question is how to recover without abandoning the plan entirely.

When something knocks the day off course, take two minutes to replan. Look at what's left of the day. Ask: *What's the most important thing I can still do?* Move it into the next available block. Drop or postpone the least important things. Use the buffer.

Don't try to catch up by working late every time. That leads to tiredness, which makes the next day worse. If you consistently can't fit your plan into a reasonable day, the plan is too big. Make tomorrow's smaller.

And if a whole day goes badly, write it off and start fresh tomorrow. One bad day doesn't ruin anything. A week of guilt about one bad day does. (Chapter 15 has much more on recovering from setbacks.)

## Plan the week too

Daily plans work best inside a weekly view. Once a week, ideally on the same day each week, spend twenty to thirty minutes looking at the week ahead:

- What are my big rocks this week?
- When will I work on each one?
- What fixed commitments do I have?
- Where are the gaps, and where are the crunch points?

Then, each day, your daily plan simply picks up the next piece. Chapter 20 has a full weekly review routine.

## Sana's second timetable

Sana's second attempt looked very different from the first.

Instead of planning every hour, she planned three study blocks a day, each fifty minutes, with ten-minute breaks: one in the morning, one in the afternoon, one in the early evening. On days with labs or lectures, she planned two. Each block had a specific target, written the night before: "Pharmacology: practice questions on anticoagulants, 15 questions, check answers." The rest of the day was left open for classes, meals, friends, rest and catching up.

She also left Sunday afternoon empty, as a buffer for anything that hadn't fitted during the week.

"It felt like far too little," she said. "Two or three hours a day, when everyone else was saying they were studying for ten. But I actually did the two or three hours, every day. And by the exams I'd covered everything, which never happened with the color-coded version."

## Try this today

Plan tomorrow in ten minutes, using the routine above.

1. Write down your fixed commitments.
2. Choose your MIT and up to two other important tasks.
3. Estimate the time each will take, then add 50 percent.
4. Block them into your calendar or a paper schedule, with the MIT in your best-energy slot.
5. Add one or two windows for shallow work, your breaks and meals, and at least one buffer block.
6. Write a specific target for each important block.
7. Look at the whole plan and ask, honestly: *Could a normal, tired human being do this?* If not, remove something.

Tomorrow evening, compare what you planned with what happened. Note where your estimates were wrong.

# Chapter 17: Protect your energy

For the first few months of his course, Tariq's routine looked heroic on paper. Work from 8:30 to 5:30. Home by 6:15. Dinner. Coding from 8:00 to 11:00, sometimes midnight. Up at 6:30 for work again. Weekends: four or five hours of coding each day.

He was proud of it, and for a while it worked. Then it didn't. By the third month he was falling asleep at the kitchen table with his hands on the keyboard. He made mistakes at his day job that he'd never made before. He snapped at his kids over nothing. And his coding slowed to a crawl. He'd stare at a problem for an hour that he'd have solved in ten minutes when he was fresh.

"I thought the answer was to push harder," he said. "I was getting maybe five and a half hours of sleep a night. Then I'd drink more coffee and push harder. It took me far too long to realize I was making everything worse."

## Focus runs on energy

Everything in this book depends on a basic fact: attention needs energy. When you're well rested, fed, moving and not overwhelmed by stress, focus comes much more easily. When you're exhausted, hungry, sedentary and stressed, even the best techniques struggle.

This chapter covers the foundations: sleep, movement, food, rest, and the boundaries that protect them. None of it is glamorous. All of it matters more than any productivity app.

## Sleep comes first

If you only improve one thing in this chapter, make it sleep.

Sleep affects almost every part of focus. When you're short of sleep, your attention lapses more often, your working memory holds less, you're more easily distracted, you find it harder to control impulses, and you feel worse, which makes procrastination more tempting. In the short term, coffee can hide some of this. It can't hide all of it, and it can't make up for weeks of short nights.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society published a joint recommendation in 2015 that adults should regularly sleep seven hours or more a night for good health. Some people need more. Teenagers and young adults often need more still. Very few people do well on much less, even if they've told themselves for years that they're fine.

Some practical ways to improve sleep:

- **Keep a consistent schedule.** Go to bed and get up at roughly the same times every day, including weekends if you can. Your body clock works best with regularity.
- **Take the phone out of the bedroom** (Chapter 6). This alone helps many people.
- **Wind down.** Spend the last thirty to sixty minutes before bed doing something calm: reading a paper book, a warm shower, light stretching, talking, prayer or quiet reflection. Avoid work email, intense games and the news.
- **Watch the caffeine.** Caffeine stays in your body for hours. In a 2013 study by Christopher Drake and colleagues, a dose of caffeine taken even six hours before bed reduced people's total sleep by more than an hour. A reasonable rule for many people is no caffeine after early afternoon.
- **Keep the bedroom dark, cool and quiet.**
- **Get morning light.** Spending some time outside in daylight in the morning helps set your body clock, which makes it easier to fall asleep at night.

If you snore heavily, regularly wake up exhausted after a full night, or lie awake most nights despite good habits, talk to a doctor. Sleep problems such as sleep apnea and insomnia are common and treatable.

## Move your body

Physical activity helps focus in two ways. In the short term, a burst of exercise, even a brisk walk, often leaves people feeling more alert and better able to concentrate. In the long term, regular activity supports better sleep, better mood and better health, all of which support attention.

The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines recommend that adults do at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as brisk walking or cycling, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, along with muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days. That sounds like a lot, but 150 minutes is just over twenty minutes a day.

You don't need a gym. Walk to the shops. Take the stairs. Go for a walk at lunch. Do ten minutes of bodyweight exercises in your living room. Use your work breaks to move (Chapter 12).

If you sit for most of the day, as most students and office workers do, it's also worth breaking up long periods of sitting, by standing up and moving around for a few minutes every half hour or so.

## Food and water

You don't need a special diet to focus. But a few basic things help most people.

- **Don't skip meals and then crash.** Long gaps without food can leave you irritable and unfocused, and then you're more likely to grab something sugary that gives you a quick high and a slump.
- **Eat regular meals with some protein, fiber and vegetables.** They give steadier energy than meals made mostly of sugar and white flour.
- **Be careful with very heavy lunches** if you have important work in the afternoon. Many people find a lighter lunch helps with the afternoon dip.
- **Drink water.** Even mild dehydration can make people feel tired and less focused. Keep a bottle on your desk.
- **Notice how alcohol affects you.** Alcohol may help you fall asleep, but it tends to disrupt sleep later in the night, which can leave you foggy the next day.

## Rest is not laziness

Many people who struggle with productivity feel guilty when they rest. If they're not working, they feel they should be. So they half-rest: watching television while answering emails, scrolling in bed, "relaxing" while thinking about their to-do list. They never really work, and they never really rest.

Real rest is something you do on purpose, without guilt, because you need it. It's part of the system, not a break from it.

Different kinds of rest help with different kinds of tiredness:

- Physical rest: sleep, naps, lying down, gentle stretching.
- Mental rest: activities that let your mind relax, like a walk without headphones, gardening, cooking, a hobby with your hands.
- Social rest: time with people who make you feel good, without the demands of work.
- Sensory rest: time away from screens, noise and bright lights.
- Creative or spiritual rest: music, art, nature, prayer, reflection, anything that reminds you what matters to you.

Notice which kind you need most. A person who's been in back-to-back video calls all day may need sensory and social quiet. A student who's been studying alone for hours may need social time and movement.

## The shutdown ritual

One of the most important boundaries for focus is a clear end to the workday. Without one, work leaks into the evening. You check email "just once" after dinner. You lie in bed thinking about tomorrow's tasks. You never fully switch off, so you never fully recover.

Cal Newport describes a shutdown ritual: a short, fixed routine at the end of each workday that closes everything down. A version you can adapt:

1. **Check your inbox and messages one last time**, and capture anything that needs doing.
2. **Look at your task list and calendar for tomorrow.**
3. **Choose tomorrow's MIT** and write it down (Chapter 9).
4. **Leave a note** on any unfinished task about where you stopped and what comes next (Chapter 8).
5. **Tidy your desk** so it's ready for tomorrow.
6. **Say a phrase** that marks the end. Newport uses "shutdown complete". You might prefer "done for today". The phrase is a signal to your mind that work is finished.
7. **Close the laptop**, switch off work notifications, and leave your workspace.

The whole ritual takes ten to fifteen minutes. It means that when a work thought pops up in the evening, you can tell yourself honestly: *I've already dealt with that. It's on the list for tomorrow.*

For people who work from home, like Daniel, the shutdown ritual is especially important, because there's no commute to mark the change. Daniel started ending each day with a ten-minute walk around the block, which became his "commute home". When he came back, work was over.

## Boundaries

Energy is also protected by boundaries: limits on how much of your time and attention others can take.

Some useful boundaries:

- **Work hours.** A clear start and end time, which you keep to on most days.
- **Response times.** Agreed expectations about how quickly you'll reply to messages outside work hours (often: not until the next working day).
- **Notification-free time.** Evenings, weekends or certain hours when work notifications are switched off.
- **Protected time.** Your deep work blocks, your exercise, your family dinners, your day off.
- **A real day off.** At least one day a week, if at all possible, with no work and no study. Many faiths and cultures build this into the week for good reason.

Boundaries can be hard to set, especially at the start of a job or when you're working for yourself. But without them, the most demanding person or task always wins, and your energy drains away without your noticing.

## Watch for burnout

Sometimes tiredness is more than tiredness.

In 2019, the World Health Organization included burnout in its International Classification of Diseases as an "occupational phenomenon", not a medical condition, caused by chronic workplace stress that hasn't been successfully managed. It describes three features: feelings of exhaustion or energy depletion; increased mental distance from your job, or feelings of negativity or cynicism about it; and a sense of reduced effectiveness at work.

Similar patterns can show up in students, carers and people working on demanding personal projects. Warning signs include:

- Feeling exhausted most of the time, even after rest.
- Dreading work or study that you used to care about.
- Becoming cynical, irritable or detached.
- Feeling that nothing you do makes a difference.
- Trouble sleeping, frequent headaches or illness.

If you recognize these in yourself, more productivity techniques are not the answer. You need to reduce the load, increase recovery, and possibly talk to someone: a doctor, a counselor, a manager, a trusted friend. Burnout is a sign that something in your situation needs to change, not a sign that you're weak.

## What Tariq changed

When Tariq finally admitted his routine wasn't working, he made three changes.

He moved two of his coding sessions to early mornings, after discovering (Chapter 13) that his energy was much higher then. To make that possible, he went to bed by 10:30 on weekdays.

He cut his evening coding to a maximum of one hour, ending by 9:30, followed by a wind-down without screens.

He took Sundays off completely. No code, no tutorials, no "just checking" the course forums. Time with his family, a long walk, and an evening with a book.

His total coding hours dropped by about a third. His progress sped up. "I'd been working three tired hours a night and getting about one hour's worth of progress," he said. "Now I work fewer hours and actually get them."

## Try this today

Do an energy check.

1. For the past week, estimate how many hours you slept each night. Write down the average.
2. Write down how much physical activity you did in the past week, in total minutes.
3. Note when you last had a full day with no work or study.
4. Choose one change for this week in each of two areas: sleep (for example, a consistent bedtime, no caffeine after 2 p.m., phone out of the bedroom) and movement (for example, a twenty-minute walk every day).
5. Design your shutdown ritual. Write down the steps you'll do at the end of each workday, and the phrase you'll use to mark the end.

Tonight, do your shutdown ritual for the first time.

### Reflection

- When do I feel most energetic, and what's different about those days?
- What am I trying to make up for with caffeine, late nights or pushing harder?
- What boundary do I most need to set, and who do I need to tell?

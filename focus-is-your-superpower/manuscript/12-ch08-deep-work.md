# Chapter 8: Deep work and single-tasking

Amira's best design work had always happened in a strange place: on the night bus home from her parents' house, two hours each way, once a month. No Wi-Fi. A sketchbook on her knee. Nothing to do but think.

For a long time she assumed it was the bus itself, some magic in the motion and the dark windows. Then one weekend she tried an experiment. She turned off the router at home, put her phone in a drawer, and sat at her kitchen table with the same sketchbook for two hours. She came up with three logo ideas for a client, and one of them was the best thing she'd drawn in months.

It wasn't the bus. It was the two hours with nothing else in them.

## Two kinds of work

The writer and computer scientist Cal Newport draws a useful line between two kinds of work.

**Deep work** is focused, demanding thinking done without distraction, the kind that stretches your abilities and produces something of real value. Writing an essay, designing a product, solving a difficult problem, learning a hard skill, analyzing data, planning a project, practicing an instrument.

**Shallow work** is the logistical, low-concentration work that can be done while distracted. Answering routine emails, filling in forms, scheduling meetings, most chat messages, tidying files.

Both matter. Shallow work keeps your job and life running. But deep work is where most of the value comes from. It's what moves your goals forward, builds your skills and produces work you're proud of. And it's exactly the kind of work that suffers most from interruptions, switching and constant availability.

The trouble is that shallow work is louder. It arrives with notifications and deadlines and people waiting. Deep work rarely has anyone chasing it. So unless you protect it on purpose, shallow work fills the day and deep work happens only in the cracks, if at all.

## Attention residue

In 2009, the management researcher Sophie Leroy published a study with a memorable title: "Why is it so hard to do my work?"

She had people work on one task, then switch to another. Sometimes they had finished the first task before switching; sometimes they hadn't. Those who switched away from an unfinished task performed worse on the second one. Part of their attention stayed with the first task, still thinking about it, still wondering how it would turn out. Leroy called this attention residue.

Think about what that means for a normal working day. You're writing a report. You glance at your email "just quickly" and see a message from a client. You don't reply, you go back to the report, but now part of your mind is drafting the reply. Ten minutes later you check the team chat. Someone has asked a question. You answer it and go back to the report, but part of you is wondering whether your answer was right.

Each check leaves a little residue. By lunchtime you've done hours of "work" on the report with only part of your mind present.

## Single-tasking

The answer is single-tasking: doing one thing at a time, fully, until it's finished or until a planned break.

That sounds obvious, but for many people it's a radical change. It means not checking email while you write. Not answering messages during a lecture. Not having a video on while you study. Not reading the news while you're on a call.

Single-tasking feels slower at first. It isn't. You'll make fewer mistakes, think more clearly, and finish tasks in less total time, because you're not paying the switching cost over and over.

It also feels better. Many people describe an unexpected sense of calm when they first work on one thing with nothing else pulling at them. The constant background hum of *I should check... I need to reply... what about...* goes quiet.

## Make deep work a fixed appointment

The most reliable way to protect deep work is to schedule it like an appointment and treat it as one.

Choose a regular time when you'll do your most demanding work. For many people, mornings work best, before the day's demands pile up and while energy is high. For others, especially night owls, it's late evening. Choose based on your own energy, not on what productivity articles say.

Then block it in your calendar. If your calendar is shared at work, give the block a name that others will respect, such as "Project planning" or "Report writing", and mark yourself as busy.

Consistency helps a lot. If deep work happens at the same time every day, it becomes a habit and you no longer need to decide each morning whether to do it. Daniel's 8:00 to 8:45 block, before email, became so automatic within a month that he felt strange on days he couldn't do it.

How much deep work is realistic? For most people, somewhere between one and four hours a day. Even very successful writers, scientists and artists often say they do only three or four hours of their hardest thinking a day. If you currently manage none, aim for one block of forty-five to ninety minutes. That alone can change what you achieve in a year.

## Batch the shallow work

If deep work gets fixed blocks, shallow work gets batches.

Instead of answering email as it arrives, choose two to four times a day to deal with it all at once. Instead of returning calls throughout the day, return them all at 4 p.m. Instead of doing small admin tasks whenever they occur to you, keep a list and do them together in a single half-hour block.

Batching reduces switching, because you're doing similar tasks together. It also shrinks the total time shallow work takes. Twenty emails answered in one sitting take far less time than twenty emails answered one at a time across the day, each with its own switch in and out.

There's some direct evidence that this helps. In a 2015 study, Kostadin Kushlev and Elizabeth Dunn at the University of British Columbia asked people to check their email only three times a day for one week, and as often as they liked in another week. During the limited week, people reported significantly less daily stress, and lower stress in turn went along with greater well-being.

## Handling other people's expectations

The biggest worry most people have about batching is how others will react. "If I don't reply within ten minutes, my boss will think I'm slacking." "My clients expect instant answers."

Sometimes that's true. Some jobs genuinely need fast replies, and Chapter 19 looks at how to find focus in those jobs. But often the expectation is weaker than we assume. Many people reply instantly not because anyone demands it, but because they've trained everyone, including themselves, to expect it.

A few things help:

- **Tell people your rhythm.** "I check email at 9, 12 and 4. If something's urgent, call me." Most people will adapt.
- **Use status messages.** Set your chat status to "Focusing until 11, will reply after" during deep work.
- **Agree on what urgent means.** With your manager or team, decide which kinds of things need an immediate response and how they'll reach you (usually a phone call), so that everything else can wait.
- **Be reliable in your windows.** If you say you'll reply by 4, reply by 4. Trust grows when people see that waiting works.

Amira set up an automatic reply on her shop's messages: "Thanks for getting in touch! I reply to messages twice a day, at around 11 a.m. and 5 p.m. For urgent order problems, please email orders@..." She worried customers would be annoyed. Instead, a few told her they liked knowing when to expect an answer.

## Before you switch, leave a note

Sometimes you have to stop a task before it's finished. The meeting starts, the child wakes up, the day ends. Attention residue will follow you out of the task unless you give your mind a reason to let go.

Before you switch, take one minute to write a short note: where you've got to, what the next step is, and anything you need to remember. "Finished section 2. Next: write the paragraph on costs, using the figures in the green folder. Check the March number with Priya."

Two things happen. First, your mind can let go, because it knows the information is safe. Second, when you come back, you can pick up in seconds instead of minutes, because the next step is waiting for you.

## The deep-work ritual

Many people who do a lot of deep work use a short ritual to get started. It doesn't need to be elaborate. The point is to signal to your mind, in the same way each time, that deep work is beginning.

A ritual might include:

- A fixed place (Chapter 7).
- A drink, such as a cup of tea or coffee, made the same way.
- Putting the phone in its home and switching on a focus mode.
- Closing everything on the computer except what's needed.
- Writing down the target for this session.
- Starting a timer.

It takes three or four minutes. After a few weeks, the ritual itself starts to bring on a focused state, the way brushing your teeth at night starts to make you feel sleepy.

## Single-tasking outside work

Single-tasking isn't only for work. Try it in the rest of your life too.

Eat a meal without a screen. Have a conversation with your phone in your pocket, not on the table. Watch a film without scrolling through your phone at the same time. Walk without headphones. Play with your children without checking messages.

These moments train the same skill: being fully where you are. They also tend to make the experience itself richer. Food tastes better when you notice it. Conversations go deeper when the other person has your full attention. And relationships improve when the people you love stop competing with a screen.

## Try this today

Plan and protect one block of deep work for tomorrow.

1. Choose the most important piece of demanding work you're facing right now.
2. Choose a time tomorrow, ideally when your energy is highest, and block 45 to 90 minutes in your calendar. Give it a clear name.
3. Write down what you want to achieve in that block.
4. Decide your ritual: where you'll sit, what you'll drink, where your phone will go, what you'll close.
5. Choose two or three times tomorrow when you'll check email and messages, and write them down.
6. If you need to, tell one or two people about your new rhythm.

Tomorrow, do the block. When it ends, write a one-line note about how it went.

### Reflection

- How much of my average day is deep work, and how much is shallow?
- When am I usually interrupted, and by whom (including myself)?
- What would change in my work or studies if I did one hour of deep work every day for a year?

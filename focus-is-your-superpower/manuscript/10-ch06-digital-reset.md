# Chapter 6: The digital reset

Most advice about phones falls into one of two camps. One says throw it in a lake: delete everything, buy a basic phone, live like it's 1995. The other says it's fine, just use it mindfully, as if a little more mindfulness could outmatch a billion-dollar engineering team.

Neither works for most people. You probably can't give up your smartphone. You need it for work, for family, for banking, for maps, for the bus timetable. And "be mindful" is advice you've almost certainly already tried.

This chapter takes a middle road. You'll keep your phone and the parts of it that genuinely help you. You'll change its settings, its layout and its place in your day so that it stops ambushing you. The goal is to make the phone a tool you pick up on purpose, not a slot machine that picks you up.

You don't have to do every step at once. Read through, then choose three to start with.

## Step 1: Clear out the notifications

Notifications are the phone's way of interrupting you. Most of them don't deserve to.

Go into your notification settings and look at every app. For each one, ask a single question: *Does a human being need me, right now, through this app?*

- A message from your partner, your child, your manager or a close friend: probably yes.
- A phone call: yes.
- A calendar reminder you set yourself: yes.
- Someone liked your photo: no.
- A news alert: no. The news will still be there in an hour.
- A shopping app telling you about a sale: no.
- A game telling you your energy has refilled: definitely no.
- Email: for most people, no. You'll check email on purpose, at set times (Chapter 8 explains how).

Turn off everything that fails the test. For many people that's most of their apps. If you're nervous, turn them off for one week and see whether you miss anything important. You almost certainly won't, and you'll notice how much quieter your days become.

For the notifications you keep, consider turning off the sound and vibration for all but calls and messages from your most important people. Most phones let you mark certain contacts as favorites or emergency contacts who can always get through.

## Step 2: Use focus modes

Modern phones have focus modes, sometimes called do-not-disturb, work mode, study mode or bedtime mode. These let you silence almost everything during certain hours, while still letting chosen people or apps through.

Set up at least two:

- **A work or study mode**, for your focus sessions. Only calls and messages from a short list of people get through.
- **A sleep mode**, starting thirty to sixty minutes before you want to be asleep and ending when you get up.

Schedule them to turn on automatically. Deciding each time is just another chance to decide not to.

## Step 3: Redesign your home screen

The first screen you see when you unlock your phone works like a shop window. Whatever's there gets tapped, often before you've decided to tap it.

Move every app that tends to swallow your time off the home screen. Social media, video, news, games, shopping: put them in a folder on the second or third page, or remove them from the home screen entirely and open them by searching. Leave only tools on the first screen: phone, messages, calendar, maps, camera, notes, perhaps a music app.

This tiny bit of friction works because so much phone use is automatic. If opening an app requires swiping twice and typing its name, you'll often notice what you're doing in time to stop.

## Step 4: Delete the worst app from your phone

Remember the app you circled in Chapter 3, the one that costs you the most for what it gives? Delete it from your phone.

You don't have to delete your account. Most social media works on a computer browser too. Use it there, on purpose, when you choose to sit down for it. You'll find that using a platform on a laptop, without the endless thumb-scroll, is a very different and much less sticky experience.

If you can't delete it, perhaps because you use it for work as Amira does, log out after each use. Having to type your password back in is another small speed bump.

## Step 5: Try grayscale

This one sounds strange, but it's worth a try. Most phones have an accessibility setting that turns the screen black and white. Search your settings for "grayscale", "color filters" or "monochrome".

Apps use bright colors, red badges and vivid images partly because color grabs attention. Remove the color, and the whole phone becomes noticeably less appealing. In a study published by Alex Holte and Richard Ferraro at the University of North Dakota, college students who switched their phones to grayscale reduced their daily screen time significantly compared with how they'd used their phones before.

You'll still be able to do everything you need. You'll just be less drawn to do things you don't. Many phones let you set up a shortcut, such as triple-clicking a button, to switch color back on briefly when you need it, for example to look at a photo.

## Step 6: Give your phone a home

Where your phone sits matters. If it's in your hand or on your desk, you'll check it. If it's in another room, you mostly won't, simply because getting up is effort.

You may have read that the mere presence of a phone on your desk, even switched off, drains your brainpower. That idea comes from a 2017 study by Adrian Ward and colleagues, which got a lot of attention. Later attempts to repeat the experiment have not found the same effect, so it's best treated as uncertain. But you don't need that study to make the case. A phone within reach is a phone you can pick up in half a second without thinking. A phone in another room takes a decision and a walk.

So give your phone a home that isn't your desk and isn't your bed. A shelf in the hallway. A basket in the kitchen. A drawer. During focus sessions, that's where it lives.

## Step 7: Take the phone out of the bedroom

If you can make only one big change, make it this one.

Phones in the bedroom cause two problems. At night, they keep you up: one more video, one more scroll, and then the light and the stimulation make it harder to fall asleep. In the morning, they grab you before you've even sat up. Many people start their day by checking messages, email and news in bed, which means their first thoughts of the day are reactions to other people's demands.

Buy a cheap alarm clock. Charge the phone outside the bedroom. For the first few nights it will feel strange. After a week or two most people say it's one of the best changes they've made.

If you truly need the phone nearby at night, for example because you're a carer or on call, put it across the room, face down, in sleep mode with only emergency contacts allowed through.

## Step 8: Create checking windows

A lot of compulsive checking comes from uncertainty. *Has anyone replied? Did something happen?* If you check whenever you wonder, you'll check all day.

Instead, decide in advance when you'll check social media and messages that aren't urgent. For example: once at lunch, once at 5 p.m., once after dinner, fifteen minutes each. Outside those windows, you don't open those apps.

This works because it replaces a hundred small decisions with one big one. When the urge to check appears at 10:30 in the morning, you don't have to argue with yourself. You already decided: not until lunch.

## Step 9: Use limits and blockers, carefully

Most phones let you set daily time limits for particular apps. When you reach the limit, the app is blocked or you get a warning. There are also apps and browser extensions that block certain sites during set hours.

These can help, especially at the start. The evidence on them is mixed, though. Many people find ways around their own blocks, such as tapping "ignore limit for today" or switching to the browser version. They work best as a reminder and a speed bump, not as a cage. If you find yourself overriding a limit every day, treat that as information: the underlying habit needs a different fix, usually a replacement (see Step 10).

For really stubborn cases, some blockers let you lock settings so they can't be changed for a set period. Use these for your hardest hours, such as exam season or a deadline week.

## Step 10: Replace, don't only remove

Here's the step people most often skip, and it's the one that makes the others stick.

Your phone habit is meeting a need. Boredom, loneliness, stress, tiredness, the wish for a short break. If you take the phone away without meeting the need some other way, the need will win and you'll be back on the phone within days.

So for each situation where you usually reach for your phone, decide what you'll do instead:

- Waiting in a queue or on the bus: a book, a podcast you chose in advance, or just looking around.
- A short break from work: stand up, stretch, refill your water, look out of a window (Chapter 12 has more ideas).
- Feeling lonely: call or message one specific person, instead of scrolling past hundreds.
- Winding down at night: a paper book, a shower, light stretching, talking with someone you live with.
- Stressed or anxious: a walk, a few slow breaths, writing down what's worrying you.

Write your replacements down. Put the book on your bedside table and the alarm clock where the phone used to be. Make the new choice easier than the old one.

## Short-form video: a special case

Short videos deserve a separate word, because many people find them harder to control than anything else on their phone. Each clip is seconds long, so there's no natural stopping point. The algorithm learns very quickly exactly what holds your attention, and the next clip is always ready.

If short videos are your main problem, the steps above still apply, but you may need to be firmer. Many people find "just a few" impossible and "none on the phone" much easier. Some delete the app and allow themselves to watch on a computer only, at set times. Others keep the app but disable autoplay, turn off notifications and set a hard daily limit. Experiment. But be honest with yourself about which approach actually works for you.

## Social media with a purpose

If you want to keep using social media, and many people have good reasons to, try deciding what each platform is for.

Amira did this with a notebook page. She wrote: "Instagram is for posting my work and replying to customers. Not for scrolling other designers' feeds." With that rule, she set her checking windows to twice a day, unfollowed accounts that made her feel inadequate, and used the platform on her laptop for posting.

"I still get sucked in sometimes," she said. "But I notice now, because I know what I'm meant to be doing there. Before, I didn't have a reason. I just went."

Cal Newport, in his book *Digital Minimalism*, suggests a stricter version: take a thirty-day break from optional technologies, then add back only the ones that clearly serve something you value, with clear rules for how you'll use them. If your phone use feels badly out of control, that kind of complete reset can be easier than gradual cutting back.

## When you slip

You will slip. One evening you'll find yourself an hour deep in videos, with no memory of choosing it. That doesn't mean the reset failed.

When it happens, skip the guilt and get curious. What was happening just before you picked up the phone? Were you tired, bored, avoiding something, lonely? Which of your new barriers did you go around, and how? What would make that barrier stronger, or what replacement would meet the need better?

Then reset. Put the phone back in its home, and carry on. A slip is a data point, not a verdict.

## Try this today

Do a twenty-minute digital reset.

1. Go through your notification settings and turn off every notification that fails the test "Does a human being need me, right now, through this app?"
2. Set up a work or study focus mode and a sleep mode, scheduled to turn on automatically.
3. Move every time-swallowing app off your home screen.
4. Choose a home for your phone during focus sessions that isn't your desk.
5. Pick three replacements for the times you usually reach for your phone, and write them down.

Tonight, if you can, charge your phone outside your bedroom.

### Reflection

- Which of these steps will be hardest for me, and why?
- What need is my phone habit meeting, and what else could meet it?
- How will I know, a month from now, that my relationship with my phone has improved?

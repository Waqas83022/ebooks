# Chapter 9: Decide what matters

On Monday morning, Daniel wrote out everything he needed to do that week. It took him twenty minutes and filled two pages: forty-seven items. Approve timesheets. Prepare slides for Thursday's review. Reply to the supplier about the delayed shipment. Book the dentist. Read the new security policy. Draft the project plan. Fix the shelf in his son's room. Call his mother. Update his LinkedIn profile. Finish the online compliance course. On and on.

He looked at the list and felt something between panic and exhaustion. Then, because he didn't know where to start, he did what most people do. He picked off the small, quick ones. Booked the dentist. Approved the timesheets. Replied to three emails. By lunchtime he'd crossed off nine items and felt good about it.

The project plan was item number six. It was still there on Friday.

## Not all tasks are equal

A to-do list treats every task as the same size and the same importance: a line of text, waiting to be crossed off. Real tasks aren't like that. A few of them matter enormously. Most of them matter a little. Some of them don't matter at all.

When everything looks equal, we tend to choose by ease and urgency: the quick things, the things someone's waiting for, the things with a deadline today. That feels productive. You get to cross things off. But it means the big, important, non-urgent work keeps getting pushed down the list until it becomes urgent, usually at the worst possible moment.

Researchers have a name for part of this. In a set of studies published in 2018, Meng Zhu, Yang Yang and Christopher Hsee found that people often chose to do urgent tasks with short deadlines over important tasks with bigger rewards, even when the important task was clearly the better choice. They called it the mere urgency effect. Urgency grabs us, whether or not it deserves to.

So the first job of any focus system is to decide what matters, on purpose, before the day decides for you.

## The urgent-important matrix

In a 1954 speech, the American president Dwight Eisenhower quoted a former college president: "I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent." Decades later, Stephen Covey and other writers turned that idea into a simple four-box tool, which is now often called the Eisenhower matrix.

Every task can be sorted by two questions: *Is it important?* (Does it move forward something I care about, or prevent a serious problem?) and *Is it urgent?* (Does it need attention now, or very soon?)

That gives four groups.

**1. Important and urgent.** Crises, real deadlines, problems that can't wait. A client's urgent request, an exam tomorrow, a burst pipe, a sick child. *Do these now.* But notice how many of them were once important and not urgent, and became urgent because they were left too long.

**2. Important but not urgent.** The work that builds your future: learning, planning, exercise, relationships, long projects, preventing problems before they happen. Daniel's project plan. Tariq's coding course. Sana's revision three weeks before the exam. *Schedule these.* This is the group that gets neglected, and it's where the biggest gains are.

**3. Urgent but not important.** Many interruptions, many emails, many meetings, many requests that feel pressing because someone else wants them now. *Limit, batch or delegate these.* Ask whether they need to be done at all, whether someone else could do them, and whether they could be done more quickly or less often.

**4. Neither urgent nor important.** Mindless scrolling, unnecessary tidying, busywork you do to avoid real work. *Drop these, or keep them for genuine leisure.* Note that leisure itself is not in this group. Rest you choose on purpose is important. It's the drifting, unchosen time that belongs here.

Most people spend far too much time in groups 3 and 4, and far too little in group 2. The aim of the matrix isn't to sort every task perfectly. It's to make the second group visible, so you can give it time before it turns into the first.

## The 80/20 rule

At the end of the nineteenth century, the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto noticed that around 80 percent of the land in Italy was owned by about 20 percent of the people. Later, others noticed similar imbalances almost everywhere: a small share of causes producing a large share of the results. It's now usually called the Pareto principle, or the 80/20 rule.

The numbers aren't exact or universal. Sometimes it's 90/10, sometimes 70/30. But the pattern is common enough to be useful. A small share of your tasks probably produces most of your results. A small share of your study methods probably produces most of your learning. A few of Amira's clients bring in most of her income. A few of Daniel's projects matter most to his director.

To use it, ask these questions about your work:

- *Which few tasks produce most of the value I create?*
- *Which few activities cause most of my stress or wasted time?*
- *If I could only do 20 percent of my list this week, which items would I choose?*

Then put more of your best time into the first group, and look hard at the second.

## The one most important task

Even with the matrix and the 80/20 rule, a long list can still feel overwhelming. So here's the simplest tool in this chapter, and possibly the most useful.

Each day, choose one task, just one, that would make the day a success if it were the only thing you did. Some people call it the MIT, for "most important task". Do it first, or at least before you do anything urgent-but-unimportant.

The question to ask is: *What's the one thing I could do today that would make everything else easier or less necessary?* That's the question the writer Gary Keller built a whole book around, and it cuts through a lot of noise.

Daniel's answer, every day for two weeks, was "the next section of the project plan". Sana's answer was usually "one past-paper question, done properly". Tariq's was "one lesson of the course, with the exercises". Amira's varied: some days it was a design, some days a difficult email to a client.

On busy days, the MIT might be all you manage of your important work. That's fine. One important thing done every day adds up to more than three hundred a year.

## Eat the frog

There's a saying, often attributed to Mark Twain although nobody has found good evidence he said it, that if you eat a live frog first thing in the morning, nothing worse will happen to you the rest of the day. The productivity writer Brian Tracy used the idea as the title of a book: *Eat That Frog!*

The frog is the task you're most likely to put off, usually because it's difficult or unpleasant, and often because it's important. Do it first, before your energy fades and before the day's urgent demands crowd in. Once it's done, the rest of the day feels lighter.

This doesn't suit everyone. Some people need a quick, easy win to get going, a warm-up before the frog. That's fine too. The principle is simply not to let the hardest important task drift to the end of the day, where it's easiest to postpone.

## The not-to-do list

Deciding what matters means deciding what doesn't. Many people find it helps to write that down too.

A not-to-do list is a list of things you've decided to stop doing, or not to start. It might include:

- Checking email before my most important task.
- Accepting meetings without an agenda.
- Watching videos on my phone in bed.
- Agreeing to new projects without checking my calendar first.
- Rewriting emails more than twice.
- Saying yes to every favor asked in the group chat.

Look at your not-to-do list every morning for a few weeks. It's a reminder that every yes to something unimportant is a no to something that matters.

## Learning to say no

Some of the biggest drains on your focus aren't tasks you choose. They're requests from other people: a colleague who needs help, a friend who wants a favor, a committee looking for volunteers, a client asking for "just one small change".

Many of these deserve a yes. Generosity and helping others are part of a good life. But if you say yes to everything, you'll have nothing left for your own priorities, and people will learn that your time is always available.

A few phrases make saying no easier:

- "I'd like to help, but I can't take that on this month."
- "I can't do that, but I could do this smaller part of it."
- "Let me check my calendar and get back to you tomorrow." (This gives you time to decide properly, instead of agreeing on the spot.)
- "If I take this on, what should I drop?" (Useful with a manager who keeps adding work.)

You don't owe anyone a long explanation. A polite, clear no is kinder in the long run than a reluctant yes followed by resentment or a missed deadline.

## Big rocks first

There's a familiar picture, popularized by Stephen Covey, that's worth keeping in mind. Imagine a jar, a pile of big rocks, some gravel, some sand and some water. If you pour in the sand and water first, the big rocks won't fit. If you put the big rocks in first, the gravel, sand and water fill the spaces around them, and everything fits.

The big rocks are your important tasks. The sand is the small, urgent, endless stuff. Put the rocks in first, by scheduling them, and the small things will usually find their place. Leave the rocks until last, and there'll be no room.

## Daniel's new Monday

The next Monday, Daniel still wrote his full list. But then he did three things with it.

First, he sorted it into the four groups of the matrix. That took ten minutes and was surprisingly revealing. Only four of his forty-seven items were truly important and not urgent, and the project plan was the biggest. Eleven items were neither urgent nor important; he crossed out seven of them on the spot.

Second, he chose his big rocks for the week: the project plan, preparing for Thursday's review, and one conversation with his director about his workload. He blocked time for each in his calendar.

Third, he wrote his MIT for Monday on a card: "Project plan: draft the risks section." He did it before opening email.

He finished the project plan on Wednesday, two days ahead of the deadline. The forty other items didn't all get done. Most of them didn't need to be.

## Try this today

Sort your list.

1. Write down everything you need or want to do in the next week. Get it all out of your head.
2. Next to each item, write 1, 2, 3 or 4, according to the urgent-important groups.
3. Cross out or delegate as many items in group 4 (and group 3) as you honestly can.
4. Choose up to three big rocks from group 2 for this week and schedule a time for each in your calendar.
5. Choose your one most important task for tomorrow. Write it on a card or a sticky note and put it where you'll see it first thing.
6. Start a not-to-do list with at least three items.

### Reflection

- Which of the four groups do I spend most of my time in?
- What important but non-urgent task have I been neglecting the longest?
- Who or what do I most need to say no to, and how will I say it?

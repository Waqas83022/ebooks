# Chapter 7: Build a room that helps you work

Daniel's desk was a small table wedged between the bed and the wardrobe. Behind him, in the camera view for every video call, was the laundry basket. To his left was the window onto the street. To his right, close enough to touch, was the bed. On the desk itself sat two coffee cups, a phone charger, his son's toy dinosaur, a pile of unopened post, a notebook, a second notebook, three pens that didn't work and a laptop with twenty-three browser tabs open.

He had never thought about the desk. It was just where the laptop was. But when he started paying attention to how his days went wrong, he noticed that the room kept handing him reasons to stop. The post reminded him of bills. The bed invited him to lie down "for five minutes" after lunch. The street outside was interesting in a way a spreadsheet never was. And because the room was also where he slept, it never fully felt like a place for work.

"I'd been trying to concentrate in a room that was designed for everything except concentrating," he said.

## Your surroundings make decisions for you

We like to think our behavior comes from our choices. A lot of it actually comes from our surroundings.

Psychologists who study habits, such as Wendy Wood at the University of Southern California, have found that a large share of everyday behavior is cued by context: the place we're in, the time of day, the objects around us, the people nearby. You reach for snacks because they're on the counter. You check your phone because it's next to your hand. You work well in the library partly because everyone else is working, and you struggle at the kitchen table partly because it's also where you eat, talk and relax.

This is good news. It means you don't have to rely only on willpower. You can change the setting, and let the setting do some of the work.

## The principle of friction

The simplest way to use your environment is to change how easy things are.

Every action has a little friction: the effort, time or thought it takes to start. A tiny change in friction can make a surprisingly large difference to how often you do something, because so many of our choices are made in a fraction of a second, on autopilot.

So the rule is:

- **Add friction to what distracts you.** Make it a bit harder, slower or more awkward to do.
- **Remove friction from what you want to do.** Make it a bit easier, faster and more obvious.

Phone in another room: friction added. Notes already open on the desk when you sit down: friction removed. Television remote in a drawer: friction added. Running shoes by the door: friction removed. Each change is small. Together they tilt the day.

## One place, one purpose, if you can

If at all possible, keep a place for focused work that you use for nothing else. When you sit there, your brain learns to expect work, the same way it learns to expect sleep when you lie in bed.

It doesn't need to be a whole room. It can be a particular chair, one end of a table, a corner of the library, a specific café. What matters is that it's consistent, and that you don't use it for scrolling, gaming or watching films. If you do those things in the same chair, the chair learns to expect them too.

If you have to use one space for several purposes, which is true for millions of people, you can create the change with a ritual instead. Clear the table. Put out your work things. Switch on a particular lamp. Put on the same headphones. When you're finished, pack everything away. The space changes character because you've changed it on purpose.

## Set up the desk

Look at your workspace and ask, for every object on it: *Does this help me with the work I'm doing now?*

If it doesn't, move it. Not into a drawer by the desk where you'll see it every time you open the drawer; somewhere else.

A good focus desk has:

- What you need for the current task, and nothing more.
- Your parking page and a pen, for capturing wandering thoughts (Chapter 4).
- Water.
- Good light. Daylight is best if you can get it, and a lamp that lights the work, not your eyes.
- A comfortable chair at a height that doesn't hurt your back or neck.

A good focus desk doesn't have your phone, unopened post, food you're trying not to eat, or anything that reminds you of a different task.

Daniel cleared his desk down to the laptop, one notebook and one working pen. He moved the post to a tray in the kitchen, where he went through it every Friday. He turned the desk ninety degrees so he faced a wall instead of the street, and bought a cheap folding screen to hide the bed from both himself and his video calls. The change took an hour and cost less than a meal out. He felt the difference the next morning.

## Sound

People differ a lot in how they handle noise. Some can work in a busy café. Others need silence. Find out which you are by trying both.

If noise bothers you, the options are:

- **Earplugs**, simple and cheap.
- **Noise-cancelling headphones**, more expensive but very effective, especially in open offices and on public transport.
- **Steady background sound**, such as white noise, rain sounds or the general hum of a café. Many people find a constant, meaningless sound easier to ignore than occasional voices.
- **Music**, with care. Research on music while working is mixed. Familiar music without lyrics seems to bother most people less than music with words, especially for reading and writing, because words compete with the words you're trying to process. If you work with music, try a few sessions without it and compare.

Headphones have another use too. In a shared space, they're a signal to other people that you're busy.

## Working in a shared space

Tariq didn't have a study. He coded at the kitchen table, in a house with two teenagers, a wife who worked shifts, and a television in the next room. His focus sessions kept being broken by perfectly reasonable questions: "Dad, where's my charger?" "What are we having for dinner?" "Can you sign this form?"

What helped him was an agreement, not a lock. He sat down with his family and explained what he was doing and why it mattered to him. Then he asked for two things. First, between 8:00 and 9:00 on weeknights, unless something was urgent, could they save their questions until 9:00? Second, if he was wearing his headphones, could they assume he was working?

They agreed, and mostly they kept to it. When they forgot, he'd point at the headphones, smile, and hold up the number of minutes left. He also made sure 9:00 was a real time when he became fully available, which made it easier for everyone to wait.

If you live with other people, a few things make this kind of agreement work:

- **Explain the why.** People are more willing to help when they understand what it's for.
- **Be specific about times.** "I need some quiet sometimes" is easy to ignore. "Between eight and nine" is clear.
- **Use a signal.** Headphones, a closed door, a sign, a particular lamp that's on when you're working.
- **Define urgent.** Agree on what counts as a real interruption: injury, emergency, something that truly can't wait.
- **Keep your side of the deal.** When the focus time ends, be present.

For parents with young children, quiet focus at home may only be possible during naps, early mornings, late evenings, or when another adult is in charge. That's not a failure of planning; it's a season of life. Chapter 19 has more on this.

## When home doesn't work

Sometimes, no matter what you do, home is too full of distractions or too full of people. Then it's worth finding another place.

Libraries are free in most places and full of people working, which helps. Some universities let the public use their libraries. Cafés work for some people, especially for tasks that don't need deep concentration. Some towns have co-working spaces that rent desks by the day. Even a parked car or a quiet corner of a park can work for reading or planning.

The very act of going somewhere to work can help. It creates a clear start (you arrive), a clear end (you leave), and it removes everything at home that might pull you away.

## Your digital environment

Your computer is also an environment, and for most of us it's a cluttered one. Dozens of open tabs, a desktop covered in files, email and chat running all day, notifications sliding in from the corner of the screen.

Apply the same principles:

- **Close what you don't need.** Before a focus session, close every tab and app you're not using for this task. If you're worried about losing them, bookmark them all in a folder called "Later".
- **Use full screen.** Most programs can be made to fill the screen, hiding everything else.
- **Turn off desktop notifications.** Email, chat and calendar notifications are as disruptive on a computer as on a phone. Turn them off during focus sessions, or completely.
- **Separate work and play.** Some people create a separate user account or browser profile for work, with no social media logged in, no entertainment bookmarks and a plain background. Logging in becomes a little ritual that says, "Now I'm working."
- **Tidy the desktop.** A clear desktop is calmer to look at. Put files in folders. It takes ten minutes.

Daniel had been keeping twenty-three tabs open out of a vague fear of forgetting something. When he finally closed them, he bookmarked the few that mattered and found he didn't miss the rest. "Most of them I'd opened weeks before and never looked at again," he said. "They were just sitting there, still asking for attention."

## Make the good choice visible

Friction can work in the other direction too. Put cues for the things you want to do where you'll see them.

If you want to read more, leave a book on your pillow or the sofa. If you want to practice an instrument, leave it out of its case on a stand. If you want to study first thing, leave your notes open on the desk the night before, at the page where you'll start. If you want to drink more water, keep a full bottle on your desk.

James Clear, in his book *Atomic Habits*, puts it simply: make it obvious. A visible cue for a good habit is a small invitation every time you walk past.

## Try this today

Redesign your workspace in thirty minutes.

1. Clear your desk or work area completely. Take everything off.
2. Put back only what you need for focused work: your current task, a parking page, a pen, water.
3. Find a new home for everything else, somewhere you won't see it while you work.
4. Check your light, your chair and your view. If you can, turn your desk so you face away from movement, windows onto busy streets or the television.
5. On your computer, close all unneeded tabs (bookmark them if you must) and turn off desktop notifications.
6. If you share your space, write down the agreement you'd like to suggest to the people you live with: what times, what signal, what counts as urgent.

Then leave tomorrow's first task open and ready, so it's the first thing you see when you sit down.

# Chapter 2: Why you put things off

Amira had a proposal to write. A small architecture firm had seen her work and asked for a quote to redesign their brand: logo, website graphics, a brochure. It was the biggest job she'd ever been offered. It was exactly what she'd been hoping for.

She didn't start it for nine days.

In those nine days she reorganized her design files into new folders. She cleaned her flat. She posted three times on her shop's account, answered every customer message within the hour, and watched most of a documentary about typography that she told herself counted as research. Every evening she opened a blank document titled "Proposal", looked at it for a minute, and closed it.

If you had asked her why, she would have said she was busy. If you'd pushed, she would have said she was lazy. Neither answer was true. She was working hard all week, just not on the proposal. And lazy people don't reorganize their file folders.

What was happening was something much more ordinary and much more human. The proposal made her feel bad, and avoiding it made the bad feeling go away for a while.

## It's about feelings, not time

For a long time, procrastination was treated as a time-management problem. If you put things off, the thinking went, you needed a better calendar, a sharper to-do list or more discipline.

Researchers who study procrastination now see it differently. Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl, two of the best-known names in the field, have argued that procrastination is mainly about mood. When a task stirs up an unpleasant feeling, such as boredom, anxiety, confusion, self-doubt or resentment, putting it off gives instant relief. You switch to something easier and the feeling fades. Your mood improves now, and the cost lands on your future self, who inherits the same task with less time to do it.

In their words, procrastination puts short-term mood repair ahead of long-term goals. The brain is choosing to feel better right now.

That is why procrastination so often looks like productivity. Amira wasn't lying on the sofa. She was doing things that felt useful, because useful-feeling activities give you relief from the avoided task without the guilt of doing nothing. Researchers sometimes call this productive procrastination. Cleaning the kitchen, answering easy emails, organizing your notes, buying new stationery: these are all ways of keeping busy near a task without touching it.

## You are not alone

Procrastination is extremely common. In a large review published in 2007, the psychologist Piers Steel brought together hundreds of studies. He reported estimates that 80 to 95 percent of college students procrastinate, and that around half do it consistently and in ways that cause them problems. Among adults in general, a smaller but still large share describe themselves as chronic procrastinators.

So if you put things off, you're in very large company. The point of mentioning this isn't to excuse it, but to take away the idea that you're uniquely weak. You are a normal human being with a normal human tendency, and normal tendencies can be managed with the right tools.

## What delay actually costs

Delay can look harmless in the short term. In one study from the 1990s, Dianne Tice and Roy Baumeister followed university students through a term. Early on, the procrastinators reported less stress than everyone else. They were, after all, not doing the work. By the end of the term the picture had reversed: the procrastinators were more stressed, reported more illness, and earned lower grades.

That pattern is worth remembering. Procrastination is a loan. It feels good when you take it, and the interest is high.

## The seven triggers

Not every task makes you want to run. Some you do easily, even happily. So what is it about the ones you avoid?

Timothy Pychyl lists seven features that make a task more likely to be put off. The more of them a task has, the stronger the urge to delay. A task is more likely to be avoided when it is:

1. **Boring.** Filing expenses, formatting references, cleaning out the inbox.
2. **Frustrating.** Software that keeps crashing, a form that keeps rejecting your answers, a client who never replies.
3. **Difficult.** A topic you don't understand yet, a problem without an obvious answer.
4. **Ambiguous.** You're not sure what "done" looks like or what's expected.
5. **Unstructured.** No clear first step or order of steps.
6. **Lacking intrinsic reward.** There's nothing enjoyable in the doing of it.
7. **Lacking personal meaning.** You can't see why it matters to you.

Amira's proposal scored high on several. It was ambiguous (how much detail did the firm want?), unstructured (where do you even start a proposal?), difficult (she had never priced a job that size) and, most of all, it touched a sore spot. Writing it meant putting a number on her own worth and waiting to be judged.

That last one deserves its own section.

## Fear in disguise

A lot of procrastination has little to do with the task itself. The real worry is what the task might say about you.

If you never start the essay, nobody can tell you it's bad. If you never send the application, you can't be rejected. If you never publish the video, you'll never read a cruel comment. As long as the work stays unfinished, it stays full of potential, and so do you.

This is why perfectionists often procrastinate more, not less. When the standard is "perfect", every attempt is a likely failure, so the safest move is not to attempt. Some people also delay as a kind of insurance. If the result is poor, they can blame the lack of time instead of their ability. "I only started it the night before" protects your self-image in a way that "I tried my best and it wasn't good enough" doesn't.

None of this is conscious, usually. Amira didn't think, "I am avoiding this proposal to protect my self-esteem." She just felt a heaviness whenever she opened the document and a lightness whenever she closed it. The avoidance did its work without her noticing.

## The procrastination equation

Piers Steel also offered a way of thinking about why we delay that some people find useful. In simplified form, your motivation to do a task goes up when you expect to succeed at it and when you value the result. It goes down when the reward is far away and when you're easily distracted.

You don't need the maths. The four parts are enough, because each one points to something you can change:

- **Raise your expectation of success.** Break the task into steps small enough that you know you can do the first one.
- **Raise the value.** Connect the task to something you care about, or add something enjoyable to it.
- **Shorten the delay.** Create a reward or deadline that comes sooner, such as a small milestone for today, not just the big deadline in a month.
- **Lower your impulsiveness.** Remove the easy escapes before you start, so the temptation is not right there.

You'll find tools for each of these later in the book. For now, notice that "try harder" isn't on the list. Every lever is about changing the task or the setting, not about becoming a different person.

## What helps

Once you see procrastination as a way of escaping a feeling, some useful things follow.

**Name the feeling.** When you notice yourself avoiding something, stop and ask: *What am I feeling about this task right now?* Bored? Anxious? Confused? Resentful? Just putting a word to it often takes some of the heat out. It also tells you which trigger you're dealing with, and that points to the fix. Ambiguity needs clarifying. Difficulty needs breaking down. Boredom needs a time limit or a reward.

**Shrink the first step.** Large, vague tasks are threatening. Small, clear ones aren't. "Write the proposal" is frightening. "Open the firm's email and list the three things they asked for" is not. Chapter 11 goes into this in detail, but the basic move is simple: make the first step so small it feels almost silly.

**Agree to only ten minutes.** Tell yourself you'll work on the task for ten minutes, and after that you're free to stop. Often you won't. Starting is the hardest part, and once you're moving, the bad feeling usually fades. But even if you do stop after ten minutes, you've made progress and you've shown yourself that the task is survivable.

**Expect the discomfort.** You don't have to feel good to start. You can feel reluctant, anxious and bored and still do the work. Waiting until the bad feeling goes away is a trap, because the feeling often only goes away once you've started. Pychyl's advice is blunt and useful: you don't need to feel like doing it. Just get started.

**Forgive yourself for yesterday.** It sounds soft. It isn't. In Chapter 15 you'll read about a study showing that students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before one exam procrastinated less before the next. Guilt and shame make tasks feel even worse, which makes you want to avoid them even more. Letting go of yesterday clears the way for today.

## What Amira did

On the tenth day, Amira tried something a friend suggested. She didn't open the proposal document. Instead, she wrote one line in her notebook: *What am I actually afraid of here?*

The answer came quickly. She was afraid of quoting too high and losing the job, or quoting too low and looking like an amateur. Everything else, the structure, the wording, the layout, she knew how to do.

That changed the task. The problem wasn't "write a proposal". It was "find out what a fair price is". That was a research question, and research she could do. She spent forty minutes looking at what other designers charged, messaged two people she'd studied with, and wrote down a price range. Then she set a timer for twenty-five minutes and wrote the ugliest possible first draft of the proposal, with the number in it.

She sent it two days later. She got the job.

"The proposal took about four hours in total," she said. "The avoiding took nine days."

## Try this today

Start a procrastination log. You'll use it again in the 30-day challenge.

For the next three days, every time you notice yourself putting something off, write down five things:

1. **The task** you're avoiding.
2. **What you did instead.**
3. **The feeling** the task gave you, in one or two words.
4. **Which of the seven triggers** it hits (boring, frustrating, difficult, ambiguous, unstructured, no reward, no meaning).
5. **A smaller first step** that would make it easier to begin.

Then pick one item from your log and do its smaller first step, for ten minutes, today.

### Reflection

- Which task have I been putting off the longest, and what feeling sits underneath it?
- What do I usually do instead when I'm avoiding something? Does it feel productive?
- If a friend told me they'd been avoiding this task, what would I say to them?

# Chapter 11: Start before you feel ready

Tariq's course had a project at the end of the fourth module: build a small working web application of your own design. He had been looking forward to it for weeks. When he reached it, he froze.

He didn't know what to build. When he picked an idea, a tool to help his son track football training, he didn't know where to begin. Should he design the screens first? Set up the database? Read more tutorials? Every evening he opened his laptop, thought about all of it at once, felt overwhelmed and closed it again. Four evenings passed like that.

On the fifth evening his daughter, who was fifteen and had no patience for any of this, looked over his shoulder and said, "Just make a button that says hello."

He laughed. Then he made a button that said hello. It took nine minutes. Then he made it say hello to a name you typed in. Then he made a list of names. By the end of the evening he had the first rough piece of the training tracker working, and he went to bed happier than he'd been all week.

## Starting is the hardest part

For most important tasks, the hardest moment isn't the middle or the end. It's the beginning. Starting means facing the blank page, the unfamiliar problem, the uncomfortable feeling you met in Chapter 2. Once you've started, momentum usually carries you. Getting from zero to one is the hard part.

That's why so many of the most useful focus techniques are really starting techniques. They don't make the whole task easy. They make the first step easy enough that you take it.

## Find the next physical action

David Allen, whose capture system you met in Chapter 10, makes a simple observation: many tasks on our lists aren't really tasks. They're projects. "Plan holiday", "write essay", "build web app", "sort out finances". You can't do a project. You can only do the next physical action of a project.

The next physical action is the very next visible thing you'd actually do. Not "plan holiday" but "text my sister to ask which week she's free". Not "write essay" but "open a document and type three possible titles". Not "sort out finances" but "log in to the bank and download last month's statement".

When a task feels heavy, ask: *What's the very next physical thing I'd do to move this forward?* Write that down instead of the big task. Often the heaviness lifts immediately, because the next action is something you obviously know how to do.

## The two-minute start

There are two popular "two-minute rules", and both are useful.

The first comes from David Allen: if a task will take less than two minutes, do it now instead of writing it down. Reply to the quick email, put the dish in the dishwasher, sign the form.

The second, used by writers such as James Clear, is about starting bigger tasks: make the first step of any habit or task take less than two minutes. Not "study for an hour" but "open my notes and read the first heading". Not "go for a run" but "put on my running shoes". Not "write the report" but "write one sentence".

The trick is that once you've done the two-minute start, you're allowed to stop. Usually you won't. But the permission to stop is what makes starting feel safe.

## The ten-minute agreement

If two minutes is too short to get going, make an agreement with yourself: you'll work on the task for ten minutes, and then you'll decide whether to continue.

You met this idea in Chapter 2. It works because procrastination is driven by the expected unpleasantness of a task, and the expectation is usually worse than the reality. Ten minutes in, the task almost always feels more manageable than it did from the outside. And if it doesn't, you've still done ten minutes, which is ten more than you'd have done otherwise.

## If-then plans

This is one of the best-researched techniques in this whole book, and it's very simple.

An if-then plan, which psychologists call an implementation intention, links a specific situation to a specific action. Instead of "I'll study more", you decide: *If it's 7 p.m. on a weekday, then I'll sit at my desk and open my pharmacology notes.* Instead of "I'll stop checking my phone", you decide: *If I feel the urge to check my phone during a focus session, then I'll write the urge on my parking page and keep working.*

The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer has studied these plans since the 1990s. In 2006, he and Paschal Sheeran reviewed 94 independent tests and found that forming if-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on whether people reached their goals. In a much larger review published in 2024, Sheeran, Gollwitzer and Olivia Listrom analyzed 642 tests. They again found if-then plans helpful across a wide range of goals, and the effects were larger when the plan used a true if-then format, when people were strongly motivated, and when they rehearsed the plan at least once.

Why do they work? A vague intention leaves the decision for later, and later is when you're tired, distracted and tempted. An if-then plan makes the decision now, while you're clear-headed. When the situation arrives, the action is already chosen. You don't have to deliberate; you just follow the plan.

To make a good if-then plan:

1. **Choose a specific cue.** A time ("at 7 p.m."), a place ("when I sit on the train"), or an event ("after I finish dinner", "when I open my laptop").
2. **Choose a specific action.** Something concrete and small enough to actually do.
3. **Write it in the form "If..., then I will..."**
4. **Say it to yourself once or twice**, or picture yourself doing it.

You can use if-then plans for starting ("If it's 8:00 a.m., then I'll open the project plan before my email") and for obstacles ("If a colleague asks me to join a meeting during my focus block, then I'll suggest a time after 11").

## The ugly first draft

A lot of hesitation at the start of a task comes from wanting the first attempt to be good. That's almost impossible. First attempts are usually bad. That's what they're for.

Give yourself explicit permission to make the first version ugly. Write the essay badly, quickly, with gaps and notes to yourself like "[find a better example here]". Sketch the design roughly. Write the code that barely works. The writer Anne Lamott, in her book *Bird by Bird*, gave this kind of draft a famously rude name and argued that every good writer produces one. Generations of writers have found that idea freeing.

Once you have something, anything, on the page, you're no longer creating from nothing. You're improving, and improving is much easier and much less frightening.

Amira now starts every client proposal by writing the worst possible version in fifteen minutes, with the timer on. "Half of it ends up being fine," she said. "The other half I fix. But I'm never staring at a blank page anymore."

## Break the task into steps

Some tasks are so big that even the next action isn't obvious. In that case, spend ten minutes breaking the whole thing down.

Write the task at the top of a page. Below it, list the main parts. Below each part, list the steps. Keep going until each step is something you could do in one session of thirty to sixty minutes. Then put the steps in order.

Tariq's web app, broken down, looked something like this:

- Decide what the app does: list three features.
- Sketch the main screen on paper.
- Build a page with a form to add a training session.
- Save the sessions so they're still there after a refresh.
- Show a list of past sessions.
- Add a simple chart of sessions per week.
- Make it look decent.
- Ask my son to test it.

Nothing on that list was frightening. Each item was one or two evenings' work. The whole project, which had felt impossible, now felt like a series of small, clear steps.

## Stop in the middle

Here's a technique for making tomorrow's start easier: don't finish today.

Ernest Hemingway described his own habit in a 1935 magazine article: "The best way is always to stop when you are going good and when you know what will happen next. If you do that every day when you are writing a novel you will never be stuck."

When you end a work session, stop somewhere you know how to continue. Leave a sentence half-written. Leave the next step obvious. Write a short note, as you learned in Chapter 8, about exactly what comes next. Then tomorrow, instead of facing a cold start, you begin by finishing something you already understand. The momentum carries you into the harder part.

## Lower the bar on bad days

Some days, even the two-minute start feels like too much. You're tired, stressed or low. On those days, lower the bar even further, but don't remove it entirely.

Decide on a minimum version of your important habit: the smallest amount that still counts. For Sana, it was "do one practice question". For Tariq, "open the course and watch one video". For Daniel, "write three bullet points for the plan". For Amira, "open the client file and look at it for five minutes".

The minimum keeps the chain going. It reminds you that you're someone who does this, even on bad days. And quite often, once you've done the minimum, you find you have more in you than you thought.

## Try this today

Choose one task you've been putting off, and use three starting techniques on it.

1. **Find the next physical action.** Write down the very next visible thing you'd do.
2. **Write an if-then plan** for when you'll do it: "If it's [time/place/event], then I will [next action]."
3. **Do a two-minute start** right now, or at the time in your plan. Set a timer for two minutes and do only the next action. When the timer ends, decide whether to continue for another ten minutes.

Then, for one important task or habit, write down its minimum version for bad days.

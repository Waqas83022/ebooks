# Notes and sources {.unnumbered}

The research and history in this book come from the sources below. Where a study is described, I have tried to report its setting and its limits along with its findings. Quotations from older works are given in the wording of a standard English translation, or paraphrased where noted. Sana, Daniel, Omar, Yusuf, Amna and Leila are composites: their names are invented and their stories are built from many lives.

## Introduction {.unnumbered}

The song is Linkin Park, "In the End," from the album *Hybrid Theory* (Warner Bros., 2000). Its chorus is described, not quoted.

The archer image is from Cicero, *De Finibus Bonorum et Malorum* (*On Ends*), Book III, section 22, where the Stoic speaker compares the wise person to someone aiming at a mark. ## Chapter 1: The contract nobody signed {.unnumbered}

Melvin J. Lerner and Carolyn H. Simmons, "Observer's reaction to the 'innocent victim': Compassion or rejection?" *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 4, no. 2 (1966): 203–210. The participants were 72 female undergraduates; the "victim" was a confederate and no shocks were given. Later studies have found the victim-derogation effect to be real but variable.

Melvin J. Lerner, *The Belief in a Just World: A Fundamental Delusion* (New York: Plenum, 1980).

## Chapter 2: Sweat is not a strategy {.unnumbered}

The woodcutters' story is a folk tale told in many versions. The "six hours sharpening the axe" line is widely credited to Abraham Lincoln, but quotation researchers have found no evidence that he said it.

K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Th. Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer, "The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance," *Psychological Review* 100, no. 3 (1993): 363–406. Ericsson's objections to the "ten thousand hour rule" are set out in K. Anders Ericsson and Robert Pool, *Peak: Secrets from the New Science of Expertise* (Boston: Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2016).

Brooke N. Macnamara, David Z. Hambrick and Frederick L. Oswald, "Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: A meta-analysis," *Psychological Science* 25, no. 8 (2014): 1608–1618. The percentages are shares of variance in performance associated with deliberate practice across the 88 studies analyzed, not the proportion of any individual's success caused by practice.

Henry L. Roediger III and Jeffrey D. Karpicke, "Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention," *Psychological Science* 17, no. 3 (2006): 249–255.

Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan, "Are Emily and Greg more employable than Lakisha and Jamal? A field experiment on labor market discrimination," *American Economic Review* 94, no. 4 (2004): 991–1013. The experiment answered job advertisements in Boston and Chicago in 2001 and 2002.

Robert K. Merton, "The Matthew effect in science," *Science* 159, no. 3810 (1968): 56–63.

Warren Buffett has used the phrase "ovarian lottery" in many talks and interviews over several decades, including Berkshire Hathaway shareholder meetings and talks to students. See also Alice Schroeder, *The Snowball: Warren Buffett and the Business of Life* (New York: Bantam, 2008).

Robert H. Frank, *Success and Luck: Good Fortune and the Myth of Meritocracy* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2016).

## Chapter 6: The old man at the frontier {.unnumbered}

The story of the old man at the frontier (*Sai Weng*) is in the *Huainanzi*, chapter 18, compiled at the court of Liu An, King of Huainan, in the second century BCE. For an English translation, see John S. Major, Sarah A. Queen, Andrew Seth Meyer and Harold D. Roth, trans., *The Huainanzi* (New York: Columbia University Press, 2010).

Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011), chapter 17, "Regression to the Mean," which tells the story of the flight instructors and gives the two equations about talent and luck.

Philip Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman, "Lottery winners and accident victims: Is happiness relative?" *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 36, no. 8 (1978): 917–927. The study was small, and later research on adaptation has added important qualifications, especially for serious disability and long-term unemployment.

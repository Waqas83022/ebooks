# Notes and sources {.unnumbered .notes}

The research and history in this book come from the sources below. Where a study is described, I have tried to report its setting and its limits along with its findings. Where the book reports what someone said or wrote, it does so in paraphrase. Sara, Daniel, Omar, Nadia, Maya, Adam and Lina are composites: their names are invented and their stories are built from many lives.

## Introduction {.unnumbered}

The song is Linkin Park, "In the End," from the album *Hybrid Theory* (Warner Bros., 2000). Its chorus is described, not quoted.

The image of the archer comes from Cicero, *De Finibus Bonorum et Malorum* (*On Ends*), Book III, section 22, where the Stoic speaker compares the wise person to someone aiming at a mark: the aim is to be pursued with everything, while the hit itself is something to be preferred, not something on which one's good depends.

## Chapter 1: The contract nobody signed {.unnumbered}

Melvin J. Lerner and Carolyn H. Simmons, "Observer's reaction to the 'innocent victim': Compassion or rejection?" *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 4, no. 2 (1966): 203–210. The participants were 72 female undergraduates; the "victim" was a confederate and no shocks were given. Later studies have found the victim-derogation effect to be real but variable.

Melvin J. Lerner, *The Belief in a Just World: A Fundamental Delusion* (New York: Plenum, 1980).

Ericsson's objections to the "ten thousand hour rule" are set out in K. Anders Ericsson and Robert Pool, *Peak: Secrets from the New Science of Expertise* (Boston: Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2016).

Oswald, "Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: A meta-analysis," *Psychological Science* 25, no. 8 (2014): 1608–1618. For an English translation, see John S. Major, Sarah A. Queen, Andrew Seth Meyer and Harold D. Roth, trans., *The Huainanzi* (New York: Columbia University Press, 2010).

## Chapter 8: If the future is fixed, why try? {.unnumbered}

The lazy argument and Chrysippus's reply are in Cicero, *De Fato* (*On Fate*), sections 28–30, where the example is illness and the calling of a doctor; the exam version in this chapter applies the same argument. For a scholarly account, see Susanne Bobzien, *Determinism and Freedom in Stoic Philosophy* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1998), chapter 5.

On the distinctions between fatalism, determinism and compatibilism, see the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entries "Fatalism," "Causal Determinism" and "Compatibilism."

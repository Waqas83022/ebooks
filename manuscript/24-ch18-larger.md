# Chapter 18: A life larger than the target

A palliative care nurse in Australia named Bronnie Ware spent several years looking after people in the last weeks of their lives. She sat with them, listened to them and eventually wrote down what they told her about their regrets, first in a blog post and then in a book, *The Top Five Regrets of the Dying*.

Two of the regrets she heard most often have stayed with me through the writing of this book, because at first they seem to pull in opposite directions.

The first was a wish to have had the courage to live a life true to oneself, rather than the life others expected. People regretted the dreams they had never even tried for: the roads not taken because they were afraid, because someone disapproved, because the odds looked too long.

The second was a wish not to have worked so hard. People regretted years spent chasing results at the expense of everything else: family, friends, health, joy. Ware wrote that she heard this from every male patient she nursed.

So the dying regretted not trying hard enough for the things that mattered. And they regretted trying too hard for things that, in the end, mattered less than they had thought.

At first this sounds like a contradiction. I have come to believe it is the whole answer.

* * *

Research on regret points the same way. In the 1990s, the psychologists Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec studied what people regret over different spans of time. In the short term, they found, people tend to regret their actions more: the thing they said, the risk that did not pay off, the decision that went badly. But over the long term, the pattern tends to reverse. Looking back across years and decades, people more often regret what they did *not* do: the chance not taken, the person not approached, the dream never attempted.

Later researchers have argued about how strong and universal this pattern is, as they should. But its core has held up well enough to deserve a place in any honest answer to the question this book began with: why try at all, if trying guarantees nothing?

One answer is this. The arrow you release may miss, and you will grieve it and recover. The arrow you never release does not miss. It simply follows you, for the rest of your life, as a question.

* * *

The second regret warns us about the opposite danger. We can try for one thing so completely that we starve everything else. We can make a single target the measure of our whole life, so that when we hit it we look around and find the rest of our life has gone silent, and when we miss it there is nothing left standing.

Viktor Frankl believed that human beings find meaning in three broad ways. We find it in what we create or do: our work, our deeds, what we give the world. We find it in what we experience and whom we love: beauty, truth, another human being. And we find it in the stance we take toward suffering we cannot avoid.

Only the first of these depends much on outcomes, and even there, much of the meaning lives in the doing rather than the result. Love does not depend on winning. The stance we take toward suffering does not depend on succeeding. A life that rests on all three is like a table with three legs. If one leg is shortened by a lost job or a failed dream, the table tips, but it does not fall.

A life that rests on one outcome is a table with one leg.

Give the goal your best. Do not give it your worth.

* * *

I promised to tell you how the stories ended. They have not really ended, of course; nobody's story ends in the last chapter of someone else's book. But here is where I last left them.

Sara took the job at the smaller firm. It paid a little less than the famous company would have. Within six months she was handling clients on her own. Within two years she was leading a small team, something she might have waited five or six years to do in the glass building. She is good at her work and respected for it, and she has been able to help her family the way she hoped.

Sometimes she still passes the glass building on the bus. When she does, there is a small sting first, and then, usually, something else: a surprised kind of gratitude for the road she ended up on. She does not know whether the other road would have been better. It might have been. She has stopped needing to know. *Who knows*, said the old man at the frontier, and she has learned to say it too.

Daniel, who got the job she wanted, did well there. For the first few months it felt like the best thing that had ever happened to him. By the end of the first year it felt like a job: some days wonderful, some days dull, with a manager who was not always fair. In his second year he was passed over for a promotion he had worked hard for and believed he deserved. He did not know why. He went home that night, sat in his kitchen and read the email twice, as if the words might rearrange themselves.

I tell you this not because I want Daniel to suffer, but because I want you to see that winning did not excuse him from being human. The arrow that hits does not end the shooting. There is always another target, another wind, another morning. The winners and losers of any one contest turn out to be the same people, at different moments in the same long life.

Omar found work after eight months, a reasonable job at a reasonable company. He still does not know whether declining that first offer was right. His decision journal tells him his reasoning was careful and his concerns were real. The result tells him it cost him eight hard months. He has made a kind of peace with holding both. Some questions do not get answered. They get carried, and over time they grow lighter.

Nadia, the student choosing between her notes and her phone, chose the notes most evenings and her phone on some. She passed two of her three papers and failed the third by a narrow margin. She retook it the next term, after changing how she studied, and passed. She now tells younger students that the failed paper taught her more than the two she passed, and she is careful to add that she would still rather have passed it the first time.

Maya is still a nurse. She still feels a small ache when she sees a doctor's white coat. And she still goes to work every day to do exactly what she wanted to do with her life.

And Adam.

It has been three years since the café. Adam has not married. Some evenings, especially around the date he once planned to propose, he still thinks of Lina, and it still hurts. He has not looked at her page since the night he put the phone face down. The Saturday dinners with his old friend became a habit, and the habit became a circle of friends. He moved to a new city for work and found, to his surprise, that he loves training the young people who join his team. Recently, cautiously, he has begun getting to know someone. He does not know where it will lead. He is not calling it destiny. He is calling it a beginning.

His life is no longer arranged around an absence. That, I think, is what recovery from a loss like his really looks like. Not forgetting. Not replacing. Just a life that has grown large enough to hold the loss inside it, instead of a loss so large that it holds the life.

* * *

These are invented people, and I have given them gentle endings, gentler perhaps than real life always gives. But I have made the endings different from one another, because real endings are. One reached a goal by changing it. One reached a goal and found it was not the end of wanting. One is still carrying a question. One failed, changed her method and passed. One is living a different version of the same dream. One is still grieving and still living.

What they share is not an outcome. It is something more important. Each of them, in their own way, refused to let a single result become the title of their life. And each of them, at some point, took a breath and acted without knowing how it would end. That is what hope looks like from the outside. It rarely looks like confidence. It usually looks like someone picking up the bow again with shaking hands.

* * *

So let me bring the four words back to the table one last time, the four words that started all of this.

*Hard work* is real. It is the bow, the part that belongs to you. It changes the odds, builds skill, creates places for luck to land and gives you something to say yes with when opportunity arrives. It deserves your full effort, aimed well and paced so you can sustain it.

*Luck* is real. It is the wind, blowing since the day you were born and blowing still. It shapes the ground you start from and the openings that appear. It can be noticed, invited and prepared for, never commanded. It explains much of what success stories leave out, and much of why sincere people lose.

*Destiny*, whatever it is, is the page. If it is written, your effort is written on it too, part of the ink and not outside it. Whether or not it is written, you can only read it backward. It is a beautiful word for making peace with the past and a dangerous word for excusing yourself from the future.

*Hope* is the breath before the release, and it turns out to be the force that holds the other three together. Without it, nobody would draw a bow whose arrow they cannot steer. At its weakest, hope is a fantasy with a date attached. At its strongest, it is a direction with many roads: the decision to act toward something that matters, in a world that promises nothing, and to keep finding another way when one closes. For many people, it is also prayer. For everyone, it is the reason to try again.

They were never really at war. They only seemed to be, because each of us was trying to live on one of them alone.

* * *

The life I wish for you after all this is not a life in which every arrow hits. No such life exists, and I would be lying if I promised it.

It is a life in which you draw the bow with your whole heart, for things that are worth it. In which you know the wind when you feel it, and do not take it personally. In which you tell your story honestly, including the parts you did not choose. In which you hope in a way that can survive a missed target, with more than one road and no appointment with the universe. And in which, when an arrow misses, as some always will, you remain: whole, attentive, still yourself, still an archer.

A life can be unfinished in its ambitions and still be full in its meaning.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Give the goal your best. Do not give it your worth.*

**Practice.** Write three short paragraphs. In the first, write what you will pursue next and why it matters to you. In the second, write how you will treat yourself and the people around you while you pursue it. In the third, write what will still be worth caring about in your life if this attempt fails. Keep the third paragraph somewhere safe. You may need to read it one day.

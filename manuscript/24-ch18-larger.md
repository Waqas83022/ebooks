# Chapter 18: A life larger than the target

A palliative care nurse in Australia named Bronnie Ware spent several years looking after people in the last weeks of their lives. She sat with them, listened to them and, eventually, wrote down what they told her about their regrets. She published her observations first in a blog post and then in a book, *The Top Five Regrets of the Dying*.

Two of the regrets she heard most often have stayed with me while I wrote this book, because they seem at first to pull in opposite directions.

The first was this: *I wish I'd had the courage to live a life true to myself, not the life others expected of me.* People regretted the dreams they had never even tried for. The roads they had not taken because they were afraid, or because someone else disapproved, or because the odds seemed too long.

The second was this: *I wish I hadn't worked so hard.* People regretted the years spent chasing results at the expense of everything else: their families, their friends, their health, their joy. Ware wrote that she heard this from every male patient she nursed.

So the dying regretted not trying hard enough for the things that mattered. And they regretted trying too hard for things that, in the end, did not matter as much as they had thought.

At first this seems like a contradiction. I have come to believe it is the whole answer.

* * *

The research on regret points in the same direction. In the 1990s, the psychologists Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec studied what people regret over different spans of time. In the short term, they found, people tend to regret their actions more: the thing they said, the risk that did not pay off, the decision that turned out badly. But over the long term, the pattern tends to reverse. Looking back across years or decades, people more often regret what they did *not* do: the chance not taken, the person not approached, the dream not attempted.

Later researchers have argued about how strong and universal this pattern is, as they should. But the core of it has held up well enough that I think it deserves a place in any honest answer to the question this book began with: *why try at all, if trying guarantees nothing?*

One answer is simply this. The arrow you release may miss. But the arrow you never release is the one that will follow you for the rest of your life.

> *The arrow that misses is a loss. The arrow never released becomes a question you carry.*

* * *

And yet the second regret, *I wish I hadn't worked so hard*, warns us about the opposite danger. We can try for one thing so completely that we starve everything else. We can make one target the measure of our whole life, so that when we hit it, we look around and find the rest of our life has gone quiet, and when we miss it, we find there is nothing else left standing.

Viktor Frankl, whose words have appeared several times in these pages, believed that human beings can find meaning in three broad ways. We can find it in what we create or do: our work, our deeds, what we give to the world. We can find it in what we experience and whom we love: beauty, truth, another human being. And we can find it in the attitude we take toward suffering we cannot avoid.

Notice that only the first of these depends very much on outcomes, and even there, the meaning is in the doing as much as in the result. Love does not depend on winning. The attitude we take toward suffering does not depend on succeeding. A person whose life rests on all three is like a table with three legs. If one leg is cut short by a lost job or a failed dream, the table tips, but it does not fall.

A person whose whole life rests on one outcome is a table with one leg.

> *You can give a goal your best without giving it your entire worth.*

* * *

I promised you that I would tell you how the stories ended. They have not really ended, of course; nobody's story ends in the last chapter of someone else's book. But here is where I last left them.

Sana took the job at the smaller firm. It paid a little less than the famous company would have. Within six months, she was handling clients on her own. Within two years, she was leading a small project team, something she might have waited five or six years to do in the glass building. She is good at her work, and she is respected for it, and she has been able to help her family in the way she hoped.

Sometimes, she still passes the glass building on the bus. When she does, there is a small sting first, and then, usually, something else: a kind of surprised gratitude for the road she ended up on. She does not know whether the other road would have been better. It might have been. She has stopped needing to know. *Who knows?* says the old man at the frontier, and she has learned to say it too.

Daniel, who got the job she wanted, did well there. For the first few months, it felt like the best thing that had ever happened to him. By the end of the first year, it felt like a job: some days wonderful, some days tedious, with a manager who was not always fair. In his second year, he was passed over for a promotion he had worked hard for and believed he deserved. He did not know why. He went home that night, sat in his kitchen and read the email twice, as though the words might rearrange themselves.

I tell you this not because I want Daniel to suffer, but because I want you to see that winning did not exempt him from the human condition. The arrow that hits does not end the shooting. There is always another target, another wind, another morning. Winners and losers in any single contest turn out to be the same people, at different moments in the same long life.

Omar found work after eight months, a reasonable job at a reasonable company. He still does not know whether declining that first offer was the right decision. His decision journal tells him that his reasoning was careful and his concerns were real. The result tells him it cost him eight hard months. He has made a kind of peace with holding both. Some questions do not get answered. They get carried, and over time, they get lighter.

Leila is still a nurse. She still feels a small ache when she sees a doctor's white coat. And she still goes to work every day to do exactly what she wanted to do with her life.

And Yusuf.

It has been three years since the café. Yusuf has not married. Some evenings, especially around the date when he once planned to propose, he still thinks of Amna, and it still hurts. He no longer checks her profile. He moved to a new city for work, made friends there and found, to his surprise, that he loves teaching the young people who join his team. Recently, cautiously, he has begun getting to know someone. He does not know where it will lead. He is not calling it destiny. He is calling it a beginning.

His life is no longer arranged around an absence. That, I think, is the real recovery from a loss like his. Not forgetting. Not replacing. Just a life that has grown large enough to hold the loss inside it, instead of a loss so large that it holds the life.

* * *

These are invented people, and I have given them gentle endings, gentler perhaps than real life always gives. But I have tried to make the endings different from one another, because real endings are. One reached a goal by changing it. One reached a goal and found it was not the end of wanting. One is still carrying a question. One is living a different version of the same dream. One is still grieving and still living.

What they share is not an outcome. It is something more important. Each of them, in their own way, refused to let a single result become the title of their life.

> *The result belongs in your story. It does not have to become the title of your life.*

* * *

So let me bring the four words back to the table one last time, the four words that started all of this.

*Hard work* is real. It is the bow, the part that belongs to you. It changes the odds, builds skill, creates places for luck to land and gives you something to say yes with when opportunity arrives. It deserves your full effort, aimed well and paced so you can sustain it. It does not deserve your entire worth.

*Luck* is real. It is the wind, blowing since the day you were born and still blowing now. It shapes the ground you start from and the openings that appear. It can be noticed, invited and prepared for, but never commanded. It explains much of what other people's success stories leave out, and much of why sincere people lose. It is not a verdict on anyone.

*Destiny*, whatever it is, is the page. If it is written, your effort and your prayers are written on it too, part of the ink and not outside it. Whether or not it is written, you can only read it backward. It is a beautiful word for making peace with the past, and a dangerous word for excusing yourself from the future.

*Hope*, and for many of us *prayer*, is the breath before the release. It names what we want, marks the edge of our power, steadies us and sends us back to work. At its healthiest, it is not a prediction but a direction; not a date set with the universe, but a faith in the end of the story and an honest look at this chapter.

They were never really in conflict. They only seemed to be, because each of us was trying to live on one of them alone.

* * *

The life I wish for you, after all this, is not a life in which every arrow hits. No such life exists, and I would be lying if I promised it.

It is a life in which you draw the bow with your whole heart, for things that are worth it. In which you know the wind when you feel it, and do not take it personally. In which you tell your story honestly, including the parts you did not choose. In which you hope in a way that can survive a missed target, and pray, if you pray, with your hands still ready to work. And in which, when an arrow misses, as some always will, you remain: whole, attentive, still yourself, still an archer.

> *A life can remain unfinished in its ambitions and still be full in its meaning.*

**Practice.** Write three short paragraphs. In the first, write what you will pursue next and why it matters to you. In the second, write how you will treat yourself and the people around you while you pursue it. In the third, write what will still be worth caring about in your life if this attempt fails. Keep the third paragraph somewhere safe. You may need to read it one day.

# Chapter 3: The ground under the ladder

In January 1985, a Canadian psychologist named Roger Barnsley went to an ice hockey game in Lethbridge, Alberta, with his wife Paula. It was a junior league game, the Lethbridge Broncos, the kind of team where talented young players try to catch the eye of the professional scouts.

Paula was flipping through the printed program, which listed every player's date of birth. And she noticed something odd. An extraordinary number of the players had been born in January, February or March. Very few had been born late in the year.

Roger was curious enough to go and check. He and his colleagues gathered the birth dates of players across Canada's elite junior leagues and the National Hockey League. The pattern held. The closer a boy's birthday fell to the beginning of the year, the more likely he was to end up playing at the highest levels. The further it fell toward December, the less likely.

Nobody believes that stars or seasons make better hockey players. The explanation was much simpler, and much more troubling.

In Canadian youth hockey, children were grouped by calendar year, with a cut-off on the first of January. A boy born on January 2 and a boy born on December 30 of the same year played in the same age group. At nine years old, that difference is enormous: almost a full year of growth, coordination and confidence. The January boy is bigger, stronger and faster, so the coaches pick him for the better team. On the better team, he gets more skilled coaching, more games and more time on the ice. By twelve, he really is better. By sixteen, he is much better. The coaches were not wrong. They picked the best players they could see.

What they could not see was that a small, accidental head start, a birthday, had been fed and watered for a decade until it grew into a real difference in skill.

The research has been repeated many times since, in other sports and in schools, and the size of the effect varies a great deal from place to place. But the lesson of that hockey program has never left me. Effort was real for every one of those players. They all trained. They all got up at five in the morning for practice. And some of them were training on ground that had been quietly raised for them before they ever picked up a stick.

* * *

The wind does not wait for us to pick up the bow. It starts blowing the day we are born.

We do not choose our parents, our country, our first language or the century we arrive in. We do not choose whether we were born healthy, whether there were books in the house, whether our family could afford for us to work without pay for a year to get experience. We do not choose whether the people with power in our society look at our names, our faces or our accents with warmth or with suspicion.

In the early 2000s, two economists, Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan, ran an experiment that has become one of the most quoted in the study of work. They responded to real job advertisements in Boston and Chicago with thousands of invented résumés. Some of the résumés were given names that sounded white, such as Emily or Greg. Others were given names that sounded African American, such as Lakisha or Jamal. Otherwise, the résumés were randomly matched in quality.

The résumés with white-sounding names received about 50 percent more calls for interviews.

Nobody in that study was lazy. No one had failed to prepare. The candidates were, by design, equally qualified. And yet half of them were starting the race several steps behind the line, for a reason that had nothing to do with their effort and nothing to do with chance either. It had to do with the choices of the people reading the paper.

I want to be careful with this study. It was conducted in particular cities in a particular country at a particular time, and it should not be used to diagnose any individual rejection; most rejected applicants will never know why they were turned down. But it shows something we should all be willing to see. Sometimes the ground under the ladder is uneven because of what people do, not just because of what happens.

* * *

There is a name for the way small advantages grow into large ones. In 1968, the sociologist Robert Merton called it the *Matthew effect*, after a line in the Gospel of Matthew: *to everyone who has, more will be given.* Merton was studying scientists. He noticed that when a famous scientist and an unknown one made similar discoveries, the famous one tended to receive most of the credit, which made them more famous, which brought more funding and more students, which led to more discoveries, which brought more credit.

The Matthew effect is everywhere once you start looking. The child who reads a little earlier enjoys reading more, so she reads more, so she learns faster. The company that gets a few early customers gets reviews, which bring more customers. The job candidate with a famous name on his CV gets the interview that gets him the next famous name.

None of this means that the people who benefit did not work. Usually they worked very hard. The January hockey players trained as hard as anyone. The famous scientist earned her first success. The point is that their work was multiplied by something they did not create. Their effort was a seed, and they happened to plant it in good soil, in a sunny spot, in a wet year.

> *A fair account of achievement includes the ground the ladder stood on.*

* * *

Warren Buffett, one of the richest people of the last century, has spent decades repeating a phrase that I wish more successful people would use. He calls it the *ovarian lottery*.

He has pointed out that he was born in the United States, in 1930, as a white man, at a moment when his particular gift, an unusual talent for judging the value of businesses, happened to be one of the most richly rewarded skills on the planet. He has said he worked hard, and he did. But he has also said, again and again, that had he been born in another time or place, the same mind and the same effort would have counted for far less.

There is something deeply attractive about a person who can say this. Robert Frank, an economist who has written a whole book about success and luck, argues that people who acknowledge the role of luck in their success tend to be more generous, and are often liked more, than people who insist they did it all themselves. And it makes sense. Someone who sees their luck can see other people's bad luck too. Someone who believes they made themselves usually believes that everyone else made themselves as well, including the people who are struggling.

* * *

Now I need to warn you about the opposite mistake, because once you have seen how much the ground matters, it is easy to fall in from the other side.

If birthdays shape hockey careers, and names shape job interviews, and parents shape everything, then perhaps effort really is a joke. Perhaps everything is decided before we start. Perhaps people who succeed were simply handed it, and people who struggle were simply robbed, and nothing any of us does makes much difference.

This sounds tough-minded. It is actually just the old contract again, turned upside down. It assumes that if effort is not *everything*, it must be *nothing*.

Look again at the hockey players. Being born in January raised a boy's odds of reaching the top. It did not put him there. Most January boys never played professional hockey; most of them, like most of everyone, played for a few years and then grew up and did other things. And some December boys, smaller and slower at nine, kept training, kept loving the game, kept showing up, and made it anyway, often after being overlooked for years. They had to climb a taller ladder. Some of them climbed it.

The honest picture is this: circumstances set the starting height and the steepness of the climb. Effort still determines a great deal about how far up you go from wherever you start. Both are real. Pretending either one is zero is a way of lying to yourself.

> *Your starting point was luck. Your direction is not only luck.*

* * *

There is one more distinction I think is important, and it is often blurred.

Some of the wind really is luck: the weather, an illness nobody could predict, a recession that arrived the year you graduated, the fact that the one person who could have helped you happened to move to another city. Nobody did this to you. It just happened.

But some of what we call "bad luck" is not luck at all. It is a barrier that was put there and kept there by people. A hiring manager who does not want a woman on the team. A rule that excludes people from a certain background. A family that will not let a daughter study. An institution that rewards connections over competence.

When we call these things luck, we do two kinds of damage. We hide the responsibility of the people who could change them. And we tell the person facing them that there is nothing to be done but endure, when sometimes there is a great deal that can be done: by organizing, by speaking up, by finding allies, by building something new, by changing the rules for the next person even if they cannot be changed in time for you.

Your effort does not only belong to your own climb. Some of it can go into fixing the ladder.

> *Advice about climbing means little when it ignores who can reach the ladder.*

* * *

Let me go back to Sana for a moment.

One of the details she did not mention, even to herself, is that Daniel spent the previous summer in an unpaid internship at a company that worked closely with the one they both applied to. Sana could not do an unpaid internship. Her family needed her salary, and she spent that summer working at an accounting firm that had nothing glamorous about it at all.

Does that mean Daniel did not deserve the job? No. He worked hard during that internship, and he learned real things there. Does it mean Sana's loss was entirely about money? No. She will never know exactly why the decision went the way it did, and it would be another kind of story to decide that she does.

But it does mean that when Sana's inner voice writes its verdict, *you just were not good enough*, it is leaving something out. The two of them were not standing on the same ground. A fair account has to include that. Not as an excuse, and not as a grievance to carry forever, but as part of the truth.

* * *

What can a person actually do with all this?

I think it starts with an honest map. Not a map of how you wish things were, and not a map of how the motivational posters say things are, but a map of your real situation.

What do you have? Skills, people who care about you, health, time, savings, a language, a network, a place to live, a faith or a philosophy that holds you up. These are resources. Some of them you earned. Some were given. All of them count.

What is in your way? Some obstacles are within your power to change. Some can be negotiated. Some belong to the wind and can only be waited out or worked around. Some are barriers held in place by others, which may need more than one person to move.

Where can you get help? This is the part that proud, hardworking people skip most often. The myth of the self-made person makes asking for help feel like cheating. It is not. Every person who climbed anything was helped, whether or not they noticed.

When you make this map, two things happen. First, the shame lifts a little. You can see that your struggle is not simply a measure of your character. Second, and this surprises people, the map usually shows more room to act than you expected. Not unlimited room. But room.

That is where the archer stands: not on perfect ground, but on real ground, with a real bow, in a real wind. Knowing exactly where she stands is the first part of aiming well.

# Chapter 3: The ground under the ladder

In January 1985, a Canadian psychologist named Roger Barnsley went to an ice hockey game in Lethbridge, Alberta, with his wife Paula. It was a junior league game, the Lethbridge Broncos, the kind of team where talented teenagers try to catch the eye of professional scouts.

Paula was flipping through the printed program, which listed every player's date of birth. She noticed something odd. An extraordinary number of the players had been born in January, February or March. Very few had been born late in the year.

Roger was curious enough to check. He and his colleagues gathered birth dates for players across Canada's elite junior leagues and the National Hockey League. The pattern held. The closer a boy's birthday fell to the start of the year, the more likely he was to reach the top levels of the sport.

Nobody believes that the month of your birth makes you better at hockey. The explanation was simpler, and more troubling.

Canadian youth hockey grouped children by calendar year, with a cut-off on the first of January. A boy born on January 2 and a boy born on December 30 of the same year played in the same age group. At nine years old, that is almost a full year of growth, coordination and confidence. The January boy is bigger and faster, so the coaches pick him for the better team. On the better team he gets better coaching, more games and more time on the ice. By twelve he really is better. By sixteen he is much better. The coaches were not wrong. They picked the best players they could see.

What they could not see was that a small, accidental head start had been fed and watered for a decade until it grew into a real difference in skill.

The research has been repeated many times since, in other sports and in schools, and the size of the effect varies a great deal from place to place. But the lesson of that hockey program has never left me. Every one of those players trained hard. They all got up in the dark for early practice. And some of them were training on ground that had been raised for them, without anyone noticing, before they ever picked up a stick.

* * *

The wind does not wait for us to pick up the bow. It starts blowing on the day we are born.

We do not choose our parents, our country, our first language or the century we arrive in. We do not choose whether we were born healthy, whether there were books in the house, whether our family could afford for us to work for free for a year to gain experience. We do not choose whether the people with power in our society look at our names, faces or accents with warmth or with suspicion.

In the early 2000s, two economists, Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan, ran an experiment that is still quoted wherever people study hiring. They answered real job advertisements in Boston and Chicago with thousands of invented résumés. Some résumés carried names that sounded white, such as Emily or Greg. Others carried names that sounded African American, such as Lakisha or Jamal. In every other respect, the names were assigned at random to résumés of matched quality.

The résumés with white-sounding names received about 50 percent more calls for interviews.

No one in that experiment was lazy. No one had failed to prepare. By design, the candidates were equally qualified on paper. Yet half of them started the race several steps behind the line, for a reason that had nothing to do with their effort and nothing to do with chance either. It had to do with the choices of the people reading the paper.

I want to handle this study carefully. It was carried out in particular cities, in one country, at one point in time, and it cannot diagnose any single rejection; most rejected applicants never learn why. But it shows something we should all be willing to see. Sometimes the ground under the ladder is uneven because of what people do, not just because of what happens.

* * *

There is a name for the way small advantages grow into large ones. Sociologists call it *cumulative advantage*. The sociologist Robert Merton first described it in 1968 while studying scientists. When a famous scientist and an unknown one made similar discoveries, the famous one tended to receive most of the credit. That credit brought more fame, which brought more funding and better students, which led to more discoveries, which brought more credit. An early lead did not just persist. It compounded.

Once you see cumulative advantage, you see it everywhere. The child who reads a little earlier enjoys reading more, so she reads more, so she learns faster. The restaurant that gets a few good early reviews gets more customers, which brings more reviews. The applicant with one famous name on her CV gets the interview that gets her the next famous name.

A team of economists led by Alex Bell and Raj Chetty found a striking version of this in the United States. They linked the records of over a million inventors to their family backgrounds. Children born into the richest 1 percent of families were about ten times as likely to grow up to become inventors as children from the bottom half. And the gap was not simply about ability. Among children who had scored at the very top of their class in maths at age eight or nine, those from wealthy families were still far more likely to become inventors than equally gifted children from poorer families. The researchers called the missing inventors "lost Einsteins": people who had the talent but never met the opportunity.

None of this means that the people who benefit did not work. Usually they worked very hard. The January hockey players trained as hard as anyone. The famous scientist earned her first success. The point is that their work was multiplied by something they did not create. Their effort was a seed, and they happened to plant it in good soil, in a sunny spot, in a wet year.

A fair account of any achievement includes the ground the ladder stood on.

* * *

Warren Buffett, one of the richest people of the last century, has spent decades repeating a phrase I wish more successful people would use. He calls it the *ovarian lottery*. He has pointed out, again and again, that he was born in the United States, in 1930, as a white man, at the very moment when his particular gift, an unusual talent for judging the value of businesses, happened to be one of the most richly rewarded skills on earth. He worked hard, and he says so. He also says that the same mind and the same effort, born into another time or place, would have counted for far less.

There is something deeply attractive about a person who can say that. The economist Robert Frank, who wrote a whole book about success and luck, argues that people who acknowledge the role of luck in their lives tend to be more generous, and are often better liked, than people who insist they did it all themselves. It makes sense. Someone who sees their own luck can see other people's bad luck too. Someone who believes they made themselves usually believes that everyone else made themselves as well, including the people who are struggling.

* * *

Now I need to warn you about the opposite mistake, because once you have seen how much the ground matters, it is easy to fall off the other side.

If birthdays shape hockey careers, and names shape interviews, and parents' income shapes who becomes an inventor, then perhaps effort really is a joke. Perhaps everything is decided before we start. Perhaps the people who succeed were simply handed it, the people who struggle were simply robbed, and nothing any of us does makes much difference.

This sounds tough-minded. It is really just the old contract turned upside down. It assumes that if effort is not *everything*, it must be *nothing*.

Look again at the hockey players. Being born in January raised a boy's odds of reaching the top. It did not put him there. Most January boys never played professional hockey; most of them, like most of everyone, played for a few years and then grew up and did other things. And some December boys, smaller and slower at nine, kept training, kept loving the game, kept showing up, and made it anyway, often after years of being overlooked. They had to climb a taller ladder. Some of them climbed it.

The honest picture is this. Circumstances set your starting height and the steepness of the climb. Effort still decides a great deal about how far you rise from wherever you begin. Your starting point was luck. Your direction is not only luck.

* * *

There is one more distinction that matters, and it is often blurred.

Some of the wind really is luck: an illness nobody could predict, a recession that arrives the year you graduate, a flood that takes the harvest, the one person who could have helped you moving to another city. Nobody did this to you. It simply happened.

But some of what we call bad luck is not luck at all. It is a barrier that was put there, and is kept there, by people. A manager who will not hire a woman. A rule that shuts out people from a certain background. A family that will not let a daughter study. A system that rewards connections over competence.

When we call these things luck, we do two kinds of damage. We hide the responsibility of the people who could change them. And we tell the person facing them that there is nothing to do but endure, when sometimes a great deal can be done: by organizing, by speaking up, by finding allies, by building something new, by changing the rules for the next person even if they cannot be changed in time for you.

Not everything called bad luck is luck. Some of it has an author. And your effort does not have to belong only to your own climb. Some of it can go into repairing the ladder.

* * *

Let me go back to Sara for a moment.

There is a detail she did not mention, even to herself. The previous summer, Daniel had done an unpaid internship at a firm that worked closely with the company they both applied to. Sara could not have done an unpaid internship. Her family needed her salary, and she spent that summer at an accounting office with nothing glamorous about it at all.

Does that mean Daniel did not deserve the job? No. He worked hard during that internship and learned real things there. Does it mean Sara lost only because of money? No. She will never know exactly why the decision went the way it did, and deciding that she does would be another kind of story.

But it does mean that when Sara's inner voice writes its verdict, *you just were not good enough*, it is leaving something out. The two of them were not standing on the same ground. A fair account has to include that. Not as an excuse, and not as a grievance to carry forever, but as part of the truth.

* * *

What can a person actually do with all this?

It starts with an honest map. Not a map of how you wish things were, and not a map of how motivational posters say things are, but a map of your real situation.

What do you have? Skills, people who care about you, health, time, savings, a language, a network, a roof, a faith or philosophy that holds you up. Some of it you earned. Some was given. All of it counts.

What is in your way? Some obstacles you can change yourself. Some can be negotiated. Some belong to the wind and can only be waited out or worked around. Some are barriers held in place by others and may need more than one person to move.

Where can you get help? Proud, hardworking people skip this question more than any other. The myth of the self-made person makes asking for help feel like cheating. It is not. Everyone who ever climbed anything was helped, whether or not they noticed.

When you draw this map, two things happen. First, some of the shame lifts. You can see that your struggle is not simply a measure of your character. Second, and this surprises people, the map usually shows more room to act than you expected. Never unlimited room. But room.

That is where the archer stands: not on perfect ground, but on real ground, with a real bow, in a real wind. Knowing exactly where she stands is the first part of aiming well.

# Chapter 6: The old man at the frontier

There is a story Chinese parents have been telling their children for more than two thousand years. Its oldest written version is in a book called the *Huainanzi*, compiled at the court of a Han dynasty prince in the second century before the common era. I will tell it in my own words, keeping close to the old version.

An old man lived near the northern frontier, where China met the lands of the nomadic horsemen. One day his horse ran away across the border. His neighbors came to console him. What terrible luck, they said.

The old man shrugged. Who knows, he said, whether this might turn out to be a blessing?

Some months later the horse came back, and it was not alone. It had brought with it a magnificent horse from the nomads' herds. The neighbors came to congratulate him. What wonderful luck!

Who knows, said the old man, whether this might turn out to be a misfortune?

His son loved to ride, and he took the new horse out often. One day he was thrown and badly broke his leg. He would walk with a limp for the rest of his life. The neighbors came again. What terrible luck.

Who knows, said the old man, whether this might turn out to be a blessing?

A year later the nomads invaded in force. Every able-bodied young man in the region was called up to fight, and nine out of ten of them died in the war. Because of his leg, the old man's son was not taken. Father and son both survived.

The story ends there. It does not tell us what happened next, and that is part of its point. There is always a next.

In Chinese, the story survives as a short saying people still use when something bad happens: the old man at the frontier lost his horse, and who could know it was not a blessing? In modern English retellings, the old man's reply is often shortened to a single word. *Maybe.*

* * *

I want to be very clear about what this story does *not* mean, because it is often misused.

It does not mean that every misfortune is secretly good fortune. The son's broken leg was not good. It hurt, and it changed his life. If you had asked him, I suspect he would have chosen two good legs and taken his chances with the war. And the story says nothing about the families of the nine young men in ten who did not come home. For them, there was no happy turn at all.

The old man never said the runaway horse was good. He never called the broken leg a gift. He said *who knows?* That is a completely different statement. He was not putting a cheerful label on events. He was refusing to put a final label on them at all.

The old man did not say it was good. He said it was not finished.

This is the first great lesson the story carries, and I think it is one of the most useful things a human being can learn. We call things luck too early. We label an event as good or bad fortune in the moment it happens, when we can see only a small part of what it will lead to. Then we feel the full weight of the label as though it were final. But events keep unfolding. The meaning of almost everything that happens to us is still being written for years afterward.

* * *

The psychologist Daniel Kahneman, who won a Nobel Prize for his work on how people make judgments, described a moment that changed his own thinking. It was not a parable. It happened while he was teaching flight instructors in the Israeli Air Force.

He was explaining a principle from the psychology of learning: rewarding good performance works better than punishing mistakes. One instructor objected. In his experience, he said, it was the other way round. When he praised a cadet for an excellent maneuver, the next attempt was usually worse. When he shouted at a cadet for a terrible one, the next attempt was usually better. So, he concluded, praise makes people worse and criticism makes them better.

The instructor was describing something real. He was simply wrong about the cause.

Any single performance, Kahneman realized, is a mixture of skill and luck. A cadet's skill does not change much from one landing to the next. Luck does: a gust of wind, a moment of distraction, a lucky correction. An exceptionally good landing usually means skill was helped, that time, by some good luck. Next time the luck will probably be more ordinary, so the result will probably be less impressive, whatever the instructor says. An exceptionally bad landing usually means skill was hurt by bad luck, so the next one will probably be better, again whatever the instructor says.

Statisticians call this *regression to the mean*. Extreme results tend to be followed by less extreme ones, because the luck that made them extreme does not usually repeat. The instructor had been watching the wind and believing he was watching the power of his own voice.

Kahneman later summed up the role of luck in a pair of short formulas. Success, he wrote, is talent plus luck. Great success is a little more talent and a lot more luck.

* * *

Regression to the mean is not just a curiosity for statisticians. It shapes, without our noticing, how we judge ourselves and other people.

You have an unusually brilliant week at work and start to believe that is who you really are. Then the next week is ordinary, and it feels like decline. You do terribly in an exam and decide you are hopeless at the subject. Then the next exam goes better, and you credit your new study method, when part of the improvement was simply the bad luck of the first day not repeating. A shop has a record month and the owner hires two new staff; the next month is average and he panics. A striker scores in five matches in a row and is called a genius; then the goals stop and he is called finished.

Sports fans even have a name for one version of this: the magazine cover jinx. An athlete has an extraordinary season and lands on the cover of a famous sports magazine. The next season is less spectacular. Is the magazine cursed? No. To make the cover, an athlete usually needs an exceptional season, and exceptional seasons usually include some good luck. The next season, the luck evens out.

This is humbling and freeing at the same time. Your best moment was probably not the full measure of your ability, and your worst moment probably was not either. A single result contains wind. A pattern over time contains you. Judge yourself by the pattern, not by the most dramatic point in it.

* * *

There is a second lesson in the frontier story, and it concerns how much the "big" events of our lives actually change us.

In 1978, a team of psychologists led by Philip Brickman did a small and unusual study. They interviewed people who had recently won large prizes in the Illinois state lottery. They also interviewed people who had been paralyzed in serious accidents, and a comparison group of people who had experienced neither.

The study was small, and later research has debated and refined it. But its findings were startling at the time. Once the excitement settled, the lottery winners were not much happier than the comparison group. They even reported taking *less* pleasure in everyday things: chatting with a friend, hearing a joke, eating breakfast. Winning had raised the bar for what felt good. The accident victims were, on average, less happy than the comparison group. But they were not nearly as unhappy as most people would predict. Their ratings of their present happiness still sat above the middle of the scale, far from the despair that outsiders imagined for them.

We will come back to this idea later, because it matters enormously to anyone who has just lost something they wanted. For now, notice what it says about luck. The events we label as life-changing fortune often change us less, and more briefly, than we expect. The events we label as life-ruining misfortune also often change us less, and more briefly, than we fear. Human beings adapt. We are more resilient, and harder to satisfy, than our labels assume.

The old man at the frontier seems to have known this. He never climbed too high or fell too low. Not because he did not care about his horse or his son, but because he knew the story was not over and that he would still be himself at the end of it.

* * *

So what do we do with this?

I am not suggesting that you meet every event with a shrug. That would be strange, and probably impossible. When something good happens, celebrate. When something bad happens, you are allowed to grieve. Feelings are not mistakes.

What I am suggesting is a small change in the words you use, especially the words you use about yourself.

When bad news arrives, the mind rushes to write a final label. *This is a disaster. My life is ruined. It's over. I'm unlucky. This always happens to me.* These are verdicts, and verdicts are written as if the story has ended.

Try putting two words in front of them: *so far*.

*So far, this is very bad.* That sentence is completely honest. It does not pretend you are fine. But it leaves a door open for what comes next. It is the old man's *who knows* in a form that does not require you to be calm.

When good news arrives, the same two words protect you from the opposite mistake. *So far, this is wonderful.* Celebrate it fully. Just do not build your whole house on it, because the horse that arrived today may throw your son tomorrow.

* * *

Months after her rejection, Sara would take a job at a smaller company, where she would be given far more responsibility, far sooner, than the famous company would ever have given her. We will come back to her story. I mention it here not to tie a bow on her loss, because some days it still stings. I mention it because on the evening she read that email in her kitchen, she was completely certain she knew what it meant.

She did not know. She could not have known. Nobody can, on the evening it happens.

That is what the old man at the frontier understood. Not that every loss becomes a gain; it does not. But that a single event, seen from inside the moment, is one page torn from the middle of a long book. You can feel what is on the page. You cannot yet read the chapter.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*The old man did not say it was good. He said it was not finished.*

**Practice.** Think of an event from at least five years ago that you labeled, at the time, as very good or very bad luck. Write down what you believed it meant on the day it happened. Then write down what it actually led to, good and bad. Notice the distance between the two accounts. Finally, take one thing troubling you now and write about it in a sentence that begins with "So far."

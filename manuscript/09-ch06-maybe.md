# Chapter 6: The old man at the frontier

There is a story that Chinese parents have been telling their children for more than two thousand years. Its oldest written version is in a book called the *Huainanzi*, compiled at the court of a Han dynasty prince in the second century before the common era. I will tell it the way it was told to me, keeping close to the old version.

An old man lived near the northern frontier, where China met the lands of the nomadic horsemen. One day his horse ran away across the border. His neighbors came to console him. *What terrible luck,* they said.

The old man shrugged. *Who knows whether this might turn out to be a blessing?*

Some months later, the horse came back. And it was not alone. It had brought with it a magnificent horse from the nomads' herds. The neighbors came to congratulate him. *What wonderful luck!*

*Who knows,* said the old man, *whether this might turn out to be a misfortune?*

His son loved to ride, and he took the new horse out often. One day he was thrown and badly broke his leg. He would walk with a limp for the rest of his life. The neighbors came again. *What terrible luck.*

*Who knows,* said the old man, *whether this might turn out to be a blessing?*

A year later, the nomads invaded in force. Every able-bodied young man in the region was called up to fight. Nine out of ten of them died in the war. The old man's son, because of his leg, was not taken. He and his father both survived.

The story ends there. It does not tell us what happened next, and that is part of its point. There is always a next.

In Chinese, the story is remembered in a short phrase that people still use when something bad happens: *the old man at the frontier lost his horse; how could anyone know it was not a blessing?* In modern retellings in English, the old man's answer is often shortened to a single word: *Maybe.*

* * *

I want to say very clearly what this story does *not* mean, because it is often misused.

It does not mean that every misfortune is secretly good fortune. The son's broken leg was not good. It hurt. It changed his life. If you had asked him, I suspect he would have chosen two good legs and taken his chances with the war. And we know nothing about the families of the nine young men out of ten who did not come back; for them, the story has no happy turn at all.

The old man never said the horse running away was good. He never said the broken leg was a gift. He said *who knows?* That is a very different statement. He was not putting a cheerful label on events. He was refusing to put a final label on them at all.

> *The old man did not say it was good. He said it was not finished.*

This is the first great lesson about luck that the story carries, and I think it is one of the most useful things a human being can learn: we call things luck too early.

We label an event as good or bad luck in the moment it happens, when we can see only a small part of what it will lead to. Then we feel the full emotional weight of that label, as though it were final. But events keep unfolding. The meaning of almost everything that happens to us is still being written for years afterward.

* * *

The psychologist Daniel Kahneman, who won a Nobel Prize for his work on how people make judgments, told a story about a moment that changed his own thinking. It was not a parable. It happened while he was teaching flight instructors in the Israeli Air Force.

He was explaining to them a principle from the psychology of learning: that rewarding good performance works better than punishing mistakes. One of the instructors objected. In his experience, he said, it was the other way around. When he praised a cadet for an excellent maneuver, the next attempt was usually worse. When he shouted at a cadet for a terrible one, the next attempt was usually better. So, he concluded, praise makes people worse and criticism makes them better.

The instructor was describing something real. He was just wrong about the cause.

Any single performance, Kahneman realized, is a mixture of skill and luck. A cadet's skill does not change much from one landing to the next. But luck does: a gust of wind, a moment of distraction, a lucky correction. An exceptionally good landing usually means the cadet's skill was helped, that time, by some good luck. The next landing, the luck will probably be more ordinary, so the result will probably be less impressive, whatever the instructor says. An exceptionally bad landing usually means skill was hurt by some bad luck, so the next one will probably be better, again whatever the instructor says.

Statisticians call this *regression to the mean*. Extreme results tend to be followed by less extreme ones, simply because the luck that helped make them extreme does not usually repeat. The instructor had been watching the wind and thinking he was watching the effect of his own voice.

Kahneman later summarized the role of luck in two short equations that I have never forgotten:

*Success = talent + luck.*

*Great success = a little more talent + a lot of luck.*

* * *

Regression to the mean is not just a curiosity for statisticians. It quietly shapes how we judge ourselves and other people.

You have an unusually brilliant day at work, and you start to believe that is who you really are. Then the next day is ordinary, and it feels like decline. You have a terrible exam, and you conclude you are hopeless at the subject. Then the next exam is better, and you credit the new study method, when some of the improvement was simply the bad luck of the first day not repeating.

Sports fans have a name for one version of this: the magazine cover jinx. An athlete has an extraordinary season and appears on the cover of a famous sports magazine. The next season, they are less spectacular. Is the magazine cursed? No. To make the cover, an athlete usually needs an exceptional season, which usually includes some good luck. The next season, the luck evens out.

What this means for you is something both humbling and freeing. Your best moment was probably not the full measure of your ability, and your worst moment was probably not either. A single result, good or bad, contains wind. Judge yourself by the pattern over time, not by the most dramatic point in it.

> *A single result contains wind. A pattern contains you.*

* * *

There is a second lesson in the frontier story, and it has to do with how much the "big" events of our lives actually change us.

In 1978, a team of psychologists led by Philip Brickman did a small and unusual study. They interviewed people who had recently won large prizes in the Illinois state lottery. They also interviewed people who had been paralyzed in serious accidents, and a comparison group of ordinary people who had experienced neither.

The study was small, and it has been debated and partly revised by later research. But its findings were startling at the time. The lottery winners, after the excitement settled, were not much happier than the ordinary people. In fact, they reported taking *less* pleasure in everyday activities: chatting with a friend, hearing a joke, eating breakfast. The winning had raised the bar for what felt good. The accident victims were, on average, less happy than the comparison group. But they were not nearly as unhappy as most people would predict. On the study's scale, their ratings of their present happiness still sat above the midpoint, far from the despair that outsiders imagined for them.

We will come back to this idea in later chapters, because it matters enormously to anyone who has just lost something they wanted. But for now, notice what it says about luck. The events we label as life-changing good fortune often change us less, and more briefly, than we expect. The events we label as life-ruining misfortune also often change us less, and more briefly, than we fear. Human beings adapt. We are far more resilient, and far less easy to satisfy, than our labels assume.

The old man at the frontier seems to have known this. He never got too high or too low. Not because he did not care about his horse or his son, but because he knew that the story was not over and that he would still be himself at the end of it.

* * *

So what do we do with this?

I am not suggesting that you meet every event in your life with a shrug. That would be strange, and probably impossible. When something good happens, you should celebrate. When something bad happens, you are allowed to grieve. Feelings are not mistakes.

What I am suggesting is a small change in the words you use, especially the words you use to yourself.

When the bad news arrives, the mind rushes to write a final label. *This is a disaster. My life is ruined. It's over. I'm unlucky. This always happens to me.* These are verdicts, and verdicts are written as if the story has ended.

Try adding two words to the front of them: *so far.*

*So far, this is very bad.* That sentence is completely honest. It does not pretend you are fine. But it leaves a door open for what comes next. It is the old man's *who knows?* in a form that does not require you to be serene.

When good news arrives, the same practice protects you from the opposite mistake. *So far, this is wonderful.* Celebrate it fully. Just do not build your whole house on it, because the horse that arrived today may throw your son tomorrow.

> *We call it luck too early. Add two words: so far.*

* * *

Sana, months after her rejection, would get a job at a smaller company, where she would be given far more responsibility, far earlier, than she would ever have been given at the famous one. We will come back to her story. I mention it here not to tie a bow on her loss, because she still sometimes thinks about the glass building, and some days it still stings. I mention it because on the evening she read that email in her kitchen, she was completely certain she knew what it meant.

She did not know. She could not have known. Nobody can, on the evening it happens.

That is what the old man at the frontier understood. Not that every loss becomes a gain; it does not. But that a single event, seen from inside the moment, is a page torn from the middle of a long book. You can feel what is on the page. You cannot yet read the chapter.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*The old man did not say it was good. He said it was not finished.*

*A single result contains wind. A pattern contains you.*

*We call it luck too early.*

**Practice.** Think of an event from five or more years ago that you labeled, at the time, as very good or very bad luck. Write down what you believed it meant on the day it happened. Then write down what it actually led to, the good and the bad. Notice how different the two accounts are. Then take one thing that is troubling you now, and write about it in a sentence that begins with "So far."

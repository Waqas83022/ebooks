# Chapter 2: Sweat is not a strategy

There is an old story about two woodcutters. It turns up in many countries and many languages, which is usually a sign that a story is true in the way that matters.

The two men agree to a contest: whoever cuts more wood by sunset wins. The first woodcutter is famous for his strength. He starts at dawn and never stops. Every time he glances across the clearing, he sees the second man sitting down, and every time he sees it, he swings harder. By midday his shoulders are burning. By afternoon his hands are bleeding. He keeps going, because that is what champions do.

At sunset they count. The second man has cut nearly twice as much.

"I don't understand," says the first woodcutter. "Every hour I looked over, and every hour you were resting."

"I wasn't resting," says the second. "I was sharpening my axe."

The story is often dressed up with a famous name. You may have seen a line credited to Abraham Lincoln about spending most of your time on a tree sharpening the axe first. Nobody has ever found evidence that he said it. Good ideas often borrow celebrities so they can travel further.

I start here because before we can talk honestly about luck or destiny, we have to be honest about effort. And the most important honest thing to say about effort is this: *hard work* is two different things wearing one coat.

* * *

The first thing is strain. Hours at the desk. Early alarms. Being tired. Sacrifice. When people say "I worked so hard," this is usually what they mean, and they are telling the truth.

The second thing is aim. Effort that targets the specific skill that decides the result, and that changes in response to feedback. This kind of work is quieter and less dramatic. It sometimes involves stopping to think, which from the outside can look exactly like resting. It is the sharpening of the axe.

The two often travel together, but they are not the same thing. When they come apart, a person can pour years of strain into a goal and get very little aim in return.

The psychologist Anders Ericsson spent his career studying how people become excellent. In a well-known study published in 1993, he and his colleagues looked at violin students at a music academy in Berlin. The best students had not simply played more. They had spent more time in what Ericsson called *deliberate practice*: working alone, on the hardest passages, at the edge of their ability, with a clear goal and immediate feedback. Playing a piece you already know from beginning to end feels like practice. Playing the same four difficult bars forty times, slowly, until the mistake disappears, *is* practice.

Ericsson's work was later squeezed into a popular slogan, the "ten thousand hour rule," which promised that anyone could master anything with enough hours. Ericsson himself spent years objecting to it. The number of hours was never the point. The kind of hours was the point.

* * *

It is also worth knowing how far practice goes, because the honest answer is more interesting than the slogan.

In 2014, the psychologists Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald pulled together the results of 88 studies that measured both how much people practiced and how well they performed. They wanted to know how much of the difference between performers could be explained by the difference in their practice.

The answer depended enormously on the field. In games like chess, practice accounted for about 26 percent of the differences between people. In music, about 21 percent. In sports, about 18 percent. In education, about 4 percent. In professions, less than 1 percent.

These numbers are easy to misread, so let me handle them carefully. They do not mean that practice is responsible for 18 percent of an athlete's success. They describe how much of the *spread* between people tracks the spread in their practice, across the people in those studies. And the remaining spread is not simply luck. It includes the age people started, how quickly they learn, what their bodies or minds were already suited to, the quality of their coaching and a hundred other things nobody measured.

But the broad message is clear. Practice matters, sometimes a great deal, and practice is not the whole story. In fields with fixed rules and fast feedback, like chess or tennis, it explains more. In the shifting, noisy worlds of school and work, where feedback is slow and the rules keep changing, it explains much less.

I find that strangely comforting. If you have worked hard and not yet reached the top of your field, there are many possible explanations, and "you are lazy" is only one of them, and usually not the most likely one. It also means that in most of real life, the *amount* of effort is a weaker lever than we think, and the *direction* of effort is a stronger one.

* * *

Here is an example I think about often.

Two students prepare for the same exam. The first reads her notes four times, slowly, with a highlighter. By the fourth reading everything looks familiar, and she feels ready. The second reads her notes once, then spends the rest of her time closing the book and testing herself: writing down everything she can remember, checking what she missed, trying again.

The second student's evening feels worse. It is frustrating to discover how much you have forgotten. The first student's evening feels better. Familiarity feels like knowledge.

In 2006, the psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke ran a version of this experiment. Students who repeatedly reread a passage felt more confident, and on a test a few minutes later they did fine. But a week later, the students who had tested themselves remembered considerably more. The rereaders had worked just as hard. Their effort was simply pointed at the feeling of knowing instead of at knowing.

Notice how easy it would be for the first student, if she failed, to say, "I studied so hard and it made no difference." She would be telling the truth about her effort. She would be wrong about what it meant.

The same pattern shows up far from any classroom. A shopkeeper works sixteen hours a day behind the counter and never once asks why customers walk past his door to the shop across the road. A footballer runs extra laps every evening while the weakness that keeps her off the team is her first touch. A job seeker sends the same unchanged CV to two hundred companies. Each of them is working hard. Each of them is mostly practicing the habit of working hard.

* * *

There is a trap here that catches the most sincere people, and the harder you are willing to work, the more likely you are to fall into it.

When something is not working, the natural response of a hardworking person is to do *more* of it. More hours. More applications. More pressure. More sacrifice. It feels like the honorable choice. Doing less, or stopping to question the method, feels like giving up.

But if the method is flawed, more of it only produces the same result faster. Two hundred identical applications are not twice as diligent as one hundred. They may be the same mistake made at scale.

The woodcutter with bleeding hands was not lazy. He was the hardest worker in the clearing. His problem was that every swing of a dull axe felt to him like proof of commitment. He could not see that the commitment itself had become the obstacle.

Persistence needs feedback as much as it needs courage. A method deserves your loyalty only while it is earning your confidence.

Some people need to hear the opposite message, and I will not pretend otherwise. Some people really are drifting, avoiding, waiting for a motivation that never arrives. For them, the next step is simply to start, and to keep going on the days they do not feel like it. If that is you, you probably know it already.

But I have noticed that the people who lie awake asking whether their effort means anything are rarely the drifters. They are usually already working past the point of exhaustion. For them, *try harder* is the least useful advice in the world. What they need is permission to stop, look at the axe and ask a few uncomfortable questions.

* * *

When I am working hard and getting nowhere, these are the questions I have learned to ask.

*What actually decides this?* Not what feels important, but what the person on the other side of the table, the exam, the customer or the market actually rewards. An interview may reward clear stories about past work far more than knowledge of the company's history. A business may live or die on one customer problem the owner keeps avoiding because building the product is more fun. Find the deciding skill and aim there.

*Where is my feedback?* Feedback is information about the gap between what you did and what you were trying to do. It can come from a coach, a practice test, a rejected draft with comments, a recording of yourself, a customer who left, a friend who will tell you the truth. Effort without feedback is archery in the dark. You can draw beautifully for years and never learn where your arrows are going.

*What will I do differently next time?* If the honest answer is "nothing," you are not practicing. You are repeating.

*Can I keep this pace?* Hardworking people avoid this question more than any other, because it sounds like weakness.

* * *

Let me stay with that last question, because our culture has made something strange out of tiredness.

Somewhere along the way, exhaustion became a badge. We tell each other how little we slept as though it were a qualification. We admire the person who never takes a holiday, who answers emails at midnight, who has not seen his children awake all week. Exhaustion feels like proof that we are serious, that we deserve to win, that if the result does not come it will at least not be our fault.

But exhaustion is a cost, not a contribution.

A tired mind learns more slowly, decides worse and notices less. A tired body gets ill. A tired person becomes harder to be around, which quietly closes doors that a rested person would have walked through without noticing. Most of what makes effort effective, the noticing, the adjusting, the patience to sit with a hard problem, the sudden idea in the shower, depends on having something left in the tank.

There is a hidden cost too. When we pour everything into one goal, the rest of life starves: friendships, health, family, faith, joy. If the goal succeeds, we sometimes look around and find we have won a prize in an empty house. If it fails, there is nothing to fall back on. The goal was carrying the whole weight of our life, and when it breaks, everything breaks with it.

I am not telling you to stop working hard. I am telling you that a bow needs tension and rest, and an archer who never unstrings it will one day find it has lost its spring.

* * *

So where does this leave Sara?

If she were my friend, after she had grieved for a few days, I would sit with her and ask some of these questions. Not to suggest the rejection was her fault; it may not have been, and she may never know. But because the next attempt is coming, and the next attempt is the one place where she has real power.

What decided that final round? She can ask. Some companies will tell you, if you ask politely and specifically. She can ask her two contacts what the final interviews tend to reward. She can practice with someone who interrupts her and pushes back, which is far closer to the real thing than the bathroom mirror ever was. She can look at the parts of her preparation that felt good and ask whether they built anything, or whether, like the highlighted notes, they only made her feel ready.

And she can rest. Two years of single-minded wanting is a long time. She has earned a few weeks of doing something else.

None of this guarantees the next job. Nothing will. But it changes the odds, and changing the odds was what effort was for all along. Work like an archer, not like someone beating the ground with a stick.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Exhaustion is a cost, not a contribution.*

**Practice.** Choose one goal you are working hard on and write four short answers. What actually decides success here? Where can I get honest feedback? What will I do differently in the next attempt? What pace could I keep for a whole year? If any answer is "I don't know," that is your next task.

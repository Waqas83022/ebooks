# Chapter 15: The person we hoped to marry

Of all the stories in this book, this is the one I have been most careful with, because it is the one that hurts the most people most deeply.

Let me tell you about Yusuf. He is a composite, like Sana, built from many lives.

Yusuf met Amna at university. They were friends first, then more than friends, carefully and slowly, the way such things often happen when families and futures are involved. For four years they talked about the life they would build. They chose, half-jokingly, the names of children. They talked about which city they would live in. Yusuf took a second job in the evenings so that he could save, because he wanted to come to her family as a man who was ready. Every night, for four years, he prayed for her and for their future together. He believed, with a certainty he could not explain, that she was written for him.

Then, over the course of a year, something changed. Amna became quieter. She was slower to answer his messages. When he asked what was wrong, she said nothing was wrong. And one evening, in a café they had been going to since their second year, she told him, gently and with tears in her eyes, that she could not marry him. She did not see her future with him. She was sorry. She had tried to feel what he felt, and she could not.

A year and a half later, Yusuf saw the wedding photographs.

* * *

I do not know why Amna changed. Neither does Yusuf, not fully. Perhaps she grew in a different direction. Perhaps her feelings were never as strong as his, and it took her time to admit it, even to herself. Perhaps there were reasons in her own life that he never saw. I am not going to invent her motives, because one of the hardest truths of this kind of loss is that the other person's heart is not ours to explain. It is theirs.

And that, I think, is where this chapter has to begin.

* * *

In every other chapter of this book, the archer has been aiming at a target. Targets do not have opinions. A job is filled by a decision, and decisions can be influenced, at least a little, by preparation and persistence. An exam is passed or failed according to fixed rules. Even luck, for all its unpredictability, has no will of its own.

Love is different. In love, the target is also an archer. She has her own bow, her own aim, her own wind and her own hopes. She is choosing too.

This means that love is the one goal in life where your effort must meet another person's freedom. And no amount of effort, devotion, sincerity or prayer can make that freedom disappear. You can be the most loving, faithful, hardworking, deserving person in the world, and the person you love can still, freely, choose a different life. Not because you failed. Because they are free.

> *In love, the target is also an archer.*

This is hard to accept, because the old contract from Chapter 1 runs especially deep in love. We absorb it from every film and song. *If you love someone enough, if you are patient enough, if you prove yourself enough, they will love you back.* It is one of the most beautiful lies we tell, and one of the most dangerous.

It is dangerous for the person who is rejected, because it tells them that they were not loving enough, patient enough, good enough. And it can be dangerous for the person who is loved, too, because a person who believes that devotion creates a right to be loved back may find it very hard to hear the word *no*. Most people, thank God, can hear it. But we all know stories where devotion curdled into pressure, or worse, because someone believed that their effort had earned them a person.

Let me say it as plainly as I can. Devotion and effort do not create a right to another person's heart. They never did. Another person's freedom is not an obstacle to love. It is part of what makes love worth having. A love that could be compelled would not be love at all.

> *Another person's freedom is part of the reality love must respect.*

* * *

Destiny plays a particular role in love, and it is worth looking at closely.

Many of us believe, or want to believe, that there is one person meant for us. The idea is ancient. In Plato's *Symposium*, written in Athens more than two thousand years ago, a group of friends at a dinner party take turns giving speeches about love. The comic playwright Aristophanes tells a myth. Human beings, he says, were once round, double creatures, with four arms, four legs and two faces. They were so strong that the gods feared them, so Zeus split each of them in half. Ever since, every human being has wandered the earth searching for their other half. Love, Aristophanes says, is simply the name we give to the longing to be whole again.

It is a beautiful story, and it has shaped how people talk about love for twenty-four centuries. Soulmates. Other halves. *The one.* *She was written for me.*

But psychologists have found that how strongly we hold this kind of belief makes a real difference to how we handle love's difficulties.

In 1998, the psychologist C. Raymond Knee published a study of what he called *implicit theories of relationships*. Some people, he found, tend to hold what he called *destiny beliefs*: the idea that two people are either meant for each other or they are not, and that you can tell fairly early. Others tend to hold *growth beliefs*: the idea that good relationships are built and developed over time, through working on problems together. Later researchers have described these as "soulmate" beliefs and "work-it-out" beliefs.

Knee followed people's dating relationships over time. People with stronger destiny beliefs were more likely to cope with relationship problems by avoiding them, and the survival of their relationships depended more heavily on how satisfying things felt at the very beginning. People with stronger growth beliefs were more likely to deal with problems by working through them. And after a relationship ended, people with stronger growth beliefs were less likely to look back and say that it had seemed wrong from the beginning. They did not rewrite the whole story because of its ending.

I find these findings deeply relevant to Yusuf, and to anyone who has lost a person they believed was destined for them. Because the belief *she was written for me* does two things after a loss, and both of them can hurt.

First, it can turn a painful loss into a total one. If there was only one person meant for me, and she has gone, then I have lost not just her but my only chance. I am, in Aristophanes' terms, permanently half.

Second, it can keep a person waiting. *If she is truly my destiny, she will come back.* So he waits. He watches her life from a distance. He turns down other possibilities, because they would be a betrayal of what was written. Months become years. This is the lazy argument from Chapter 8, applied to the heart, and it can cost someone the years in which a new love might have begun.

> *A shared future cannot be built from one person's certainty alone.*

* * *

I want to be fair to destiny here. For many people, faith that their life is in good hands is exactly what allows them to survive a loss like this. *If she was not written for me, then someone else is, or something else is.* That can be a deeply healthy thought. It looks backward at the loss and makes peace with it, and it looks forward with openness rather than certainty. It does not say *I know who is written for me.* It says *I trust that my story is not over.*

The difference is the difference we saw in Chapter 8: destiny used to make peace with the past, versus destiny used to dictate the future. *It was not meant to be* can bring peace. *She is meant to be, so I will wait* can bring years of pain.

* * *

There is another version of this story that I should mention, because in many parts of the world it is at least as common as Yusuf's.

Sometimes two people love each other completely, both want to marry and the marriage is prevented by something outside them both: families who refuse, differences of community or class or faith, a visa, money, distance, a parent's illness that ties one of them to another city. In this case, the target was not choosing differently. The target was willing. The wind was simply too strong.

This loss has its own particular grief, because it can carry anger as well as sorrow, and sometimes a lifelong *what if*. If this is your story, everything in Chapter 3 about barriers held in place by people applies to you. Some of those barriers can be moved, with time, patience, allies and courage. Some cannot. Knowing which is which is one of the hardest discernments a person can be asked to make, and you should not have to make it alone.

* * *

Whatever form your loss has taken, I want to say something about the grief itself.

When we love someone and imagine a life with them, we do not just hope for that life. We begin to *live* in it, in our minds. Yusuf had lived in that future for four years. He had walked through the rooms of the house they would share. He had held the children they had named. When Amna said no, he did not only lose a person. He lost a future he had already begun to inhabit.

> *Some losses take away a future we had already begun to live in our minds.*

The family therapist Pauline Boss has a name for one especially painful kind of grief: *ambiguous loss*. It is the loss of someone who is still alive but no longer present in the way they were. There is no funeral. There are no rituals. The world does not recognize what has happened, so it does not offer the support it would offer after a death. And yet something has died: a relationship, a shared future, a version of yourself.

If you are grieving a love that did not become a marriage, please know that your grief is real grief, and it deserves the time and care that any grief deserves. You do not have to be over it by a certain date. You do not have to pretend it was for the best. You do not have to call it a gift.

> *You do not have to call a loss a gift to build a life after it.*

* * *

So what can a person in Yusuf's position actually do?

The first thing is to honor the other person's choice. This is painful, and it is also a kind of dignity. It means not pressuring, not pleading endlessly, not waiting outside her life for a change of heart. It means accepting that *no* is a complete answer. Strangely, many people find that this acceptance is the beginning of their own freedom. As long as you are fighting her choice, you are tied to it. When you honor it, you can begin to walk away.

The second is to protect yourself from the votive tablets. In the age of social media, it is possible to watch the life of the person you lost unfold in real time: the engagement, the wedding, the new home. Every picture is a fresh wound. Muting, unfollowing or simply not looking is not petty. It is medicine. You would not reopen a physical wound every morning to check whether it was healing.

The third is to separate the person from the needs. Ask yourself, when you are ready, what that relationship gave you or promised you. Companionship. Being truly known. Shared faith or values. Laughter. A partner in building a family. A sense of home. These needs were real, and they did not die when the relationship did. *She* cannot be replaced; no person can. But the needs she met, or might have met, can be met again, in time, in a different way, by different people. That is not betrayal. It is life continuing.

The fourth is to let people help you. Grief that is hidden grows heavier. Find the friend, the sibling, the counselor, the imam or priest or elder, the stranger in a support group, who will let you say it out loud.

And the fifth, when the time comes, and only when it comes, is to let your hope find a new direction. Not a new person to fixate on, and not a rushed replacement, but a reopening of the possibility that love may come again in a shape you have not yet imagined.

> *Hope should leave room for a future you have not yet imagined.*

* * *

Yusuf did not do these things quickly. For months he did almost none of them. He checked her profile at night. He went back to the café alone. He prayed for her to come back, and then, later, he prayed simply to stop hurting.

The change, when it came, was small. One night, instead of praying for Amna, he found himself praying *for* her: that she would be happy, that her marriage would be good, that she would be well. He was surprised to find he meant it. Something in his chest loosened. He did not stop loving her that night. But he stopped holding on to her, and his hands, for the first time in a long time, were free.

We will see where those hands led him in the last chapter. For now, I want to leave you with the truest sentence I know about love and effort.

You can give someone your whole heart. You cannot give them your will. And you cannot take theirs.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*In love, the target is also an archer.*

*Another person's freedom is part of the reality love must respect.*

*A shared future cannot be built from one person's certainty alone.*

*You do not have to call a loss a gift to build a life after it.*

**Practice.** If you are grieving a love that did not become the life you hoped for, write two lists. In the first, write what you loved about that person specifically. Let it be as long as it needs to be; this is part of grieving. In the second, write the needs that relationship met or promised to meet: companionship, being known, shared values, family, home. Read the second list slowly. These needs are still yours, and they are still worthy of being met.

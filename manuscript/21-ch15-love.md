# Chapter 15: Two yeses

Some goals depend on a committee, a market or the weather. The most painful ones depend on a single human heart.

You can want a business partner who does not want you. You can want a father who will not forgive you. You can want a friendship back after a falling-out the other person has decided to let stand. You can want your family to bless a marriage they will not bless. And you can want, more than you have ever wanted anything, to spend your life with a person who does not want to spend theirs with you.

Of all the stories in this book, this is the one I have been most careful with, because it hurts the most people most deeply.

Let me tell you about Adam. He is a composite, built from many lives.

Adam met Lina at university. They were friends first, then more than friends, slowly and carefully, the way such things often go when families and futures are involved. For four years they talked about the life they would build. They chose, half-joking, the names of their children. They argued about which city they would live in. Adam took a second job in the evenings so he could save, because he wanted to come to her family as a man who was ready. He believed, with a certainty he could not explain, that she was meant for him.

Then, over the course of a year, something changed. Lina grew quieter. She was slower to answer his messages. When he asked what was wrong, she said nothing was wrong. And one evening, in a café they had gone to since their second year, she told him, gently and with tears in her eyes, that she could not marry him. She did not see her future with him. She was sorry. She had tried to feel what he felt, and she could not.

A year and a half later, Adam saw the wedding photographs.

* * *

I do not know why Lina changed. Neither does Adam, not fully. Perhaps she grew in a different direction. Perhaps her feelings were never as strong as his, and it took her time to admit it, even to herself. Perhaps there were reasons in her life he never saw. I will not invent her motives, because one of the hardest truths of this kind of loss is that the other person's heart is not ours to explain. It is theirs.

And that is where this chapter has to begin.

* * *

In almost every other chapter of this book, the archer has been aiming at a target. Targets do not have opinions. An exam is marked by rules. A job is filled by a decision that preparation can at least influence. Even luck, for all its unpredictability, has no will of its own.

Love is different. Here the target can choose. It has its own aim, its own wind and its own hopes.

This makes love the one goal where your effort must meet another person's freedom. No amount of effort, devotion, sincerity or prayer can make that freedom disappear. You can be the most loving, faithful, hardworking, deserving person in the world, and the person you love can still, freely, choose a different life. Not because you failed. Because they are free.

A future together takes two yeses. One person's yes, however strong, is only half of it.

This is hard to accept, because the old contract from Chapter 1 runs especially deep in love. We absorb it from every film and every song. *If you love someone enough, if you are patient enough, if you prove yourself enough, they will love you back.* It is one of the most beautiful lies we tell, and one of the most dangerous.

It is dangerous for the person who is turned down, because it tells them they were not loving enough, patient enough or good enough. And it can be dangerous for the person who is loved, because someone who believes that devotion earns a right to be loved back may find it very hard to hear the word *no*. Most people can hear it, thankfully. But we all know stories where devotion curdled into pressure, or worse, because someone believed their effort had earned them a person.

Let me say it as plainly as I can. Devotion and effort do not create a right to another person's heart. They never did. The other person's freedom is not an obstacle to love. It is part of what makes love worth having. A love that could be forced would not be love at all.

* * *

Destiny plays a special role in love, and it deserves a close look.

Many of us believe, or want to believe, that there is one person meant for us. The idea is ancient. In Plato's *Symposium*, written in Athens more than two thousand years ago, a group of friends at a dinner party take turns giving speeches about love. The comic playwright Aristophanes tells a myth. Human beings, he says, were once round, double creatures, with four arms, four legs and two faces. They were so strong that the gods feared them, so Zeus split each of them in half. Ever since, every person has wandered the earth searching for their other half. Love is simply the name we give to the longing to be whole again.

It is a beautiful story, and it has shaped how people talk about love for twenty-four centuries. Soulmates. Other halves. *The one.* *She was meant for me.*

Psychologists have found that how strongly we hold this kind of belief changes how we handle love's difficulties.

In 1998, the psychologist C. Raymond Knee published a study of what he called *implicit theories of relationships*. Some people, he found, hold strong *destiny beliefs*: two people are either meant for each other or they are not, and you can tell fairly early. Others hold strong *growth beliefs*: good relationships are built over time, by working through problems together. Later researchers described these as "soulmate" beliefs and "work-it-out" beliefs.

Knee followed people's dating relationships over time. People with stronger destiny beliefs were more likely to cope with problems by avoiding them, and the survival of their relationships depended more heavily on how good things felt at the very start. People with stronger growth beliefs were more likely to work through problems. And after a relationship ended, people with stronger growth beliefs were less likely to look back and declare it had been wrong from the beginning. They did not rewrite the whole story because of its ending.

These findings matter to anyone who has lost someone they believed was destined for them. Because the belief *she was meant for me* does two things after a loss, and both can hurt.

First, it can turn a painful loss into a total one. If there was only one person meant for me and she has gone, then I have lost not just her but my only chance. I am, in Aristophanes' terms, permanently half.

Second, it can keep a person waiting. *If she is truly meant for me, she will come back.* So he waits. He watches her life from a distance. He turns down other possibilities because they feel like betrayals of what was meant. Months become years. This is the lazy argument from Chapter 8, applied to the heart, and it can cost someone the very years in which a new love might have begun.

You are not half of anything. You were whole before you met them, and you are whole now, even if it does not feel that way tonight.

* * *

I want to be fair to destiny here. For many people, faith that their life is in good hands is exactly what helps them survive a loss like this. *If she was not meant for me, then someone else is, or something else is.* That can be a deeply healthy thought. It looks backward at the loss and makes peace with it, and it looks forward with openness instead of certainty. It does not say *I know who is meant for me.* It says *I trust that my story is not over.*

The difference is the one we met in Chapter 8: destiny used to make peace with the past, versus destiny used to dictate the future. *It was not meant to be* can bring peace. *She is meant to be, so I will wait* can bring years of pain.

* * *

There is another version of this story, and in many parts of the world it is at least as common as Adam's.

Sometimes both people say yes, and someone else says no. Two people love each other completely, both want to marry, and the marriage is blocked by something outside them: families who refuse, differences of community or class or faith, a visa, money, distance, a parent's illness that ties one of them to another city. Here the target was willing. The wind was simply too strong.

This loss has its own particular grief, because it often carries anger as well as sorrow, and sometimes a lifelong *what if*. If this is your story, everything in Chapter 3 about barriers held in place by people applies to you. Some of those barriers can be moved, with time, patience, allies and courage. Some cannot. Knowing which is which is one of the hardest judgments a person is ever asked to make, and you should not have to make it alone.

* * *

Whatever form your loss has taken, remember what we saw in Chapter 11. When you love someone and imagine a life with them, you do not only hope for that life. You begin to live in it. Adam had lived in that future for four years. He had walked through the rooms of the house they would share. He had held children they had named. When Lina said no, he lost a person and a future at the same time, and there was no funeral for either.

His grief was real grief. Yours is too. You do not have to be over it by a certain date, and you do not have to call it a gift.

* * *

So what can someone in Adam's position actually do?

First, honor the other person's choice. This is painful, and it is also a kind of dignity. It means no pressure, no endless pleading, no waiting outside their life for a change of heart. It means accepting that *no* is a complete answer. Many people find, to their surprise, that this is where their own freedom begins. As long as you are fighting their choice, you are tied to it. When you honor it, you can start to walk away.

Second, protect yourself from the planes that came back. Social media lets you watch the life of the person you lost unfold in real time: the engagement, the wedding, the new home. Every picture is a fresh wound. Muting, unfollowing or simply not looking is not petty. It is medicine. You would not reopen a cut every morning to check whether it was healing.

Third, separate the person from the needs. When you are ready, ask what the relationship gave you or promised you. Companionship. Being truly known. Shared values. Laughter. A partner in building a family. A sense of home. Those needs were real, and they did not end when the relationship did. The person cannot be replaced; no person can. But the needs can be met again, in time, in a different way, by different people. That is not betrayal. It is life continuing.

Fourth, let people in. Grief that is hidden grows heavier. Find the friend, the sibling, the counselor, the elder, the stranger in a support group who will let you say it out loud.

And fifth, when the time comes, and only then, let your hope find a new direction. Not a new person to fixate on, and not a rushed replacement, but a reopening to the possibility that love may come again in a shape you have not yet imagined.

* * *

Adam did not do these things quickly. For months he did almost none of them. He checked her profile every night before sleep. He went back to the café alone. He wrote long messages to her and deleted them.

The change, when it came, was small and ordinary. One evening, nearly a year after the café, he picked up his phone at eleven o'clock as usual to look at her page. And this time he put it face down on the table instead. He sat there for a minute, feeling slightly ridiculous. Then he picked it up again and sent a message to an old friend from university he had been avoiding for months: *Are you free on Saturday? Let's get food.*

The friend replied within a minute. They went out that Saturday, and the one after. Nothing dramatic happened. He did not stop loving Lina that night. But he stopped arranging his evenings around her absence, and for the first time in a long while, his hands were free for something else.

It was not a revelation. It was a choice, made once, and then made again the next night, and the night after that. That is usually how the turn begins. Not with a new feeling, but with a new action, repeated until the feeling follows.

We will see where those free hands led him in the last chapter. For now, here is the truest sentence I know about love and effort. You can give someone your whole heart. You cannot give them your will, and you cannot take theirs.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*A future together takes two yeses.*

**Practice.** If you are grieving a future that needed someone else's yes, write two lists. In the first, write what you loved about that person or that relationship, as long as it needs to be; this is part of grieving. In the second, write the needs it met or promised to meet. Then choose one small action for tonight that serves one of those needs in a different way: a call, a plan with a friend, an evening spent somewhere new.

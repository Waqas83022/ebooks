# Chapter 7: A good decision can lose

On the first of February 2015, in the final seconds of the Super Bowl, the most-watched sporting event in the United States, a football coach named Pete Carroll made a decision that would follow him for the rest of his career.

His team, the Seattle Seahawks, were losing to the New England Patriots by four points. There were twenty-six seconds left on the clock. Seattle had the ball just one yard from the end zone, with three chances left to score. On the field was Marshawn Lynch, one of the most powerful running backs in the game, a man famous for carrying the ball through crowds of defenders. Almost everyone watching expected Carroll to hand the ball to Lynch.

Instead, Carroll called a pass. The quarterback threw it. A little-known Patriots player named Malcolm Butler stepped in front of the receiver and intercepted it. The game was over. New England won.

The next morning, the newspapers were nearly unanimous. It was the worst call in Super Bowl history. Carroll was a fool. He had thrown away a championship.

The poker champion Annie Duke opens her book *Thinking in Bets* with this moment, and she makes an argument that, the first time I heard it, I resisted. Carroll's decision, she suggests, may not have been bad at all.

There was a logic to it. With little time left and only one timeout, a pass that fell incomplete would stop the clock, leaving time for two more attempts. Passes from the one-yard line were rarely intercepted. If the pass had been caught for a touchdown, people would have called Carroll clever. If it had fallen incomplete, nobody would remember it; Lynch would have got his chances on the next two plays. Only one of the likely outcomes, and an unlikely one at that, would turn the call into a disaster. And that is the one that happened.

Football experts still argue about whether the call was wise. That is fine. My point, and Duke's, is narrower. The *outcome* alone cannot settle it. A good decision can lose. And the world, almost always, judges the decision by the loss.

* * *

Poker players have a word for this mistake. They call it *resulting*: judging the quality of a decision by the quality of its result.

It is an easy mistake to see in poker, because poker makes the role of luck so obvious. A player can make exactly the right decision, betting with the best hand, and lose because the last card goes against her. Another player can make a reckless bet with a poor hand and win because the last card goes his way. Over one hand, the reckless player looks smart. Over a thousand hands, the careful player takes his money.

Professional poker players train themselves, painfully, to stop judging their play by individual hands. They review their decisions by asking whether the choice was sound given what they knew at the time, and they let the cards fall where they fall. If they did not, they would be driven mad, or they would start learning the wrong lessons from good luck and bad luck alike.

Most of us, in the rest of life, are not trained this way. We judge our choices entirely by how they turn out.

* * *

Let me offer you a simple picture that has helped me.

Every decision we make, and every result that follows, can land in one of four boxes.

In the first box, a good decision leads to a good result. You prepare carefully for an exam and you pass. We call this *deserved success*, and it is the box the old contract promised we would always land in.

In the second box, a good decision leads to a bad result. You take the safe road home and a drunk driver hits you. You choose a stable job and the company collapses. This is *bad luck*.

In the third box, a bad decision leads to a good result. You drive home after drinking and arrive safely. You invest your savings on a tip from a stranger and it doubles. This is *dumb luck*, and it is dangerous, because it teaches the wrong lesson.

In the fourth box, a bad decision leads to a bad result. This is the box where honest learning lives, and where real responsibility belongs.

The trouble is that when we look back, we tend to collapse these four boxes into two. Good results must have come from good decisions. Bad results must have come from bad decisions. The driver who made it home safely concludes that he drives fine after a few drinks. The person who chose carefully and lost concludes that she chose badly.

> *Judge the decision by what you knew, not by what the dice did.*

* * *

Let me tell you about Omar. He is a composite, too.

Omar was offered a job at a large company. The salary was good, a little better than his current one. But during the interviews, he noticed things that worried him. The manager he would work for spoke dismissively about the people who had left the team. Two former employees he contacted told him, separately, that the department burned through people in a year. The role was also further from the kind of work he wanted to be doing in five years.

Omar took his time. He talked to his wife and to a mentor he trusted. He looked at his savings. He thought about how likely he was to find something better within a few months, and concluded that it was reasonably likely, given the state of the market that spring. He declined the offer.

Two months later, an economic shock rippled through his industry. Hiring froze almost everywhere. The better job he had expected to find did not appear. Omar's own company began laying people off, and in the autumn, he was one of them. He spent eight months unemployed.

Throughout those eight months, people told him, some kindly and some not, that he had made a terrible mistake. He should have taken the offer. What was he thinking?

Was it a terrible mistake?

If we judge by the result, yes. Obviously. If he had taken the job, he would have had a salary during the hard months.

But if we judge by the decision, it is much less obvious. Omar had good reasons for his concerns. He gathered evidence instead of guessing. He sought advice. He considered his finances. The economic shock that followed was something that very few people predicted. And there is no way of knowing what would have happened in the job he declined; that company also cut staff later that year, and the newest employees are often the first to go.

Omar may have made a reasonable choice that landed in the second box. He may also have made some real mistakes. Perhaps he underestimated the risk of a downturn, or did not have a backup plan. Those are worth examining. What is not worth doing is concluding, simply from the bad result, that the whole decision was foolish and that he cannot trust his own judgment.

> *An outcome is evidence to examine, not a verdict on the person who faced it.*

* * *

I want to be careful not to swing too far the other way, because "it was just bad luck" can become a hiding place.

Some people use luck as a permanent excuse. Every failure is the market, or the boss, or the universe. Nothing is ever their responsibility. This is as much a distortion as the opposite, and it is just as dangerous, because a person who believes every bad result is bad luck will never learn anything from their mistakes.

The goal is not to protect yourself from responsibility. The goal is to place responsibility accurately. That means separating three things that tend to blur together when we look back.

*What did I know at the time?* Be honest. Not what you know now, but what was available to you then.

*What could I have known, with reasonable effort?* Sometimes the information was out there and you did not look. That is worth learning from.

*What could nobody have known?* The interception. The economic shock. The mold spore on the dish. These belong to the wind.

When you separate these three, two things happen. You become much harder on yourself in a useful way, because you can see clearly where you did not do your homework. And you become much gentler on yourself in a necessary way, because you can see clearly what was never in your hands.

* * *

There is a practice that some investors, doctors and poker players use, and I think it is one of the most useful habits a thoughtful person can build. It is called a decision journal.

Before you make an important decision, write down a few things. What you are deciding. Why. What you expect to happen, and how confident you are. What alternatives you considered. What would make you change your mind. And a date, a few months or a year away, when you will look at it again.

That is all. Then you make the decision and get on with your life.

When the review date comes, read what you wrote before you knew how things turned out. This is the part that matters most, because our memories are not honest about our past reasoning. Once we know the result, we tend to remember ourselves as having been more certain, or more doubtful, or wiser, or more foolish than we really were. Psychologists have shown this many times; we will meet the research in Chapter 9. The journal is a letter from your past self that your present self cannot edit.

You will learn two kinds of things from it. Sometimes you will find that you made a careful decision and the world simply went the other way. You can let go of the shame. Sometimes you will find that you ignored a warning sign you had actually written down. You can learn from that, specifically, without condemning your entire character.

* * *

I find the four boxes strangely consoling, and I want to tell you why.

If good decisions always led to good results, then every failure would be proof of a bad decision, and every disappointment would be a judgment on your intelligence, your character or your faith. That is the world the old contract describes, and it is a harsh world to live in, because everyone in it eventually fails.

But we do not live in that world. We live in one where good decisions can lose and bad ones can win. It sounds unfair, and in a sense it is. But it also means that your worth as a decision-maker, and as a person, cannot be read off any single result. It can only be seen in the way you choose, over and over, across many years, many decisions and many different kinds of weather.

Pete Carroll, for what it is worth, did not stop coaching after that night. He went back to work, took the criticism and kept making decisions. Some of them won. Some of them lost. That is what a career looks like from the inside, and what a life looks like, too.

> *A good decision can lose. A bad decision can win. Only one of them can be repeated on purpose.*

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Judge the decision by what you knew, not by what the dice did.*

*An outcome is evidence to examine, not a verdict on the person who faced it.*

*A good decision can lose. A bad decision can win. Only one of them can be repeated on purpose.*

**Practice.** Before your next important decision, write a short note: what you are deciding, why, what you expect, what alternatives you rejected, what would change your mind, and a date to review it. Seal it, literally or digitally. When the date comes, compare your reasoning with the result, and sort what happened into what you knew, what you could have known and what nobody could have known.

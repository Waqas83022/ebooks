# Chapter 7: A good decision can lose

On the first of February 2015, in the final seconds of the Super Bowl, the most-watched sporting event in the United States, a football coach named Pete Carroll made a decision that would follow him for the rest of his career.

His team, the Seattle Seahawks, were four points behind the New England Patriots. Twenty-six seconds remained. Seattle had the ball one yard from the end zone, with three chances left to score. On the field was Marshawn Lynch, one of the most powerful runners in the game, famous for carrying the ball through crowds of defenders. Almost everyone watching expected Carroll to hand the ball to Lynch.

Instead, he called a pass. The quarterback threw it. A little-known Patriots defender named Malcolm Butler stepped in front of the receiver and caught it. The game was over. New England won.

The next morning the verdict was close to unanimous. Commentators called it the worst decision in the history of the Super Bowl. Carroll was a fool. He had thrown away a championship.

The poker champion Annie Duke opens her book *Thinking in Bets* with this moment, and she makes an argument that I resisted the first time I heard it. Carroll's decision, she suggests, may not have been bad at all.

There was a logic to it. With little time left and only one timeout, a pass that fell incomplete would stop the clock and leave time for two more attempts. Passes from the one-yard line were rarely intercepted. If the pass had been caught for a touchdown, people would have called Carroll brilliant. If it had fallen incomplete, nobody would remember it; Lynch would have had his chances on the next two plays. Only one of the likely outcomes, and an unlikely one, turned the call into a disaster. That is the one that happened.

Football experts still argue about whether the call was wise. That is fine. My point, and Duke's, is narrower. The *outcome* alone cannot settle it. A good decision can lose. And the world almost always judges the decision by the loss.

* * *

Poker players have a word for this mistake. They call it *resulting*: judging the quality of a decision by the quality of its result.

The mistake is easy to see in poker because the cards make the role of luck so obvious. A player can make exactly the right decision, betting with the best hand, and lose because the last card goes against her. Another player can make a reckless bet with a weak hand and win because the last card goes his way. Over one hand, the reckless player looks clever. Over a thousand hands, the careful player takes his money.

Professional players train themselves, painfully, to stop judging their play by single hands. They review each decision by asking whether it was sound given what they knew at the time, and they let the cards fall where they fall. If they did not, they would either go mad or start learning the wrong lessons from good luck and bad luck alike.

Most of us, in the rest of life, are not trained this way. We judge our choices entirely by how they turn out.

A doctor chooses the treatment with the best evidence behind it, and the patient has a rare reaction. The family blames the doctor. A farmer plants the crop that has done well in his region for twenty years, and an unusual season ruins it, while his neighbor's careless gamble on a different crop pays off. The village decides the neighbor is wise. A student picks the university course that fits her talents, and the industry it leads to shrinks five years later. Her relatives tell her she should have listened to them.

In every case, the result is real. In every case, the judgment of the decision may be completely wrong.

* * *

Here is a simple picture that has helped me.

Every decision we make, and every result that follows, can land in one of four boxes.

In the first box, a good decision leads to a good result. You prepare carefully for an exam and you pass. This is *deserved success*, the box the old contract promised we would always land in.

In the second box, a good decision leads to a bad result. You take the safe road home and a drunk driver hits you. You choose a stable employer and it collapses. This is *bad luck*.

In the third box, a bad decision leads to a good result. You drive home after drinking and arrive safely. You put your savings on a stranger's tip and it doubles. This is *dumb luck*, and it is dangerous, because it teaches the wrong lesson.

In the fourth box, a bad decision leads to a bad result. This is where honest learning lives, and where real responsibility belongs.

The trouble is that when we look back, we collapse the four boxes into two. Good results must have come from good decisions. Bad results must have come from bad ones. The driver who made it home concludes that he drives fine after a few drinks. The person who chose carefully and lost concludes that she chose badly.

Judge the decision by what you knew, not by what the dice did.

* * *

Let me tell you about Omar. He is a composite, too.

Omar was offered a job at a large company. The salary was good, slightly better than his current one. But during the interviews he noticed things that worried him. The manager he would work for spoke with contempt about people who had left the team. Two former employees he contacted told him, separately, that the department burned through people within a year. The role also pulled him away from the work he wanted to be doing in five years.

Omar took his time. He talked to his wife and to a mentor he trusted. He looked at his savings. He thought about how likely he was to find something better within a few months, and concluded, given the state of the market that spring, that it was reasonably likely. He declined the offer.

Two months later, an economic shock rippled through his industry. Hiring froze almost everywhere. The better job he had expected did not appear. His own company began cutting staff, and that autumn he was one of them. He spent eight months unemployed.

Throughout those months, people told him, some kindly and some not, that he had made a terrible mistake. What had he been thinking?

Was it a terrible mistake?

Judged by the result, obviously. If he had taken the job, he would have had a salary through the hard months.

Judged as a decision, it is far less obvious. Omar had real reasons for his concerns. He gathered evidence instead of guessing. He sought advice. He considered his finances. The shock that followed was something very few people saw coming. And there is no way to know what would have happened in the job he declined; that company also cut staff later that year, and the newest employees are often the first to go.

Omar may have made a reasonable choice that landed in the second box. He may also have made some genuine mistakes. Perhaps he underestimated the risk of a downturn, or had no backup plan. Those are worth examining. What is not worth doing is concluding, from the bad result alone, that the whole decision was foolish and that he can no longer trust his own judgment.

An outcome is evidence to examine. It is not a verdict on the person who faced it.

* * *

I do not want to swing too far the other way, because "it was just bad luck" can become a hiding place.

Some people use luck as a permanent excuse. Every failure is the market, the boss or the universe. Nothing is ever their responsibility. That is as much a distortion as the opposite, and just as dangerous, because a person who believes every bad result is bad luck never learns anything.

The goal is not to protect yourself from responsibility. It is to place responsibility accurately. That means separating three things that blur together when we look back.

*What did I know at the time?* Not what you know now. What was actually available to you then.

*What could I have known, with reasonable effort?* Sometimes the information was there and you did not look. That is worth learning from.

*What could nobody have known?* The interception. The economic shock. The mold spore on the dish. These belong to the wind.

When you separate these three, two things happen at once. You become harder on yourself in a useful way, because you can see exactly where you skipped your homework. And you become gentler on yourself in a necessary way, because you can see exactly what was never in your hands.

* * *

There is a practice that some investors, doctors and poker players use, and I think it is one of the most valuable habits a thoughtful person can build. It is called a decision journal.

Before an important decision, write down a few things. What you are deciding. Why. What you expect to happen, and how confident you are. What alternatives you considered. What would change your mind. And a date, a few months or a year away, when you will look at it again.

Then make the decision and get on with your life.

When the review date comes, read what you wrote before you knew how things turned out. This is the part that matters most, because our memories are not honest about our past reasoning. Once we know the result, we tend to remember ourselves as more certain, or more doubtful, or wiser, or more foolish than we really were. Psychologists have shown this many times; we will meet the research in Chapter 9. The journal is a letter from your past self that your present self cannot edit.

You will learn two kinds of things from it. Sometimes you will find you made a careful decision and the world simply went the other way, and you can put down the shame. Sometimes you will find you ignored a warning you had actually written down, and you can learn from that specific mistake without condemning your whole character.

* * *

I find the four boxes strangely consoling, and I want to tell you why.

If good decisions always led to good results, every failure would prove a bad decision, and every disappointment would be a judgment on your intelligence, your character or your faith. That is the world the old contract describes. It is a harsh world to live in, because everyone in it eventually fails.

We do not live in that world. We live in one where good decisions can lose and bad decisions can win. It sounds unfair, and in a sense it is. But it also means your worth as a decision-maker, and as a person, cannot be read from any single result. It can only be seen in the way you choose, again and again, across many years and many kinds of weather.

Pete Carroll, for what it is worth, did not stop coaching after that night. He took the criticism, went back to work and kept making decisions. Some of them won. Some of them lost. That is what a career looks like from the inside. It is what a life looks like, too. A bad decision can win once. Only a good one can be repeated on purpose.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Judge the decision by what you knew, not by what the dice did.*

**Practice.** Before your next important decision, write a short note: what you are deciding, why, what you expect, which alternatives you rejected, what would change your mind, and a date to review it. Seal it, on paper or on your phone. When the date comes, compare your reasoning with the result, and sort what happened into what you knew, what you could have known and what nobody could have known.

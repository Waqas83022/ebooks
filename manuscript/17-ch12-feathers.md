# Chapter 12: The thing with feathers

The oldest story about hope I know is also one of the strangest.

The Greek poet Hesiod, writing around seven hundred years before the common era, tells how the god Zeus, angry with humankind, created the first woman, Pandora, and sent her to earth with a large storage jar. (Centuries later, a translator turned the jar into a "box," and the box is how most of us know the story.) Inside were all the evils that would trouble human life: sickness, toil, sorrow and the rest. Until then, people had lived without them.

Pandora lifted the lid. The evils flew out and scattered across the world, where they have been ever since. In terror, she slammed the lid back down. One thing was left inside, caught under the rim, unable to escape.

It was hope.

For nearly three thousand years, people have argued about what this means. Why was hope in a jar full of evils? Was it kept inside so that it would stay with us, the one consolation we kept? Or was hope itself one of the evils, the cruelest of them all?

The German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche chose the darkest reading. Zeus, he argued, did not want people to throw away their lives, however much the other evils tormented them. He wanted them to keep enduring the torment. So he gave them hope, which keeps human suffering going longer than it otherwise would, and is therefore the worst evil of all.

When I first read that, I wanted to argue. Then I thought of the people I know who spent years waiting for something that was never going to come, and I was less sure.

* * *

About seventeen years before Nietzsche wrote those words, in a small town in Massachusetts, a woman who rarely left her family's house wrote a poem that gives the opposite answer. Emily Dickinson published almost nothing in her lifetime; her poems were gathered and printed only after her death. In one of them she pictures hope as a small bird with feathers that lives inside the soul. It sings a song without words. It never stops singing, not even in the fiercest storm, in the coldest land, on the strangest sea. And in the poem's last lines she notices something remarkable about this bird: in all its years of singing through her worst moments, it never once asked her for even a crumb.

For Dickinson, hope is not a torment. It is a gift that asks for nothing. It keeps singing whether or not the singing changes anything, and keeps a person company in the dark.

So who is right? Is hope the last evil in the jar, or the bird that sings in the storm?

I have come to believe they are both right, because they are describing two different things that share one name. The rest of this chapter is about telling them apart, and about growing the kind that helps.

* * *

The first thing to understand is that hope is not optimism, though we often use the words as if they were twins.

In 2005, the psychologists Patricia Bruininks and Bertram Malle asked people to describe what they meant by hope, optimism, wishing and related states, and to write about times they had felt each one. A clear pattern emerged. Optimism was about outcomes people expected to go well. Hope was about outcomes that mattered more to them, that were less likely, and that were less under their own control. People were optimistic about things that seemed probable. They hoped for things that were important and uncertain.

That small finding explains a great deal. Hope lives exactly where this whole book lives: at the edge of our control, where effort meets the wind. You do not need hope to switch on a light. You need it to apply for the job, start the treatment, wait for the result, ask the question, plant the field. Optimism is a forecast. Hope is a decision to keep moving toward something you cannot be sure of.

Which means that hope is not a soft feeling for people who cannot face facts. It is the particular courage required to act when facts run out.

* * *

In the 1980s and 1990s, a psychologist at the University of Kansas named Charles Richard Snyder set out to study hope scientifically. Until then, most psychologists treated it as a vague, warm mood. Snyder argued that real hope is more specific and more practical, and that it has two parts.

The first he called *agency*. It is the sense that you can get yourself moving toward a goal and keep going. Snyder sometimes called it willpower: the inner voice that says, *I can start, and I can keep at it.*

The second he called *pathways*. It is the ability to see routes to your goal, and, most important, to find new routes when the first one is blocked. Snyder sometimes called it waypower: the inner voice that says, *Here is one way. And if that does not work, here is another.*

Snyder and his colleagues built a short questionnaire to measure these two parts, and over the following decades researchers used it in hundreds of studies. People who scored higher on hope tended to set clearer goals, think of more ways to reach them and keep going longer when they hit obstacles. Higher hope has been linked, in many of these studies, with better results in school and sport and with better coping during illness.

What I find most useful is the second part. High-hope people are not simply people who feel sure things will work out. They are people who can see more than one road. When one closes, they do not conclude that the destination is lost. They start looking for another way there.

That is the job of this chapter. In the last chapter we stood at the place where a road ended. Now we look for others.

* * *

The psychologist Gabriele Oettingen, working first in Germany and later in New York, discovered something about hope that surprised even her, and that everyone who dreams about anything should know.

In one study, published with Doris Mayer in 2002, she asked university students who were about to graduate how often they fantasized about getting their dream job, and how positive those fantasies were. Then she followed them for two years.

You might expect the students with the most vivid, glowing fantasies to have done best. They were the most hopeful, surely. The opposite happened. Two years later, the graduates who had indulged most in positive fantasies had received fewer job offers and were earning lower salaries than those whose thoughts about the future had been more mixed. Oettingen found similar patterns elsewhere: among patients hoping to recover well from hip surgery, and among students hoping that a crush would turn into a relationship.

Her explanation is that a vivid positive fantasy lets the mind taste the reward in advance. It feels wonderful. And because it feels as if we have already arrived, it drains away some of the energy we need to make the actual trip.

Oettingen did not conclude that we should stop dreaming. She found that a dream becomes powerful when it is joined to an honest look at the obstacle. She called this *mental contrasting*: first imagine the wish and its best outcome as vividly as you can, then imagine, just as vividly, the main thing inside you or around you that stands in the way. When people did this, those with a realistic chance of success became more energized and more likely to act. She later combined it with a simple if-then plan, *if this obstacle appears, then I will do that*, into a method she calls WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan.

I should be honest about the size of the effect. A review published in 2021 found that combining mental contrasting with if-then plans helps people reach their goals on average, but the effect is small to moderate, and some published studies may have overstated it. It is a useful tool, not a magic one. But the insight underneath matches what I have seen in life. The people who dream best are not the people who dream most. They are the people whose dream can look the obstacle in the eye.

Fantasy rehearses the arrival. Hope rehearses the road.

* * *

There is one more voice I want to bring into this conversation, because it reaches the deepest layer of hope.

Václav Havel was a Czech playwright who spent years opposing the communist government of his country. He was watched, harassed and imprisoned. His plays were banned. The regime looked permanent, and he had every reason to believe that nothing he did would change anything. In a long series of conversations recorded in the mid-1980s, later published as *Disturbing the Peace*, he was asked whether he still had hope.

His answer has been repeated around the world ever since. Hope, he said, is not the same as optimism. It is not a conviction that things will turn out well. It is a certainty that what you are doing makes sense, however it turns out.

A few years later, in 1989, the regime fell, almost without bloodshed, and Havel became president of his country. But notice that his hope never depended on that. He was not hoping because he predicted the government's collapse. He was hoping because he believed that living truthfully was worth doing whether the government fell or not.

This is hope that the outcome cannot break, because it was never staked on the outcome. It is staked on meaning. And it is the kind the archer needs most. She hopes to hit the target, of course. But beneath that hope lies a deeper one: that drawing the bow well is worth doing, that the practice and the aim and the courage to let go make sense, whether this particular arrow lands or not.

* * *

For many people, hope has an older and more serious form, and its name is prayer.

I will not tell you what happens on the other side of a prayer. That is a question of faith, and people answer it differently. But anyone, believer or not, can see what prayer does on this side, in the life of the person who prays, and it is remarkably close to everything this chapter has described.

Prayer names what you want, and in naming it, you often discover what you really want. A person who prays every night for "the job" may find one night that the words have changed into a prayer for security, or dignity, or a chance to use their gifts. That discovery opens new roads to the same place.

Prayer marks the edge of your power. Nobody prays to be able to lift their own hand. We pray about the things we cannot fully control: the decision on the other side of the desk, the result of the scan, the heart of another person. It draws the line, honestly, between the bow and the wind.

And in every tradition I know of, prayer was never meant to replace effort. The people most respected for their prayers were also respected for their work. You breathe in. Then you draw the bow.

One more thing, because so many people carry this wound. An unanswered prayer is not a verdict on the one who prayed. On the morning of Sara's interview, her mother prayed for her. On the same morning, Daniel's grandmother prayed for him. Both prayers were sincere. Both came from love. There was one job. Whatever you believe about how prayer works, a world where many people want the same scarce things cannot grant every sincere prayer in the form it was asked. A *no* is not proof that you were not loved, or not sincere enough, or not good enough.

* * *

Now we can see what Nietzsche and Dickinson were each describing.

Nietzsche's hope is the one that becomes a substitute for action and a refusal to look at reality. It is the fantasy that keeps a person waiting at a door that has closed, rehearsing a reunion that will never come, refusing every other possibility because *this* one must come true. That kind of hope really can prolong suffering. It is a bird that sings so sweetly you never leave the cage.

Dickinson's hope is something else. It does not demand a particular outcome. It asks for no crumb. It is a basic turning of the soul toward life, a refusal to let the dark have the last word, even when you cannot see what morning will bring.

So here is a working picture of healthy hope.

It has a *direction*: something you value, something worth moving toward. That is Havel's hope, and Dickinson's bird.

It has *roads*: a concrete next step, and more than one route. That is Snyder's pathways.

It looks at the *obstacle* honestly. That is Oettingen's contrast.

And it never makes a single outcome the condition of its own survival.

Give your hope more than one road to travel.

* * *

When Sara was preparing for her interview, she sometimes let herself imagine her first day: the lanyard, the call to her father. That was natural, and I would not take the pleasure from her. But the parts of her preparation that actually changed her odds were not the fantasies. They were the moments when she looked straight at the obstacle. *I freeze when they ask about failures. So I will practice that answer until I don't.* That was hope rehearsing the road.

After the rejection, once she had grieved, her hope had to change shape. It could no longer be a hope for that particular glass building. But when she asked what the building had stood for in her imagination, she found three things underneath it: the security of a good salary, the respect that comes from doing serious work, and the chance to learn fast from talented people. None of these lived only inside one company.

She wrote the three words on a card and stuck it above her desk. Then she wrote, underneath, three different roads that could lead to them. It took her ten minutes. It was the first time in weeks she had felt the bird begin to sing.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Give your hope more than one road to travel.*

**Practice.** Write down one thing you are hoping for. Underneath it, write what this hope is really about, the value beneath the outcome. Then write the main obstacle, inside you or around you, as honestly as you can, and one if-then plan: "If this obstacle appears, then I will..." Finally, list three different roads that could lead toward the value, even if they do not lead to the original outcome.

# Chapter 12: The thing with feathers

The oldest story about hope that I know is also one of the strangest.

The Greek poet Hesiod, writing around seven hundred years before the common era, tells how the god Zeus, angry with humankind, created the first woman, Pandora, and sent her to earth with a large storage jar. (Centuries later, a translator turned the jar into a "box," and the box is how most of us know the story.) Inside the jar were all the evils that would trouble human life: sickness, toil, sorrow and the rest. Before then, people had lived without them.

Pandora lifted the lid. The evils flew out and scattered across the world, where they have been ever since. In terror, she slammed the lid back down. And one thing was left inside, caught under the rim of the jar, unable to escape.

It was hope.

For nearly three thousand years, people have argued about what this means. Why was hope in a jar full of evils? Was it trapped inside so that it would stay with us, the one consolation we kept? Or was it kept from us? Or was hope itself one of the evils, the cruelest of them all?

The German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche chose the darkest reading. Zeus, he wrote, did not want human beings to throw their lives away, however much the other evils tormented them. He wanted them to go on letting themselves be tormented. So he gave them hope, which is in truth the worst of all evils, because it drags out human suffering.

When I first read that, I wanted to argue with it. Then I thought of the people I know who spent years waiting for something that was never going to come, and I was less sure.

* * *

Some seventeen years before Nietzsche wrote those words, in a small town in Massachusetts, a woman who rarely left her family's house wrote a poem that gives the opposite answer. Emily Dickinson published almost nothing in her lifetime, and her poems were only gathered and printed after her death. This is one of them:

*"Hope" is the thing with feathers —*\
*That perches in the soul —*\
*And sings the tune without the words —*\
*And never stops — at all —*

The poem goes on to say that this small bird keeps singing through the worst storms, in the coldest land and on the strangest sea. And then it ends with two lines that I find almost unbearably moving:

*Yet — never — in Extremity,*\
*It asked a crumb — of Me.*

For Dickinson, hope is not a torment. It is a gift that asks for nothing. It keeps singing whether or not the singing changes anything. It is what keeps a person company in the dark.

So who is right, Nietzsche or Dickinson? Is hope the last evil in the jar, or the bird that sings in the storm?

I have come to believe they are both right, because they are describing two different things that share a name.

* * *

In the 1980s and 1990s, a psychologist at the University of Kansas named Charles Richard Snyder set out to study hope scientifically. Until then, most psychologists had treated hope as a vague, warm feeling, something like optimism. Snyder argued that real hope is something more specific, and more practical.

Hope, in his account, has two parts.

The first he called *agency*. It is the sense that you can get yourself moving toward a goal and keep going. Snyder sometimes called it *willpower*. It is the inner voice that says, *I can do this. I will keep at it.*

The second he called *pathways*. It is the ability to see routes to your goal, and, most important, to find new routes when the first one is blocked. Snyder sometimes called it *waypower*. It is the inner voice that says, *Here is how. And if that doesn't work, here is another way.*

Snyder and his colleagues developed a short questionnaire to measure these two parts, and over the following decades, researchers used it in hundreds of studies. People who scored higher on hope tended to set clearer goals, to come up with more ways to reach them and to keep going longer when they met obstacles. Higher hope has been linked, in many of these studies, with better results in school and sport and with better coping in illness.

What I find most useful about Snyder's idea is the second part. High-hope people are not simply people who feel sure things will work out. They are people who can see more than one road. When one closes, they do not conclude that the destination is lost. They start looking for another way.

> *Give your hope more than one road to travel.*

* * *

The psychologist Gabriele Oettingen, working in Germany and later in New York, discovered something about hope that surprised even her, and that I think every person who dreams about something should know.

In one of her studies, published with Doris Mayer in 2002, she asked university students who were about to graduate how often they fantasized about getting their dream job, and how positive those fantasies were. Then she followed them for two years.

You might expect that the students with the most vivid and positive fantasies would do best. They were the most hopeful, weren't they? The opposite happened. The graduates who had indulged most in positive fantasies about their future had, two years later, received fewer job offers and were earning lower salaries than those whose thoughts about the future had been more mixed. Oettingen found similar patterns elsewhere: in people hoping to recover well from surgery, in students with a crush hoping to start a relationship.

Her explanation is that a vivid positive fantasy lets the mind taste the reward in advance. It feels wonderful. And because it feels as though we have already arrived, it drains away some of the energy we need to actually make the trip. Fantasy is a rehearsal of the destination. It is not a rehearsal of the road.

But Oettingen did not conclude that we should stop dreaming. She found that the dream becomes powerful when it is joined to a clear look at the obstacle. She called this *mental contrasting*: first imagine the wish and the best outcome, and then imagine, just as vividly, the main thing inside you or around you that stands in the way. When people did this, those who had a reasonable chance of success became more energized and more likely to act. She later combined it with a simple if-then plan, *if this obstacle appears, then I will do that*, into a method she calls WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan.

I should be honest about the size of the effect. A review of the research published in 2021 found that combining mental contrasting with if-then plans helps people reach their goals on average, but the effect is small to moderate, and some of the published studies may have overstated it. It is a useful tool, not a magic one. But the underlying insight seems sound to me, and it matches what I have seen in life. The people who dream best are not the people who dream most. They are the people who let their dream look straight at the obstacle.

> *Fantasy rehearses the arrival. Hope rehearses the road.*

* * *

Now we can see what Nietzsche and Dickinson were each describing.

Nietzsche's hope is the hope that becomes a substitute for action and a refusal to look at reality. It is the fantasy that keeps a person waiting at a door that has closed, rehearsing a reunion that will not happen, putting off every other possibility because *this* one must surely come true. That kind of hope really can prolong suffering. It is a bird that sings so sweetly that you never leave the cage.

Dickinson's hope is something else. It does not demand a particular outcome. It does not ask a crumb. It is closer to a basic orientation of the soul toward life, a refusal to let the darkness have the last word, even when you do not know what morning will bring.

And Snyder's hope, the hope of agency and pathways, is the practical bridge between them. It keeps the bird singing, but it also gets you walking.

* * *

There is one more voice I want to bring into this conversation, because I think it reaches the deepest place.

Václav Havel was a Czech playwright who, for years, opposed the communist government of his country. He was harassed, watched and imprisoned. His plays were banned. During those years, he had every reason to believe that nothing he did would ever change anything; the regime looked permanent. In a series of conversations recorded in the mid-1980s and later published as *Disturbing the Peace*, he was asked whether he had hope.

His answer is one of the most quoted things ever said on the subject:

*Hope is not the conviction that something will turn out well, but the certainty that something makes sense, regardless of how it turns out.*

A few years after he said this, in 1989, the regime fell, almost without bloodshed, and Havel became the president of his country. But notice that his hope did not depend on that. He was not hoping because he predicted the fall of the government. He was hoping because he believed that living truthfully *made sense*, whether the government fell or not.

This is hope that cannot be broken by the outcome, because it was never staked on the outcome. It is staked on meaning. And it is, I think, the kind of hope the archer needs most. She hopes to hit the target. Of course she does. But underneath that hope is a deeper one: that drawing the bow well is worth doing, that the practice and the aim and the courage to release make sense, whether this particular arrow lands or not.

> *Hope is not a prediction. It is a direction.*

* * *

Let me pull these threads together, because I think we now have a working picture of healthy hope.

Healthy hope has a *direction*: something you value, something worth moving toward. That is Havel's hope, and Dickinson's bird.

Healthy hope has *roads*: concrete next steps and more than one route. That is Snyder's pathways.

Healthy hope looks at the *obstacle*: it does not pretend the road is clear. That is Oettingen's contrast.

And healthy hope does not set a single outcome as the price of its own survival. It is the bird that asks for no crumb.

Unhealthy hope tends to be the reverse: a single fixed outcome, no roads but one, no look at the obstacles, and a mind that tastes the arrival so often that it forgets to walk. That is the hope Nietzsche feared, and he was right to fear it.

* * *

When Sana was preparing for her interview, she sometimes let herself imagine her first day at the company: the lanyard, the call to her father. That was natural, and it gave her pleasure, and I would not take it from her. But the parts of her preparation that actually changed her odds were not the fantasies. They were the moments when she looked straight at the obstacle. *I freeze when they ask about failures. So I will practice that answer until I don't.* That was hope rehearsing the road.

After the rejection, when she was ready, her hope had to change shape. It could no longer be a hope for that particular glass building. But when she asked herself what that job had stood for in her imagination, she found three things underneath it: the security of a good salary, the respect of doing serious work and the chance to learn quickly from talented people. None of those lived only inside one building.

Her hope, in other words, had a direction that survived the loss of one road. That is the kind of hope that does not get trapped in the jar.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Fantasy rehearses the arrival. Hope rehearses the road.*

*Give your hope more than one road to travel.*

*Hope becomes practical when it can name a next step.*

*Hope is not a prediction. It is a direction.*

**Practice.** Write down one thing you are hoping for. Underneath it, write the main obstacle, inside you or around you, as honestly as you can. Then write one if-then plan: "If this obstacle appears, then I will..." Finally, write one other road that could lead to what this hope is really about.

# Introduction: Four words I could not put down {.unnumbered}

For most of my adult life I have been carrying on an argument with four words.

Hard work. Luck. Destiny. Hope.

I could add a fifth, because for many of us hope has an older and more serious form, and its name is prayer. Hope is what you feel at night about tomorrow. Prayer is what you say about it, out loud or in silence, to whoever you believe is listening.

They are ordinary words. Children learn them early. We use them at weddings and funerals, in job interviews and hospital corridors, on the night before an exam and the morning after a rejection. Parents hand them to their children like heirlooms. Work hard. Pray. Don't lose hope. What is written is written.

And yet, inside my own head, these four words have never once agreed with each other.

I noticed the first thing so many times that I eventually stopped calling it coincidence. When I worked hard, my luck seemed to work hard too. When I prepared properly for something, the right question came up. When I put in the hours, the right person happened to walk past at the right moment. I am not talking about miracles. I am talking about the small, suspicious kindnesses of timing that seem to arrive more often for people who are already moving. You have probably noticed it as well. There is an old line that says the harder you work, the luckier you get, and it survives because so many people have felt it to be true.

Then I noticed the second thing, and it did not fit the first at all.

I watched people do everything right and lose.

I have seen people prepare for years for a single job, study the company, rehearse every answer, pray before the interview and walk out knowing they had given their best, only to receive a short, polite email a week later. I have seen people love someone with their whole heart, build a future with that person in their imagination brick by brick, pray for it every night, and then watch that future go to somebody else. These were not lazy people. They were not careless people. They did not lack faith or hope. They had more of both than most.

There is a song by Linkin Park called "In the End" that a whole generation once shouted along to in their bedrooms and their cars. Its chorus is a confession: all that trying, all that distance covered, and at the end none of it seemed to count. It is easy to dismiss a rock song. But I have noticed that people tend to quote that chorus at exactly the moment when they have stopped believing in something they once believed in completely. It is the sound of a promise breaking.

So the questions began, and they would not leave.

If our destiny is already written, and we will receive what we were always going to receive, then what is the point of working so hard? What is the point of praying? Why build hopes at all, if the ending is already in the book?

But if we receive what we work for, if effort really does decide the outcome, then what is destiny for? Why do some people work themselves to the bone and get nothing, while others stroll into the very thing they barely wanted?

And if luck is the real master here, if it is all a roll of the dice, then why does it seem to favor people who prepare?

I would think my way to one side of the argument, and then life would push me back to the other. I would decide that effort was everything, and then I would watch a good man fail. I would decide that everything was written, and then I would watch someone's sheer stubbornness change the shape of their life. I would decide that hope was foolish, and then I would meet someone whose hope was the only reason they were still standing.

Maybe you know this feeling. Maybe it is why you picked up this book.

* * *

What I slowly understood is that these four words are more than ideas. They are pillars. People build their whole lives on one or another of them, often without realizing they have chosen.

Some people stand on the pillar of hard work. Their creed is effort, discipline, early mornings, no excuses. They are admirable, and they get a great deal done, and they are often merciless with themselves when the results do not come, because in their system a bad result can only mean one thing: you did not work hard enough.

Some people stand on the pillar of luck. They have seen enough of the world's unfairness to stop believing in the neat story. They are often wise about how much is out of our hands, and they are often tempted to stop reaching for anything at all.

Some people stand on the pillar of destiny. What is meant for you will find you; what is not was never yours. This belief can give enormous peace. It can also, if held in the wrong way, become a beautiful excuse for not trying, or a cage that keeps someone waiting for years at a door that has already closed.

And some people stand on the pillar of hope and prayer. They have learned that the future is not finished, and they lean toward it with their whole hearts. They are often the bravest people in the room. They are also the ones who are hurt the most when the answer is no.

Each of these pillars holds up part of the truth. The trouble starts when we try to live on only one of them. A life built on effort alone turns cruel when effort fails. A life built on luck alone turns passive. A life built on destiny alone can turn into waiting. A life built on hope alone can turn into fantasy.

This book is my attempt to get all four words to sit at the same table and talk to each other. I want to know how they fit together, because I am convinced they do.

* * *

Long before any of us were born, philosophers in Athens and Rome were stuck on the same questions. The Stoics, a school of thinkers who spent centuries arguing about fate and freedom, left behind an image that I have come to love more than almost any other. The Roman statesman Cicero records it in a book called *On Ends*.

Imagine an archer, they said. A good archer does everything in her power to hit the target. She trains for years. She chooses her bow and her arrows with care. She studies the distance, sets her feet, steadies her breathing, draws, aims and releases. All of that belongs to her.

But once the arrow leaves the string, it does not belong to her anymore. A gust of wind can push it aside. The target can move. The bowstring may have been frayed in a place she could not see. Hitting the target is something she wants, something she has every right to want, and something she has worked for with her whole body. Yet the Stoics said that hitting the mark is "to be chosen, not to be desired." She aims for it with everything she has. She does not stake her worth on it.

When I first met this image, I felt something in my chest unclench. Because it answers the question that had tormented me, or at least it begins to.

The archer does not stop drawing the bow because of the wind. And the wind does not stop blowing because the archer trained hard. Both are real. Both shape where the arrow lands. Neither cancels the other.

So this book is organized around the archer and the forces that act on the arrow.

**The bow** is your effort. It is the training, the preparation, the work you do with your own hands. It is the one part of the story that is yours to shape.

**The wind** is luck: everything that pushes on your life that you did not choose and cannot control. Your birthplace, your parents, the economy, the mood of the person reading your application, the stranger who happens to sit next to you on a train.

**The written page** is destiny. Maybe you believe the arrow's path was always known, inscribed somewhere before you drew the bow. Maybe you believe destiny is only the story we tell afterward, when we look back and see a path that seemed inevitable. Either way, the page is the place where the arrow lands, and the meaning we give it.

**The breath** is hope, and for many of us, prayer. Every archer knows the moment just before release, when you breathe in, hold still and send something silent toward the target. It does not move the arrow by force. But no archer who has ever lived has skipped it.

And then there is **the archer**: you. The one who has to hold all four of these at once and still decide, every morning, whether to pick up the bow again.

* * *

I should tell you plainly what this book will not do.

It will not promise you that hard work always wins. It does not, and you already know it does not, and any book that says otherwise is selling you something. It will not tell you that every loss is secretly a gift, or that everything happens for a reason you will understand one day. Some losses stay losses. You can build a good life after them without pretending to be grateful for them.

It will not settle the great theological questions about destiny. I am not a scholar of any religion, and this is not a book of theology. People of many faiths, and of no faith, will read these pages, and I want each of them to find room here. Where I mention the wisdom of a particular tradition, it is because the story is too good and too true to leave out, not because I am trying to persuade you of a creed.

What this book will try to do is this. It will give you a way to think about effort, luck, destiny and hope so that they stop fighting inside you. It will show you what the research actually says about why luck seems to love the hardworking, why the wrong people sometimes win, and why some kinds of hope keep people alive while other kinds quietly kill them. It will give you words for disappointment, which is one of the loneliest experiences there is, partly because we have so few good words for it. And it will help you decide what to do next, when you have tried hard, hoped deeply and still lost: whether to draw again at the same target, or to turn and aim somewhere new.

You will meet some people along the way. A few of them are composites: invented names and lives that I have built from the many stories I have watched unfold, so that no real person's private pain is put on display. When someone is a composite, I will tell you. Everyone else, the scientists, the soldiers, the philosophers, the prisoners, the poets, the farmer with the runaway horse, either really lived or comes from a story that people have been telling for centuries. You will find my sources in the Notes at the back of the book.

At the end of each chapter there is a short section called *For your quiver*. An archer's quiver holds the arrows she will need later. Mine holds a few lines worth remembering and one small practice to try. Take what is useful. Leave the rest.

* * *

I wrote this book for a particular person. Perhaps it is you.

You have worked hard for something. You have wanted it in a way that kept you awake. Perhaps you prayed for it. Perhaps you still do. And something went the other way: the job, the exam, the visa, the marriage, the business, the recovery, the child, the second chance. Now you are standing in the field with an empty bow, looking at the place where your arrow should have landed, and wondering whether you were a fool to aim at all.

You were not.

But I do not want to just tell you that. I want to show you why, carefully and honestly, with evidence and with stories, until you can see it for yourself.

Let's begin with a promise that most of us believed long before we knew we believed it.

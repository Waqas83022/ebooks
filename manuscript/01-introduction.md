# Introduction: Four words I could not put down {.unnumbered}

Luck has a strange habit. It tends to show up just after you have prepared for it.

I have noticed this in my own life more times than I can count. When I work hard, my luck seems to work hard too. Perhaps you have felt it. You study properly for once, and the questions you practiced are the ones on the paper. You finally finish the piece of work you have been sweating over, and that same week the one person who could open a door happens to see it. You walk into a room ready, and the chance that appears is one you are able to take. It never happens on schedule and it never happens every time. But it happens often enough that you stop calling it coincidence. Somewhere deep down, you begin to believe that effort and good fortune are secretly on the same side.

And then you meet someone like Sara.

Sara is a composite, built from many people I have known and many more I have heard about, but you will recognize her. For two years she wanted one job. She read everything the company had published. She taught herself the software they used. She practiced her answers in front of the bathroom mirror until her younger brother banged on the door. On the morning of the final interview, her mother prayed for her, and Sara prayed too, harder than she had prayed in years. The interview went well. She knew it went well.

Nine days later, a two-line email told her they had chosen someone else.

She read it standing in her kitchen. Within the hour, her mind had turned the company's decision into a verdict on her life. *You were never good enough. All that work was for nothing. There was no point in hoping, and no point in praying either.*

Sara is not unusual. She is everywhere.

A student studies for a full year for a medical entrance exam and misses the cut-off by two marks. A farmer does everything right, and the rain does not come. A man puts his savings into a small shop on a busy road, and six months later a new bypass takes the traffic somewhere else. A couple try for a child for five years. An athlete trains through every winter of her teens and tears a ligament in the week of the national trials. A family waits four years for a visa and is refused in a single paragraph. Someone loves another person with their whole heart and watches that person build a life with somebody else.

None of these people were lazy. Many of them prayed. All of them hoped.

* * *

So I was left holding two truths that refused to sit together.

The first: effort seems to attract luck.

The second: effort guarantees nothing.

Out of the gap between them came questions that followed me for years.

If our destiny is already written, and we will receive exactly what we were always going to receive, then why work so hard? Why pray? Why build hopes at all, if the ending is already on the page?

But if we receive what we work for, if effort really decides the outcome, then what is destiny for? Why do some people work themselves to the bone and get nothing, while others stroll into the very thing they barely wanted?

And if luck is the real master, if it is all a roll of the dice, why does it seem to favor the people who prepare?

There is a song by Linkin Park called "In the End" that a whole generation once shouted along to in bedrooms and cars. Its chorus is a confession that all the trying, all the distance covered, came to nothing in the end. I have noticed that people reach for that chorus at a very particular moment: the moment they stop believing in something they once believed in completely. It is the sound of a promise breaking.

This book is my attempt to answer those questions honestly, and to keep the promise from breaking you.

* * *

These are not abstract questions. People build entire lives on their answers, usually without noticing that they have chosen.

Some stand on the pillar of hard work. Discipline, early mornings, no excuses. They get a great deal done. They are also merciless with themselves when the results do not come, because in their system a bad result can only mean one thing: you did not work hard enough.

Some stand on the pillar of luck. They have seen enough unfairness to stop believing the neat story. They are often wise about how much is out of our hands, and often tempted to stop reaching for anything at all.

Some stand on the pillar of destiny. What is meant for you will find you. This belief can bring deep peace. Held the wrong way, it can also become a beautiful excuse for not trying, or a waiting room where people sit for years in front of a door that has already closed.

And some stand on the pillar of hope. They lean toward the future with their whole hearts. They are often the bravest people in the room. They are also the ones who bleed the most when the answer is no.

Every one of these pillars holds up part of the truth. The trouble starts when we try to live on only one of them. Effort alone turns cruel when it fails. Luck alone turns passive. Destiny alone turns into waiting. Hope alone turns into fantasy.

The question is not which pillar is true. The question is how they stand together.

* * *

The answer I eventually found began with a very old picture.

More than two thousand years ago, a school of philosophers called the Stoics argued for centuries about fate, freedom and what a good life requires. The Roman statesman Cicero preserved one of their images in a book called *On Ends*, and it has stayed with me more than almost anything else I have read.

Think of an archer, they said. A good archer does everything in her power to hit the target. She trains for years. She chooses her bow and arrows with care. She reads the distance, sets her feet, steadies her breathing, draws and releases. All of that belongs to her.

But once the arrow leaves the string, it is no longer hers. A gust of wind can push it aside. The target can shift. The string may have been frayed in a place she could not see. The Stoics said that hitting the mark is something a good archer aims at with everything she has, and something she must not stake her worth on. Her aim is hers. The landing is not.

The first time I understood that image, something in my chest unclenched. The archer does not stop drawing the bow because of the wind. The wind does not stop blowing because the archer trained hard. Both are real. Both shape where the arrow lands. Neither cancels the other.

So this book is built around the archer and the forces that act on her arrow.

*The bow* is your effort: the training, the preparation, the work you do with your own hands. It is the one part of the story that is yours to shape.

*The wind* is luck: everything that pushes on your life that you did not choose and cannot control. Where you were born, the economy, the mood of the person reading your application, the stranger who happens to sit beside you on a train.

*The written page* is destiny. Perhaps you believe the arrow's path was known before you drew the bow. Perhaps you believe destiny is only the story we tell afterward, when we look back and see a path that seemed inevitable. Either way, the page is where the arrow lands, and the meaning we give to where it lands.

*The breath* is hope. Every archer knows the moment just before release, when you breathe in and hold still. It is the moment of hope before you take an action whose result you cannot know. For many people, that moment has a name, and the name is prayer. For others, it is a silent *please*, or simply the decision to let go and see.

I will say more about hope than you might expect, because it is the force we talk about most loosely and need most deeply. Hope is not the pleasant feeling that things will work out. It is the reason the archer picks up the bow at all. Without it, there is no draw, no aim and no arrow. Some kinds of hope keep people alive through years of waiting. Other kinds break their hearts. Learning the difference may be the most important thing in this book.

And then there is *the archer*: you. The one who has to hold all four at once and still decide, every morning, whether to pick up the bow again.

* * *

Let me be plain about what this book will not do.

It will not promise you that hard work always wins. It does not, you already know it does not, and any book that says otherwise is selling you something. It will not tell you that every loss is secretly a gift. Some losses stay losses, and you can build a good life after them without pretending to be grateful.

It will not settle the great religious questions about destiny either. People of many faiths, and of none, will read these pages, and I want every one of them to find room here. I will not tell you what is written. I will show you why, whatever is written, your effort still counts.

Here is what it will do. It will give you a way to think about effort, luck, destiny and hope so they stop fighting inside you. It will show you what the research actually says about why luck seems to love the hardworking, why the best candidate often loses, why the past always looks inevitable, and why some hope heals while some hope harms. It will give you words for disappointment, which is one of the loneliest experiences there is, partly because we have so few good words for it. And it will help you decide what to do next, when you have tried hard, hoped deeply and still lost: draw again at the same target, change the way you shoot, or turn and aim somewhere new.

You will meet a handful of people along the way: Sara, Daniel, Omar, Maya, Adam and Lina. They are composites, invented names wrapped around real patterns, so that no real person's private pain is put on display. Everyone else, the scientists, the pilots, the prisoners, the philosophers, the farmer with the runaway horse, either really lived or comes from a story that people have been telling for centuries. You will find my sources in the Notes at the back.

At the end of each chapter there is a short section called *For your quiver*. An archer's quiver holds the arrows she will need later. Mine holds one line worth remembering and one small practice to try.

* * *

Before we begin, here is the whole book in five questions. I call it the archer's card. You can use it today, on whatever you are facing.

*The bow.* What is mine to do next?

*The wind.* What is not mine to control?

*The page.* What story am I telling myself about this?

*The breath.* What am I hoping for, and what action will I take without knowing how it ends?

*The archer.* Who am I, apart from this result?

Each part of the book goes deep into one of these questions. In Chapter 17 they become a full practice you can use for any goal, decision or disappointment. For now, just keep them close. Ask them the next time a result goes against you, before your mind starts writing its verdict.

I wrote this book for a particular person. Perhaps it is you. You worked hard for something. You wanted it in a way that kept you awake. Perhaps you prayed for it, and perhaps you still do. And something went the other way. Now you are standing in a field with an empty bow, looking at the place where your arrow should have landed, and wondering whether you were a fool to aim at all.

You were not. But I do not just want to tell you that. I want to show you, carefully and honestly, until you can see it for yourself.

Let's begin with a promise most of us believed long before we knew we believed it.

# Chapter 4: Why luck loves the hardworking

I told you at the beginning of this book about the pattern I noticed so often that I stopped calling it coincidence: when I worked hard, my luck seemed to work hard too.

It turns out that I am in very large company. One of the most repeated sayings in the English language is some version of *I'm a great believer in luck, and I find the harder I work, the more I have of it.* You will see it printed on office walls and posted under photographs of sunrise runs. It is usually credited to Thomas Jefferson.

Jefferson never said it. The researchers at Monticello, his former home, have looked carefully and found no evidence that he said or wrote anything like it. The quotation detectives who trace these things have found earlier versions in American publications of the 1920s, and a later, punchier version, *the harder I practice, the luckier I get*, in the mouths of professional golfers around 1960. Nobody knows who said it first. It seems to have been said by many people, independently, because so many people had felt it to be true.

I love that history, because it is a small lesson in luck all by itself. A saying about how luck rewards effort was, in the end, carried into fame by a lucky accident: somebody attached it to a famous name, and the name made it travel. The anonymous people who actually worked the idea out got no credit at all.

But the history does not answer the real question. Is the pattern true? And if it is, *why*? Luck is supposed to be random. Why would something random favor people who work?

* * *

In September 1928, a Scottish scientist named Alexander Fleming came back to his laboratory in London after a summer holiday. Before leaving, he had stacked a pile of glass dishes in which he had been growing bacteria, and now he began sorting through them. On one dish, he noticed a patch of mold. That in itself was unremarkable; contaminated dishes were a daily nuisance, and many people would have thrown it straight into the cleaning tub.

But Fleming noticed something else. Around the mold, there was a clear ring where the bacteria had died.

That dish led to penicillin, and penicillin went on to save an unknowable number of lives. It is one of the most famous strokes of luck in the history of science. The mold spore that drifted onto that dish, probably from another laboratory in the same building, was pure chance. The timing of the holiday and the weather that summer, which allowed the mold and bacteria to grow in just the right way, was chance.

But here is the part of the story that is not luck. For years before that morning, Fleming had been hunting for substances that could kill bacteria. Years earlier he had discovered an antibacterial substance in human tears and mucus, which taught him exactly what such an effect might look like on a glass dish. When the accident arrived, it arrived in front of perhaps one of the few people in London whose mind had been shaped, by years of work, to recognize what it meant.

Louis Pasteur had put this into words seventy-four years earlier, in a lecture at the University of Lille: *In the fields of observation, chance favors only the prepared mind.*

Notice what Pasteur did not say. He did not say that preparation creates chance. The mold would have landed on that dish whether or not Fleming was prepared. What preparation did was turn an accident into an opportunity. Without it, the same dish would have been washed and forgotten.

> *Preparation cannot create the accident. It decides whether the accident becomes an opportunity.*

* * *

In the 1990s, a British psychologist named Richard Wiseman decided to study luck the way a scientist studies anything else. He placed advertisements asking for people who considered themselves exceptionally lucky or exceptionally unlucky, and over the years he interviewed and tested hundreds of them.

One of his experiments has become famous, and I think it is one of the most revealing small experiments in all of psychology.

Wiseman gave each volunteer a newspaper and asked them to count the number of photographs inside. That was the whole task. On average, the people who described themselves as unlucky took about two minutes. The people who described themselves as lucky took a few seconds.

The reason was printed on the second page. There, in type more than two inches high, taking up half the page, was a message: *Stop counting. There are 43 photographs in this newspaper.*

It was staring everyone in the face. The unlucky people, Wiseman reported, tended to miss it. The lucky people tended to see it.

Then, just for fun, he placed a second large message halfway through the newspaper: *Stop counting, tell the experimenter you have seen this and win $250.* Again, the unlucky people tended to miss it. They were too busy counting photographs.

When Wiseman looked more closely at the differences between his two groups, he found that the "unlucky" people tended to be more tense and anxious. Anxiety narrows attention. It makes us focus hard on the task in front of us, which sounds like a virtue, but it also makes us blind to anything we were not expecting. The "lucky" people tended to be more relaxed and more open. They also tended to meet more people, to talk to strangers, to vary their routines. In other words, they put themselves in front of more chances, and they were more likely to notice a chance when it appeared.

Wiseman's conclusion was that lucky people are not, for the most part, people to whom more random good things happen. They are people who are better at noticing and creating chance opportunities, and better at making something of the bad ones.

I would add one gentle note of caution. Wiseman's volunteers were people who *described* themselves as lucky or unlucky, and some of the difference between them may be about how they tell the story of their lives, not just how their lives went. But the newspaper is hard to argue with. Two groups of people looked at the same pages. Only one group saw the message.

* * *

So why does luck seem to love people who work hard? Having spent a long time with this question, I think there are four reasons, and none of them requires magic.

The first is simply *exposure*. The wind can only carry arrows that have been released. A person who sends out ten applications has ten chances for something unexpected to happen. A person who sends out one has one. A writer who finishes five stories has five lottery tickets; a writer who is still polishing the first page of the first story has none. Hard work, in its most basic form, puts more of your life into contact with chance. More contact means more good accidents, along with more bad ones.

A software developer named Jason Roberts once described this as your *luck surface area*. The more you do, and the more people you tell about what you are doing, the larger the surface on which luck can land. It is a wonderfully practical idea. Lucky breaks rarely find people who are hidden.

The second reason is *readiness*. Opportunities come to many people. Only some of them can use what arrives. The young musician who has practiced every day for three years can step in when the band's guitarist falls ill on the night of a big show. Her friend who has been meaning to practice cannot. Both of them got the same phone call. Only one of them had something to say yes with.

The third reason is *visibility*. When you work seriously at something, other people notice. They remember you. They mention you to someone else. A teacher recommends you. A former colleague forwards your name. A customer tells a friend. Much of what we call luck arrives through other people, and other people can only bring opportunities to someone they know is looking.

The fourth reason is *attention*, the newspaper effect. When you are deeply engaged in a field, your mind keeps scanning, without being asked, for anything relevant to it. You notice the article, the overheard conversation, the strange result on a glass dish. Someone who is not engaged walks straight past the same signs, because they mean nothing to them.

Put these four together, and the pattern that started this book makes sense. Hard work does not bend the laws of chance. It widens the space in which opportunity can find you, and it makes you ready to recognize and use what arrives.

> *Work does not summon luck. It builds a place for luck to land.*

* * *

Now, the honest part. Because if I only told you this half of the story, I would be repeating the contract from Chapter 1 in a cleverer form.

The first honest point is that our memories are not neutral. When a lucky break arrives after a season of hard work, it makes a great story, and we remember it. It confirms something we want to believe about ourselves and about the world. But we tend to forget the lucky breaks that arrived when we had done nothing to deserve them, because those make a less satisfying story. And we tend to forget, or at least to stop telling, all the seasons of hard work that produced no luck at all. When you look back and feel that your hard work always brought good luck, some of that feeling may be produced by which memories you have kept.

The second honest point is that we are very bad at judging how much control we have. In 1975, the psychologist Ellen Langer ran a simple experiment with lottery tickets in an office. Some employees were allowed to choose their own ticket. Others were simply handed one. Later, when asked how much money they would want to sell their ticket, the people who had chosen their own asked for roughly four times as much. The odds were identical. Choosing just made the ticket feel more like theirs, and more likely to win. Langer called this the *illusion of control*. It is part of why we feel, after a lucky break, that our effort must have caused it.

The third honest point is that luck often has a human face. When we look back on our good fortune, we often tell it as a story about our own preparation meeting a lucky moment. We forget the person who mentioned our name, the teacher who stayed late, the stranger who held the door, the parent who paid for the course. These people were not luck. They were generosity. Leaving them out of the story makes it more flattering and less true.

> *Luck often has a human face. Remember to thank it.*

And the fourth honest point is the one that brings us back to the people I mentioned at the start of this book: the ones who prepared perfectly and lost. Exposure, readiness, visibility and attention all improve your odds. None of them can decide what arrives. A prepared musician may never get that phone call. A brilliant scientist may spend a whole career at the bench and never see the clear ring around the mold. The lucky people in Wiseman's study still had bad days. Work can widen the space where opportunity finds you. It cannot choose what walks through the door.

* * *

There is a way of holding all this that I have found steadying.

Instead of thinking of luck as a reward that hard work earns, think of it as weather. You cannot control the weather. But you can decide whether you are standing outside when it is good. You can make sure your field is plowed when the rain comes. You can learn to read the sky well enough to know when to plant. And when a storm comes that no preparation could have prevented, you can know that the storm was not a verdict on your farming.

This way of thinking keeps both parts of the truth. Your effort is real, and it really does tilt the odds; that is why you noticed the pattern in the first place. And the weather is real, and it does not owe you anything; that is why the pattern has exceptions that break people's hearts.

* * *

If you want to see this in your own life, there is a simple experiment you can run, and I recommend it.

For two weeks, keep a small notebook, or a note on your phone. Each evening, write down four things. What you did that day toward the things that matter to you. Any unexpected good thing that happened, however small. Any unexpected bad thing. And any help you received from another person.

Be honest. Write down the bad surprises as carefully as the good ones. Write down the lucky breaks that came when you had done nothing to earn them.

At the end of two weeks, read it all at once. Most people find two things. They find that more of their good luck than they expected came through other people. And they find that the days when they were active, when they were reaching out, trying things, showing their work, contained more surprises of every kind, good and bad, than the days when they stayed inside their routines.

That second finding is the whole chapter in miniature. Effort does not buy good luck. It buys more weather. And over a lifetime, a person who stands in more weather, with a plowed field and an attentive eye, tends to catch more rain.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Work does not summon luck. It builds a place for luck to land.*

*The wind can only carry arrows that have been released.*

*Preparation cannot create the accident. It decides whether the accident becomes an opportunity.*

*Luck often has a human face. Remember to thank it.*

**Practice.** Keep a luck log for two weeks. Each evening, note what you did toward what matters, one unexpected good thing, one unexpected bad thing and any help you received. Record disappointments as carefully as surprises. At the end, read it all at once and notice where your luck came from.

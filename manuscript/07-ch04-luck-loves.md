# Chapter 4: Why luck loves the hardworking

I began this book with a pattern I noticed so often that I stopped calling it coincidence: when I work hard, my luck seems to work hard too.

I am in very large company. One of the most repeated sayings in the English language is some version of the idea that the harder you work, the luckier you get. You will find it on office walls and under photographs of sunrise runs. It is usually credited to Thomas Jefferson.

Jefferson never said it. The researchers at Monticello, his former home, have looked carefully and found no evidence that he said or wrote anything like it. People who trace the history of famous sayings have found earlier versions in American publications of the 1920s, and a punchier version in the mouths of professional golfers around 1960. Nobody knows who said it first. It seems to have been discovered by many people independently, because so many people felt it to be true.

I love that history, because it is a small lesson in luck all by itself. A saying about how luck rewards effort was carried into fame by a lucky accident: somebody attached it to a famous name, and the name made it travel. The unknown people who actually worked the idea out got no credit at all.

But the history does not answer the real question. Is the pattern true? And if it is, *why*? Luck is supposed to be random. Why would something random favor the people who work?

* * *

In September 1928, a Scottish scientist named Alexander Fleming came back to his laboratory in London after a summer holiday. Before leaving, he had stacked a pile of glass dishes in which he had been growing bacteria, and now he began sorting through them. On one dish he noticed a patch of mold. That was nothing special; contaminated dishes were a daily nuisance, and most people would have thrown it straight into the washing tub.

But Fleming noticed something else. Around the mold there was a clear ring where the bacteria had died.

That dish led to penicillin, and penicillin went on to save more lives than anyone can count. It is one of the most famous strokes of luck in the history of science. The mold spore that drifted onto that dish, probably from another laboratory in the same building, was pure chance. So was the summer weather that let the mold and the bacteria grow in exactly the right way.

Here is the part that was not luck. For years before that morning, Fleming had been hunting for substances that could kill bacteria. Years earlier, he had discovered an antibacterial substance in human tears and mucus, which taught him exactly what such an effect might look like on a glass dish. When the accident arrived, it arrived in front of one of the very few people in London whose mind had been shaped, by years of work, to understand what it meant.

Seventy-four years earlier, the French scientist Louis Pasteur had told an audience in the city of Lille that, in the world of observation, chance helps only the minds that are ready for it. Notice what Pasteur did not claim. He did not say preparation creates chance. The mold would have landed on that dish whether or not Fleming was prepared. What preparation did was turn an accident into an opportunity. Without it, the same dish would have been washed and forgotten.

Preparation cannot create the accident. It decides whether the accident becomes an opportunity.

* * *

In the 1990s, a British psychologist named Richard Wiseman decided to study luck the way a scientist studies anything else. He placed advertisements asking for people who considered themselves exceptionally lucky or exceptionally unlucky, and over the following years he interviewed and tested hundreds of them.

One of his experiments is among the most revealing small experiments I know.

Wiseman handed each volunteer a newspaper and asked them to count the photographs inside. That was the whole task. On average, the people who described themselves as unlucky took about two minutes. The people who described themselves as lucky took a few seconds.

The reason was printed on the second page. Taking up half the page, in letters more than two inches high, was a message telling the reader to stop counting, because the newspaper contained 43 photographs. It was staring everyone in the face. The unlucky people tended to miss it. The lucky people tended to see it.

Then, just for fun, Wiseman placed a second large message halfway through the paper. It told readers to stop counting, tell the experimenter they had seen the message, and win $250. Again, the unlucky people tended to miss it. They were too busy counting photographs.

When Wiseman looked more closely at his two groups, he found that the "unlucky" volunteers tended to be more tense and anxious. Anxiety narrows attention. It makes us focus hard on the task in front of us, which sounds like a virtue, but it also blinds us to anything we were not expecting. The "lucky" volunteers tended to be more relaxed and open. They also tended to meet more people, to talk to strangers, to vary their routines. They put themselves in front of more chances, and they were more likely to notice a chance when it appeared.

His conclusion was that lucky people are not, for the most part, people to whom more random good things happen. They are people who are better at noticing and creating chance opportunities, and better at making something of the bad ones.

One gentle note of caution. Wiseman's volunteers *described* themselves as lucky or unlucky, and some of the difference between them may be about how they tell the story of their lives, not only about how their lives went. But the newspaper is hard to argue with. Two groups of people looked at the same pages. Only one group saw the message.

* * *

There is one more piece of research that explains where much of our luck comes from.

In the early 1970s, a young sociologist named Mark Granovetter interviewed people in a Boston suburb who had recently changed jobs. He asked how they had found their new positions. Many had found them through personal contacts, which nobody found surprising. What surprised everyone was *which* contacts. More often than not, the useful contact was not a close friend or family member. It was an acquaintance: someone they saw only occasionally or rarely. A former colleague. A friend of a friend. Someone from a club they had stopped going to.

Granovetter called this *the strength of weak ties*. Your close friends mostly know what you know and meet whom you meet. Your acquaintances live in other circles, hear about other openings and can carry your name into rooms you will never enter. Decades later, in 2022, researchers working with data from millions of LinkedIn users ran a large experiment and found a similar pattern: moderately weak connections were the ones most likely to help people move to new jobs.

This matters for anyone who works hard in silence. A great deal of what we call luck travels through people, and especially through people at the edge of our lives. They can only carry what they know about.

* * *

So why does luck seem to love the people who work hard? After a long time with this question, I believe there are four reasons, and none of them requires magic.

The first is *exposure*. The wind can only carry arrows that have been released. A person who sends out ten applications has ten chances for something unexpected to happen. A person who sends out one has one. A writer who finishes five stories has five tickets in the draw; a writer still polishing the first page of the first story has none. Hard work, in its most basic form, puts more of your life into contact with chance. More contact means more good accidents, along with more bad ones.

A software developer named Jason Roberts once described this as your *luck surface area*: the more you do, and the more people you tell about what you are doing, the larger the surface on which luck can land. Lucky breaks rarely find people who are hidden.

The second is *readiness*. Opportunities come to many people. Only some can use what arrives. The musician who has practiced every day for three years can step in when the band's guitarist falls ill on the night of a big show. Her friend who has been meaning to practice cannot. Both received the same phone call. Only one had something to say yes with.

The third is *visibility*. When you work seriously at something, other people notice. They remember you. They mention you to someone else. A teacher recommends you. A former colleague forwards your name. A customer tells a friend. Much of what we call luck arrives through other people, and other people can only bring opportunities to someone they know is looking.

The fourth is *attention*, the newspaper effect. When you are deeply engaged in a field, your mind keeps scanning, without being asked, for anything relevant to it. You notice the article, the overheard conversation, the strange result on a glass dish. Someone who is not engaged walks straight past the same signs, because they mean nothing to them.

Put these four together and the pattern that began this book makes sense. Hard work does not bend the laws of chance. It widens the space in which opportunity can find you, and it makes you ready to recognize and use what arrives. Work does not summon luck. It builds a place for luck to land.

* * *

Now the honest part, because if I told you only this half of the story I would be repeating the old contract in a cleverer coat.

First, our memories are not neutral. When a lucky break arrives after a season of hard work, it makes a great story, and we keep it. It confirms something we want to believe about ourselves. We tend to forget the lucky breaks that came when we had done nothing to deserve them, because they make a less satisfying story. And we tend to stop telling the stories of the long seasons of hard work that produced no luck at all.

Second, we are poor judges of how much control we have. In 1975, the psychologist Ellen Langer ran a simple experiment with lottery tickets in an office. Some employees chose their own ticket. Others were handed one. Later, when asked how much they would want to sell their ticket, the people who had chosen their own asked for roughly four times as much. The odds were identical. Choosing simply made the ticket feel more theirs, and more likely to win. Langer called this the *illusion of control*. It is part of why we feel, after a lucky break, that our effort must have caused it.

Third, luck often has a human face. When we look back on our good fortune, we tend to tell it as a story of our own preparation meeting a lucky moment. We forget the person who mentioned our name, the teacher who stayed late, the stranger who held the door, the parent who paid for the course. Those people were not luck. They were generosity. Leaving them out makes the story more flattering and less true.

Fourth, and this brings us back to the people at the start of the book: exposure, readiness, visibility and attention all improve your odds. None of them can choose what arrives. A prepared musician may never get that phone call. A brilliant scientist may spend a whole career at the bench and never see the clear ring around the mold. The lucky people in Wiseman's study still had bad days. Work can widen the space where opportunity finds you. It cannot decide what walks through the door.

* * *

There is a way of holding all of this that I have found steadying.

Instead of thinking of luck as a reward that hard work earns, think of it as weather. You cannot control the weather. But you can decide whether you are standing outside when it is good. You can make sure your field is plowed before the rain. You can learn to read the sky well enough to know when to plant. And when a storm comes that no preparation could have prevented, you can know that the storm was not a verdict on your farming.

This way of thinking keeps both halves of the truth. Your effort is real, and it really does tilt the odds; that is why you noticed the pattern in the first place. And the weather is real, and owes you nothing; that is why the pattern has exceptions that break people's hearts.

Effort does not buy good luck. It buys more weather. And over a lifetime, a person who stands in more weather, with a plowed field and open eyes, tends to catch more rain.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Work does not summon luck. It builds a place for luck to land.*

**Practice.** Keep a luck log for two weeks. Each evening, write one line about what you did toward what matters, one unexpected good thing, one unexpected bad thing and any help you received. Record disappointments as carefully as surprises. At the end, read it all in one sitting and notice where your luck actually came from, and how much of it came through people.

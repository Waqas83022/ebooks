# Chapter 5: The planes that came back

During the Second World War, American and British bombers flew over Europe every day, and every day many of them did not return. Armor could save them, but armor is heavy. A plane covered in it would be too slow to fly its mission and too thirsty to make it home. So the question was where to put a limited amount of protection.

The military did the sensible thing. It looked at the planes that came back and recorded where they had been hit. The bullet holes clustered in certain places, along the wings and the body of the aircraft. The obvious conclusion was to reinforce those areas. Put the armor where the holes are.

In New York, a small group of mathematicians was working on problems for the military at Columbia University. One of them was Abraham Wald, a Hungarian-born statistician who had fled Europe before the war. Wald saw what everyone else had missed.

These were the planes that *came back*.

The holes showed where a plane could be hit and still make it home. The places where the returning planes had few holes, around the engines for example, were not places that rarely got hit. They were places where a hit tended to bring the plane down, so those planes were never in the hangar to be counted. The armor, Wald argued, should go where the holes were not.

Historians have since pointed out that Wald's real work was more technical than the legend; he wrote a series of mathematical memos about how to estimate a plane's weak points from the damage on the survivors. But the heart of the legend is true, and it has become the most famous example of what statisticians call *survivorship bias*: drawing conclusions from the cases that survived some process, while forgetting the ones that did not.

The planes that never returned wrote nothing in the report. And once you understand that, you start to notice the missing planes everywhere.

* * *

A business magazine profiles the founder who dropped out of university and became a billionaire. It does not profile the many people who dropped out, started companies and went bankrupt. The interview with the actor who moved to the city with nothing and refused to give up does not include the actors who moved to the city with nothing, refused to give up, and are still waiting tables. The successful investor explains his strategy on television. Nobody invites the hundreds who used the same strategy and lost their savings.

These stories are not lies. The founder really did drop out; the actor really did persist. The problem is that the stories arrive without their missing half, so we draw the wrong lesson. We conclude that dropping out, persisting or following that strategy *causes* success, when we have no idea how often it leads to failure.

Think of one of the best-loved success stories of our time. J. K. Rowling's first Harry Potter manuscript was turned down by around a dozen publishers before a small London house took a chance on it. The story is told and retold to young writers as proof that you should never give up.

It is a beautiful story, and I would not take it away from anyone. But Wald would ask a simple question. How many manuscripts were rejected twelve times, then twelve more, then fifty more? Their authors did not give up either. They kept sending the pages out, year after year. We do not hear their stories. They are the planes that did not come back.

Persistence may well be necessary for most kinds of success. That is very different from saying it is enough. The returning planes can teach us the first. They cannot teach us the second. Before you copy a winner, count the people who did the same thing and lost.

* * *

There is a more unsettling question underneath all this. Suppose we could see every failure as well as every success. Would the best work always win?

In 2006, the sociologists Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds and Duncan Watts tried to find out by building an artificial world. They created a website where more than fourteen thousand people could listen to and download songs by unknown bands. There were forty-eight songs, and none of the participants had heard any of them before.

Some participants were placed in an "independent" world, where they saw only the names of the bands and songs and made up their own minds. The rest were divided into eight separate worlds, each of which showed participants how many times each song had already been downloaded by others in that world. All eight worlds began identically. Each was a small parallel universe in which the same songs, heard by similar people, competed for attention.

The results were striking. In the worlds where people could see what others were choosing, the popular songs became far more popular and the unpopular ones far less, compared with the independent world. Success became more unequal. It also became much harder to predict. The same song could become a hit in one world and sit in the middle of the pack in another. Early downloads, which were essentially random, snowballed. A small lead at the beginning tended to grow into a large lead at the end.

Quality was not irrelevant. The songs that did best in the independent world rarely did terribly in the social worlds, and the songs that did worst rarely did brilliantly. But across a wide middle range, which song became a hit depended heavily on what happened to be downloaded first.

I want to be fair to this study. It was one experiment, in one artificial music market, two decades ago. It cannot tell us the exact role of chance in any particular career. But it is a serious challenge to the comforting belief that the best work always rises to the top. In a world where people watch each other, and they always do, early luck can be amplified until it looks like destiny.

* * *

The economist Robert Frank, whom we met in Chapter 3, added another piece with a simple computer simulation.

Picture a competition with thousands of entrants. Each entrant has a score for skill and effort, and a much smaller score for luck. Even when luck counts for only a tiny share of the final result, Frank found, the winner of a large contest is usually *not* the most skilled entrant.

The reason is simple once you see it. When thousands of people compete, the best of them are extremely close together in ability. There is very little daylight between the top hundred. So the small amount of luck, the good day, the favorable judge, the timing, becomes the tie-breaker. It does not take much wind to decide a contest between archers who are all excellent.

This has a strange consequence. The bigger the prize and the fiercer the competition, the more likely it is that the winner was both very good *and* very lucky. The many people who were equally good but less lucky are invisible. And the winner, looking back, will quite naturally explain the victory in terms of skill and effort, because they really were skilled and they really did work hard. Everything they say will be true. It will simply be missing the wind.

* * *

None of this is meant to make you cynical about success, or to take anything away from people who have achieved great things. Most of them earned their place among the finalists through years of work. The point is to see clearly what their stories can and cannot teach.

When you hear a success story, it helps to ask a few quiet questions, the way Wald did in the hangar.

*How many people did the same thing and failed?* You will usually not know the number. Simply asking the question loosens the story's grip.

*What advantages did this person have that the story leaves out?* Money, connections, health, timing, a particular city, a particular decade.

*What would have happened with a slightly different roll of the dice?* If the first investor had said no. If the song had been downloaded fourth instead of first. If the plane had been hit two feet to the left.

*Does the person telling the story acknowledge luck?* In my experience, the ones who do are often the most trustworthy guides.

These questions do not make success stories useless. They make them honest. A survivor can still teach you a great deal about how to fly. They just cannot promise that their way of flying brings everyone home.

* * *

There is one more hangar where the returning planes are counted, and most of us walk through it every day.

After her rejection, Sara did what so many of us do when we are hurting. She opened her phone and scrolled. And there she found plane after plane that had made it home. A school friend announcing a job at a famous company. A cousin's engagement photographs. A former colleague's promotion. An acquaintance's new house.

What Sara could not see were the planes that did not return. The friend's forty rejections before this one. The cousin's two broken engagements. The colleague turned down for the same promotion twice. The acquaintance's debt. People rarely post their storms, only their landings. So the screen gives you a picture of a world in which everyone else's arrows hit the target and only yours fall into the grass.

That picture is false. Not because the posts are false, but because the hangar is missing its other half.

So when other people's success makes your own failure feel sharper, remember Wald. Look at the holes. Admire the planes that made it home; many of them flew through terrible weather. Then ask, gently, where the others are. You will not find them. But knowing they exist is enough to stop the screen from lying to you about your own life.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Before you copy a winner, count the people who did the same thing and lost.*

**Practice.** Choose one success story that has shaped how you think, about a famous person or someone you know. Write down three things that are missing from it: people who did the same thing and failed, advantages the story does not mention, and moments where a different roll of the dice would have changed the ending. Then ask what lesson is still standing.

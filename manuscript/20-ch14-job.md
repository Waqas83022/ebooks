# Chapter 14: The job that did not come

Three weeks after the email, Sana applied for two more positions. One sent a rejection within forty-eight hours, an automated message that did not even use her name. The other never replied at all.

There is a particular loneliness to looking for work. It happens mostly in silence, at a screen, late at night. The rejections arrive one by one, and each one is small enough that you feel you should not be so upset about it, and yet they pile up inside you like stones in a pocket. Other people do not see them. Often you do not tell anyone, because you are embarrassed, and because there is nothing anyone can do.

And in many families, a job is never only a job. It is money, of course. But it is also status, security, the answer to the question relatives ask at every gathering. It may be tied to marriage prospects, to a parent's pride, to the sacrifices that were made to pay for your education. When Sana was rejected, she did not only feel that she had failed. She felt, without anyone saying it, that she had let her family down.

If you are in a season like this, I want this chapter to sit beside you for a while. Not to cheer you up, but to help you think clearly at a time when clear thinking is hard.

* * *

Let me start with some good news, and it is real news, from careful research.

In 1998, the psychologists Daniel Gilbert, Timothy Wilson and their colleagues published a set of studies about how people predict their own feelings after bad events. They looked at a range of disappointments: the end of a romantic relationship, seeing a favored candidate lose an election, receiving negative feedback about one's personality. One of their studies involved young professors waiting to hear whether they would be given tenure, the permanent position that most academics spend years working toward. Another involved people turned down for a job they wanted.

Again and again, the same pattern appeared. People predicted that the bad event would make them miserable, and that the misery would last a long time. When the bad event actually happened, they felt bad, but less bad than they had predicted, and for less time. The professors who were denied tenure, for example, had predicted that they would be unhappy for years. Some years later, they were reporting happiness levels not far from those of colleagues who had been promoted.

Gilbert and Wilson's explanation is that we all have what they call a *psychological immune system*: a set of mental processes that help us recover from pain, find new meaning, notice new possibilities and make our peace with what happened. Like the body's immune system, it does its work in the background. And because we rarely see it working, we forget it is there. So when we imagine a future disappointment, we imagine the pain without imagining the recovery.

This does not mean that rejection does not hurt. It does. The first days can be very hard. But it does mean that the voice in your head saying *I will never get over this* is, on the evidence, probably wrong.

> *You are usually stronger than your forecast.*

* * *

The second thing I want to say is about what a rejection actually tells you.

When you are turned down for a job, your mind immediately wants a reason. Not knowing is almost unbearable. So the mind supplies one, and the reason it supplies is usually the most painful available: *I wasn't good enough.*

But think about all the reasons a person might be rejected for a particular job.

There may have been a real gap in your skills or experience. Your application may have been weaker than it could have been, even if you are well qualified. There may have been stronger candidates; or equally strong ones, and someone had to be chosen. There may have been an internal candidate the company always intended to hire. The budget may have changed, or the role may have been redesigned. The interviewer may have had a bad day, or a personal preference, or a bias, conscious or not. There may have been a simple matter of timing; another candidate interviewed first, and set the standard. And there are almost always reasons you will never learn.

Out of all these possibilities, only the first two are really about you, and even those are about your application and your current skills, not your worth as a person.

So when the mind supplies its verdict, try supplying the whole list instead. Not to excuse yourself, and not to blame the company, but to restore the truth. A single rejection is a single closed door. It is not a map of the building.

> *A rejection tells you that one door closed. It does not map every door you can reach.*

* * *

Now, there is real value in finding out which of those reasons applied, when you can. This is where effort comes back in, in its most useful form.

Some companies will give feedback if you ask. Many will not, often for legal reasons, and some will not reply at all. But it costs little to ask, politely and specifically. A good question is a narrow one: *Is there one thing that would have made my application stronger?* People find it much easier to answer one specific question than a vague request for "feedback."

You can also seek feedback from other places. A friend who works in the industry. A former manager. A career adviser. Someone who has hired people for similar roles. Show them your application. Practice an interview with them, and ask them to be honest, even unkind.

When feedback comes, it will sometimes sting. Let it. Feedback is information about your approach. It is not information about your value. The danger in a long job search is that these two things get confused, so that every rejection lowers not your estimate of your application, but your estimate of yourself.

> *Let feedback change your approach before rejection changes your estimate of your worth.*

And sometimes, after you have asked everyone you can, you will still not know why. That is the hardest outcome, and also a very common one. In that case, I would suggest a rule that has helped me: *do not invent an explanation for what remains unknown.* Improve what you can see. Let the rest stay a question mark. A question mark is much lighter to carry than a false verdict.

* * *

What about the job search itself? Here, the lessons of the earlier chapters become practical.

Remember the woodcutter in Chapter 2. If you have sent out fifty applications in the same form and received nothing, the next step is not fifty more of the same. Stop, sharpen the axe, change something and then go again.

Remember the luck surface area from Chapter 4. Many jobs are filled, in part, through people: someone who knows someone, who mentioned a name, who forwarded a message. Applying through websites is necessary, but it is often the least lucky route. Talking to people in the field, showing your work in public, telling others what you are looking for, all of these widen the space where luck can find you.

Remember the drowned sailors of Chapter 5. When you see other people announcing their new jobs, you are seeing their votive tablets. You are not seeing their forty rejections.

Remember the four boxes of Chapter 7. A rejection is an outcome. It might come from a bad decision or a good one. Examine your process, not just the result.

And remember Stockdale. Put your dates on your actions, not on the outcome. *I will send five well-made applications this week. I will ask two people for feedback. I will review my approach at the end of the month.* Those are promises you can keep. *I will have a job by March* is not.

* * *

There is one more thing that a long job search can teach, and it is the thing that changed Sana's story.

About two months after the first rejection, Sana sat down one evening and asked herself the question from Chapter 12. What had that famous company actually *stood for* in her imagination? What was she really hoping for, underneath the glass building and the lanyard?

She wrote down three things. Security: a salary that would let her help her family and stop worrying. Respect: doing serious, skilled work that people valued. And growth: learning quickly, from people who were better than her.

Then she noticed something. None of those three things lived only in that one company. In fact, she began to suspect that at a famous firm, a junior analyst might wait years before she was trusted with anything serious, while at a smaller firm she might be given real responsibility almost immediately.

So she widened her aim. She kept applying to large companies, but she also began looking at smaller ones that did serious work in her field. She asked one of the two former employees she had met over coffee, months earlier, whether she knew anyone at such firms. The woman did. A conversation led to an introduction, and an introduction led to an interview.

I am not going to tell you yet how it turned out, because that belongs to the last chapter. And I want to be clear: widening her aim was not a guarantee. It could have led to more rejections, and for a while it did. What it gave her was more roads for the same hope to travel.

* * *

I want to end this chapter with a word about fairness, because not every job search is simply a matter of skill and luck.

Some people face barriers in hiring that have nothing to do with their ability: their name, their gender, their religion, their accent, their age, their disability, the neighborhood they come from. We saw evidence of this in Chapter 3. If you suspect that this is part of what you are facing, two things can be true at once.

It can be true that you should keep improving your applications, building your skills and widening your search, because those are things within your power.

And it can also be true that the barrier is wrong, that it is not your fault and that it is not only your problem to solve. You can seek out employers with better records. You can find organizations that support people in your position. You can, when you are in a position to do so, help change the rules for the next person. Effort that goes into making a system fairer is still effort. It is some of the most important effort there is.

* * *

If you are reading this in the middle of a season of rejection, let me say plainly what I would say if I were sitting next to you.

Your pain makes sense. You wanted something good, and you worked for it, and it did not come. You are allowed to grieve that.

But the email, or the silence, is not allowed to speak for your whole life. It is one result, from one place, at one time, for reasons you may never know. You are probably stronger than you feel today. There is probably more room around you than you can see from where you are standing. And there are certainly doors you have not yet knocked on.

Sharpen the axe. Ask for feedback. Widen your aim. Rest when you need to. And then draw the bow again, not because the next arrow is guaranteed to land, but because the archer who keeps drawing is the only one who ever hits anything at all.

# Chapter 14: The door that did not open

Rejection comes in many envelopes.

A university writes to say it received many outstanding applications this year. A publisher returns the manuscript with a printed note. An investor listens to the whole pitch, nods warmly and never replies. A casting director says *thank you, next* before you reach the second verse. A scholarship committee announces its winners, and your name is not among them. An embassy stamps a refusal in a passport. A company sends an automated message that does not even use your name.

Each of these is a different door. But the moment they close feels the same. A small, hard silence. Then the mind starts talking.

Three weeks after her first rejection, Sara applied for two more positions. One sent a refusal within forty-eight hours. The other never answered at all. There is a particular loneliness to this kind of season. It happens mostly in silence, at a screen, late at night. Each rejection is small enough that you feel you should not be so upset about it, and yet they pile up inside you like stones in a pocket. Other people do not see them. Often you do not tell anyone, because you are embarrassed, and because there is nothing anyone can do.

In many families, these doors carry more than one person's hopes. A university place, a job, a visa can be tied to money, status, marriage prospects, a parent's pride and sacrifices made years ago so that you could stand in front of that door. When it stays shut, people do not only feel that they failed. They feel, without anyone saying it, that they let everyone down.

If you are in a season like this, I want this chapter to sit beside you for a while. Not to cheer you up, but to help you think clearly at a time when clear thinking is hard.

* * *

Let me start with some good news, and it is real news from careful research.

In 1998, the psychologists Daniel Gilbert, Timothy Wilson and their colleagues published a set of studies on how well people predict their own feelings after bad events. They looked at a range of disappointments: the end of a romantic relationship, seeing a favored candidate lose an election, receiving negative feedback about one's personality, young professors waiting to hear whether they would receive tenure, the permanent post most academics spend years working toward, and people being turned down for a job they wanted.

The same pattern appeared again and again. People predicted that the bad event would make them miserable, and that the misery would last a long time. When the event actually happened, they felt bad, but less bad than they had predicted, and for less time. The professors who were denied tenure had expected to be unhappy for years. Some years later, they were reporting happiness not far from that of colleagues who had been promoted.

Gilbert and Wilson's explanation is that we all have a *psychological immune system*: a set of mental processes that help us recover from pain, find new meaning, notice new possibilities and make peace with what happened. Like the body's immune system, it works in the background. And because we rarely see it working, we forget it is there. So when we imagine a future disappointment, we imagine the pain and forget to imagine the recovery.

This does not mean rejection does not hurt. It does, and the first days can be very hard. But it does mean that the voice saying *I will never get over this* is, on the evidence, probably wrong. You are usually stronger than your forecast.

* * *

The second thing I want to say is about what a rejection actually tells you.

When a door closes, the mind wants a reason. Not knowing is almost unbearable, so the mind supplies one, and it usually supplies the most painful one available: *I wasn't good enough.*

But think of all the reasons a door might stay shut.

There may have been a real gap in your skills or experience. Your application may have been weaker than it could have been, even though you are well qualified. There may have been stronger candidates, or equally strong ones, and someone had to be chosen. There may have been an internal candidate the organization always meant to pick. The budget may have changed. The program may have been full before it opened. The person deciding may have had a bad day, a private preference or a bias, conscious or not. There may have been a quota, a policy, a deadline you did not know about. And there are almost always reasons you will never learn.

Of all these possibilities, only the first two are really about you, and even those are about your application and your current skills, not your worth as a person.

So when the mind supplies its verdict, supply the whole list instead. Not to excuse yourself, and not to blame the people on the other side, but to restore the truth. A rejection is a closed door, not a map of the building.

* * *

There is real value in finding out which reasons applied, when you can. This is where effort comes back in, in its most useful form.

Some organizations will give feedback if you ask. Many will not, often for legal reasons, and some will not reply at all. But it costs little to ask, politely and specifically. A good question is a narrow one: *Is there one thing that would have made my application stronger?* People find it much easier to answer one clear question than a vague request for feedback.

You can also find feedback elsewhere. A friend in the field. A former teacher or manager. Someone who has made these decisions themselves. Show them your application, your portfolio, your pitch. Rehearse with them, and ask them to be honest, even unkind.

When feedback comes, it will sometimes sting. Let it. Feedback is information about your approach. It is not information about your value. The danger in a long season of rejection is that the two get confused, so that each closed door lowers not your estimate of your application, but your estimate of yourself. Let feedback change your approach before rejection changes your opinion of your worth.

And sometimes, after you have asked everyone you can, you still will not know why. That is the hardest outcome, and a very common one. Here is a rule that has helped me: *do not invent an explanation for what remains unknown.* Improve what you can see. Let the rest stay a question mark. A question mark is far lighter to carry than a false verdict.

* * *

What about the search itself? Here the lessons of earlier chapters become practical.

Remember the woodcutter. If you have sent out fifty applications in the same form and received nothing, the next step is not fifty more of the same. Stop, sharpen the axe, change something and go again.

Remember the weak ties. Many opportunities travel through people, and especially through acquaintances. Applying through websites is necessary, but it is often the least lucky route. Talking to people in your field, showing your work where it can be seen, telling others what you are looking for, all of this widens the space where luck can reach you.

Remember the planes that came back. When you see other people announcing their new jobs, places and contracts, you are seeing the planes that made it home. You are not seeing their forty rejections.

Remember the four boxes. A rejection is an outcome. It could follow a good decision or a bad one. Examine your process, not just your result.

And remember Stockdale. Put your dates on your actions, not on the outcome. *I will send five strong applications this week. I will ask two people for feedback. I will review my approach at the end of the month.* Those are promises you can keep. *I will be accepted by March* is not.

* * *

There is one more thing that a long season of rejection can teach.

In Chapter 12, Sara wrote three words above her desk: security, respect, growth. Now she had to act on them. She began to suspect that at a famous company, a junior analyst might wait years before being trusted with anything serious, while at a smaller firm she might be given real responsibility almost at once. So she widened her aim. She kept applying to large companies, but she also began looking at smaller ones doing serious work in her field.

She messaged one of the two former employees she had met over coffee months earlier, someone she barely knew, and asked whether she knew anyone at such firms. She did. A conversation led to an introduction. The introduction led to an interview.

I will not tell you yet how it turned out, because that belongs to the last chapter. Widening her aim was not a guarantee; for a while it led to more rejections. What it gave her was more roads for the same hope to travel.

The same move is available far beyond job searches. The student refused by one university can ask what that university stood for: a subject, a city, a kind of teacher, a door into a career. Then she can look for other places that offer the same things. The writer turned down by twenty publishers can ask what being published meant: readers, respect, income, a voice. Some of those can be reached by other doors that are open now.

* * *

Not every closed door is simply a matter of skill and luck, and I want to say that plainly.

Some people face barriers that have nothing to do with their ability: their name, gender, religion, accent, age, disability, nationality or the neighborhood they come from. We saw evidence of this in Chapter 3. If you suspect this is part of what you face, two things can be true at once.

It can be true that you should keep improving your applications, building your skills and widening your search, because those things are in your power.

And it can be true that the barrier is wrong, that it is not your fault and that it is not only your problem to solve. You can seek out organizations with better records. You can find groups that support people in your position. When you are in a position to do so, you can help change the rules for the next person. Effort spent making a system fairer is still effort. It may be the most important kind there is.

* * *

If you are reading this in the middle of a season of closed doors, here is what I would say if I were sitting beside you.

Your pain makes sense. You wanted something good, you worked for it and it did not come. You are allowed to grieve.

But the letter, or the silence, does not get to speak for your whole life. It is one result, from one place, at one time, for reasons you may never know. You are probably stronger than you feel today. There is probably more room around you than you can see from where you stand. And there are certainly doors you have not yet knocked on.

Sharpen the axe. Ask for feedback. Widen your aim. Rest when you need to. Then draw the bow again, not because the next arrow is guaranteed to land, but because the archer who keeps drawing is the only one who ever hits anything at all.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*A rejection is a closed door, not a map of the building.*

**Practice.** Write a one-page plan for the next month. Include one specific improvement to your application, portfolio or pitch, one person you will ask for feedback, a manageable number of attempts you will make each week and a date to review your approach. Then write what the door you lost really stood for, and one other door that leads toward the same thing.

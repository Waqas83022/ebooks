# Chapter 17: The archer's discipline

In Japan, there is a form of archery called *kyudo*, "the way of the bow." It is practiced in quiet halls, with long asymmetrical bows of bamboo, and it has been taught for centuries as much as a discipline of the mind as a sport.

Students of kyudo learn the shot as a sequence of eight stages. The archer places her feet. She settles her body. She readies the bow. She raises it. She draws. She holds at full draw. She releases. And then comes the eighth stage, which is the one I want you to remember.

It is called *zanshin*, which can be translated as "the remaining mind" or "the continuing spirit." After the arrow has left the string, the archer does not drop her arms or turn away. She holds her posture, her attention and her composure, for a moment longer. The shot is not over when the arrow leaves. It is over when the archer has finished being present to it, whatever the arrow does.

Kyudo teachers have a saying: *a true shot is a hit*. They mean that if every stage of the shot is done rightly, the arrow tends to find its mark. But the practice does not focus on the target. It focuses on the shooting. The target is where the attention goes last, not first.

I have come to think that this is the closest thing I know to a picture of how to live with effort, luck, destiny and hope all at once. Everything that comes before the release is yours to do with all your heart. The release is a letting go. And what comes after, *zanshin*, is the part most of us were never taught: how to remain yourself, whole and attentive, while the arrow flies and lands wherever it lands.

This chapter is about turning the ideas in this book into a practice: something you can actually do, on an ordinary Tuesday, when you are facing something you want and cannot guarantee.

* * *

When I try to boil everything in this book down, I am left with four questions, one for each of the forces acting on the arrow, and a fifth for the archer herself. They are worth asking whenever you face a goal that matters, a decision you cannot be sure of or a disappointment you do not know what to do with.

*The bow: What is mine to do?*

This is the question of effort. What is the skill that actually decides this? What is the next concrete action? Where can I get feedback? What can I do this week that would change the odds, even a little? This question deserves your full energy. It is the part of the story that belongs to you.

*The wind: What is not mine?*

This is the question of luck and circumstance. What forces are acting on this that I did not choose and cannot control? Other people's decisions, the economy, timing, health, the ground I started from. Naming these does two things. It helps you prepare for them, and widen your luck surface where you can. And it stops you from blaming yourself for things that were never in your hands.

*The page: What story am I telling?*

This is the question of destiny and meaning. When I think about this situation, what story is running in my head? Is it a verdict, *I am not good enough, it was never meant for me, nothing ever works out*? Is it a fantasy, *this is destined, it will surely come*? Am I using destiny to make peace with the past, or to excuse myself from the future? Can I add the words *so far*?

*The breath: What am I hoping for, and what is my next step?*

This is the question of hope and prayer. What direction is this hope pointing in, underneath the specific outcome? What is the value I am really reaching for? What is the main obstacle? What is one if-then plan? Is there more than one road? And if you pray, this is the place for it: to name what you want, to mark the edge of your power and to ask for the strength to do your part.

*The archer: Who am I, apart from this result?*

This is the fifth question, and it is the one that holds the others together. If this arrow misses, what will still be true about me? What will I still care about, still be able to do, still be worth? What will remain of my life that this result cannot touch?

> *The next useful step can be clear even while the final outcome remains uncertain.*

* * *

Let me show you how differently these questions can play out, depending on the target.

When Sana asked them about her job search, the answers looked like this.

*What is mine to do?* A great deal. She could improve her interview answers with honest practice, ask for feedback, widen her search to smaller firms and reach out to the contacts she already had. The bow, in her case, was large.

*What is not mine?* The hiring decisions themselves, the number of positions available, the other candidates, the internal politics of companies she would never see, the economy. Also, she realized, the fact that she could not afford an unpaid internship, which had shaped her CV.

*What story am I telling?* She noticed she had been telling herself a verdict: *I'm not good enough for the places that matter.* She rewrote it: *So far, I have not got the job I wanted. I got to the final round of a very competitive process, and I do not know why I was not chosen.*

*What am I hoping for, and what is my next step?* Underneath the famous company, she was hoping for security, respect and growth. The main obstacle was her nerves in final interviews. Her if-then plan: *if I feel myself freezing, then I will pause, breathe and tell a short story about a real piece of work.* Her next step: three conversations with people at smaller firms by the end of the month.

*Who am I, apart from this result?* A person who works seriously, learns fast, loves her family and is good at what she does, whether or not a particular company recognizes it.

Now look at how the same questions played out for Yusuf, about Amna.

*What is mine to do?* Much less. He could not make her love him. He could be honest about his own feelings, which he had been. He could respect her decision. He could look after himself. He could choose what to do with his evenings, his attention, his social media, his grief.

*What is not mine?* Almost everything about the outcome. Her heart, her choices, her life. The bow, in his case, was very small, and the target was an archer.

*What story am I telling?* *She was written for me, so my life is ruined.* He came, slowly, to a different one: *I loved someone who did not choose me. That is one of the hardest things a person can live through. It does not mean I will never be loved.*

*What am I hoping for, and what is my next step?* For a long time, he was hoping for her to return. That hope had a single road, and it was closed. When he was ready, he found what lay underneath: companionship, being known, building a family with someone who chose him back. His next step was very small: to stop checking her profile, and to say yes the next time his friends invited him out.

*Who am I, apart from this result?* A man who is capable of loving faithfully and deeply. That capacity did not leave with her.

Notice how different the two cases are. In Sana's, the questions led to a list of actions. In Yusuf's, they led mostly to acceptance and to a very small first step. Both are right. The point of the questions is not always to produce more effort. It is to put effort exactly where it belongs, and nowhere else.

* * *

Some people like to have something they can write on. If you are one of them, here is a single page you can use for any goal or decision. I call it the archer's card.

At the top, write the *goal*: what you are aiming for, in one sentence.

Underneath, write the *value*: why it matters, what it is really about.

Then write *my next action*, with a date. Just one. The smallest real step.

Then *what I do not control*. Name the wind.

Then *the main obstacle*, and *if it appears, I will...*

Then *the help I need*, and from whom.

Then *another road*: one other way the same value could be served, if this one closes.

Then *if this fails, I will...* This one feels uncomfortable to write. Write it anyway.

> *A plan becomes more honest when it includes what you will do if it fails.*

And finally, a *review date*, when you will look at this card again and ask whether to continue, change, pause or leave.

The card takes ten minutes to fill in, and it does two things at once. It makes you more serious about the effort, because the next action has a date. And it makes you less afraid of the outcome, because the failure already has a plan.

* * *

If you would like to practice these ideas more deliberately, here is a simple month you can try. It is not a program and it does not promise any particular result. It is just a way of living with these questions for four weeks, so that they become habits rather than ideas.

In the *first week*, choose one goal that matters to you and fill in an archer's card for it. Spend the week noticing where your energy goes. How much of it goes to the bow, the things that are yours to do? How much goes to worrying about the wind?

In the *second week*, sharpen one method. Find one source of honest feedback on how you are pursuing your goal, and change one thing in response to it. Think of the woodcutter.

In the *third week*, widen your luck surface. Tell three people what you are working on. Show your work somewhere it can be seen. Keep the luck log from Chapter 4: each evening, a line about what you did, what surprised you, good and bad, and who helped.

In the *fourth week*, review. Read your card and your log. Choose one change to keep, one thing to stop and one uncertainty to carry forward without forcing an answer.

Throughout the month, include rest. Include setbacks. Include days where nothing happens. Write down the bad surprises as carefully as the good ones. And above all, do not turn this into another test that you can pass or fail. The point of keeping small records is not to score yourself. It is to see your life more accurately than the large, dramatic stories that disappointment tells.

> *Small records can correct the large stories disappointment tells us.*

> *Measure what helps you act more wisely, and leave the rest of your life room to breathe.*

* * *

Let me return, at the end, to the eighth stage of the kyudo shot: *zanshin*, the remaining mind.

Most of us have been taught a great deal about the first seven stages, even if we never thought of them that way. We have been taught to prepare, to work, to aim, to try. Some of us have been taught to pray. What almost none of us were taught is what to do in the moment after the release, when the arrow is gone and we are waiting, or when it has landed and it is not where we wanted.

Zanshin says: stay. Do not collapse. Do not turn away in shame or rush to the next arrow in panic. Hold your form. Watch with clear eyes where the arrow went. Feel whatever you feel. And remain yourself, an archer, whatever the target says.

Then, when you are ready, lower the bow, walk to the line and draw again.

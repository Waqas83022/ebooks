# Chapter 17: The archer's discipline

In Japan there is a form of archery called *kyudo*, "the way of the bow." It is practiced in quiet halls with long, asymmetrical bows of bamboo, and for centuries it has been taught as much as a training of the mind as a sport.

Students of kyudo learn each shot as a sequence of eight stages. The archer places her feet. She settles her body. She readies the bow. She raises it. She draws. She holds at full draw. She releases. And then comes the eighth stage, the one I want you to remember.

It is called *zanshin*, which can be translated as "the remaining mind." After the arrow has left the string, the archer does not drop her arms or turn away. She holds her posture, her attention and her composure for a moment longer. The shot is not over when the arrow leaves. It is over when the archer has finished being present to it, whatever the arrow does.

Kyudo teachers like to say that a shot made correctly is already a hit. They mean that when every stage is done well, the arrow tends to find its mark. But the practice does not fix its attention on the target. It fixes its attention on the shooting. The target is where the eyes go last, not first.

This is the closest thing I know to a picture of how to live with effort, luck, destiny and hope at once. Everything before the release is yours to do with your whole heart. The release itself, the moment of hope, is a letting go. And what comes after, *zanshin*, is the part most of us were never taught: how to stay whole and attentive while the arrow flies and lands wherever it lands.

This chapter turns the ideas in this book into a practice, something you can actually do on an ordinary Tuesday when you face something you want and cannot guarantee.

* * *

At the end of the introduction, I gave you the archer's card in five short questions. Here they are again, now with everything the book has added to them.

*The bow: What is mine to do?*

This is the question of effort. What skill actually decides this? What is the next concrete action? Where can I get honest feedback? What can I do this week that would change the odds, even a little? Is my pace one I can keep? This question deserves your full energy. It is the part of the story that belongs to you.

*The wind: What is not mine?*

This is the question of luck and circumstance. What forces act on this that I did not choose and cannot control? Other people's decisions, the economy, timing, health, the ground I started from. Naming them does two things. It helps you prepare for them and widen your luck surface where you can. And it stops you blaming yourself for things that were never in your hands.

*The page: What story am I telling?*

This is the question of destiny and meaning. When I think about this, what story is running in my head? Is it a verdict: *I'm not good enough, it was never meant for me, nothing ever works out*? Is it a fantasy: *this is destined, it will surely come*? Am I using destiny to make peace with the past, or to excuse myself from the future? Can I add the words *so far*?

*The breath: What am I hoping for, and what will I do without knowing how it ends?*

This is the question of hope, and it has grown the most since the introduction. Underneath the specific outcome, what is the value I am really reaching for? What is the main obstacle, and what is my if-then plan? What is one action I will take this week even though I cannot know how it ends? What other roads lead toward the same value? Is my hope tied to a date I do not control, and can I move that date onto my own actions instead? And if you pray, this is where prayer belongs: naming what you want, marking the edge of your power and asking for the strength to do your part.

*The archer: Who am I, apart from this result?*

This is the question that holds the others together. If this arrow misses, what will still be true about me? What will I still care about, still be able to do, still be worth? What part of my life can this result not touch?

The next useful step can be clear even while the final outcome stays uncertain. These questions exist to find it.

* * *

Here is how differently the questions can play out depending on the target.

When Sara asked them about her job search, the answers looked like this.

*What is mine to do?* A great deal. She could practice interviews with someone who would push back, ask for feedback, widen her search to smaller firms and use the contacts she already had. In her case, the bow was large.

*What is not mine?* The hiring decisions, the number of positions, the other candidates, internal politics she would never see, the economy. Also, she realized, the summer she could not afford to work for free.

*What story am I telling?* She noticed a verdict: *I'm not good enough for the places that matter.* She rewrote it: *So far, I haven't got the job I wanted. I reached the final round of a very competitive process, and I don't know why I wasn't chosen.*

*What am I hoping for, and what will I do without knowing how it ends?* Beneath the famous company, she was hoping for security, respect and growth. Her main obstacle was freezing in final interviews. Her if-then plan: *If I feel myself freezing, I will pause, breathe and tell a short story about a real piece of work.* Her uncertain action for the week: three conversations with people at smaller firms, any of which might lead nowhere.

*Who am I, apart from this result?* A person who works seriously, learns fast, loves her family and is good at what she does, whether or not one company noticed.

Now look at the same questions for Adam, about Lina.

*What is mine to do?* Much less. He could not make her love him. He could be honest about his feelings, which he had been. He could respect her decision. He could choose what to do with his evenings, his attention, his phone and his grief.

*What is not mine?* Almost everything about the outcome. Her heart, her choices, her life. His bow was small, and the target had a will of its own.

*What story am I telling?* For a long time it was *She was meant for me, so my life is ruined.* Slowly it became: *I loved someone who did not choose me. That is one of the hardest things a person can live through. It does not mean I will never be loved.*

*What am I hoping for, and what will I do without knowing how it ends?* For months he hoped only for her return, a hope with a single road, and that road was closed. When he was ready, he found what lay beneath it: companionship, being known, building a family with someone who chose him back. His uncertain action was the one we saw in Chapter 15: putting the phone face down, and messaging an old friend about Saturday. He had no idea whether it would help.

*Who am I, apart from this result?* A man capable of loving faithfully and deeply. That capacity did not leave with her.

Notice how different the two cases are. For Sara, the questions led to a list of actions. For Adam, they led mostly to acceptance and one very small step. Both are right. The point is not always to produce more effort. It is to put effort exactly where it belongs, and nowhere else.

* * *

Some people like something to write on. If you are one of them, here is a single page you can use for any goal or decision. This is the full archer's card.

At the top, write the *goal*: what you are aiming for, in one sentence.

Underneath, the *value*: why it matters, what it is really about.

Then *my next action*, with a date. Just one: the smallest real step.

Then *what I do not control*. Name the wind.

Then *the story I am telling*, and a truer version that begins with *so far*.

Then *the hope*: the value beneath the outcome, the main obstacle, and *if it appears, I will...*

Then *the help I need*, and from whom.

Then *other roads*: at least one other way the same value could be served if this road closes.

Then *if this fails, I will...* This line feels uncomfortable to write. Write it anyway. A plan becomes more honest when it includes what you will do if it fails.

And finally, a *review date*, when you will read the card again and decide whether to continue, change the method, pause or leave.

The card takes ten minutes, and it does two things at once. It makes you more serious about the effort, because the next action has a date. And it makes you less afraid of the outcome, because failure already has a plan and hope already has more than one road.

* * *

If you want to practice these ideas more deliberately, here is a simple month you can try. It is not a program, and it promises no particular result. It is just a way of living with these questions for four weeks, so they become habits rather than ideas.

In the *first week*, choose one goal that matters and fill in an archer's card for it. Spend the week noticing where your energy goes. How much goes to the bow, the things that are yours to do? How much goes to worrying about the wind?

In the *second week*, sharpen one method. Find one source of honest feedback and change one thing in response. Think of the woodcutter.

In the *third week*, widen your luck surface. Tell three people what you are working on, including at least one acquaintance. Show your work somewhere it can be seen. Keep the luck log from Chapter 4: each evening, a line about what you did, what surprised you, good and bad, and who helped.

In the *fourth week*, review. Read your card and your log. Choose one change to keep, one thing to stop and one uncertainty to carry forward without forcing an answer. Then rewrite the hope line of your card. Notice whether it has more roads than it did a month ago.

Throughout the month, include rest. Include setbacks. Include days when nothing happens. Write down the bad surprises as carefully as the good ones. And do not turn this into another test you can pass or fail. The point of small records is not to score yourself. It is to see your life more accurately than the large, dramatic stories that disappointment tells.

* * *

Let me come back, at the end, to the eighth stage of the kyudo shot: *zanshin*, the remaining mind.

Most of us have been taught a lot about the first seven stages, even if we never called them that. We have been taught to prepare, to work, to aim and to try. Some of us have been taught to pray. Almost none of us were taught what to do in the moment after the release, when the arrow is gone and we are waiting, or when it has landed somewhere we did not want.

Zanshin says: stay. Do not collapse. Do not turn away in shame or rush to the next arrow in a panic. Hold your form. Watch with clear eyes where the arrow went. Feel whatever you feel. And remain yourself, an archer, whatever the target says.

After the arrow leaves, the archer remains.

Then, when you are ready, lower the bow, walk back to the line and draw again.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*After the arrow leaves, the archer remains.*

**Practice.** Fill in one full archer's card this week for something you care about: the goal, the value, your next dated action, what you do not control, the story you are telling and a truer *so far* version, your hope with its obstacle and if-then plan, the help you need, other roads, what you will do if it fails and a review date. Keep it where you will see it every day.

# Chapter 5: The drowned do not paint pictures

On the Greek island of Samothrace, more than two thousand years ago, there was a temple where sailors came to give thanks. When a storm caught a ship at sea, the frightened crew would make a vow to the gods: *save us, and we will honor you.* Those who reached land alive kept their promise. They commissioned small painted tablets showing their ship in the storm and their rescue, and they hung them on the temple walls. Over the years, the walls filled up.

The Roman writer Cicero tells a story about a visitor to this temple, a man named Diagoras, who had a reputation for doubting the gods. A friend took him to see the tablets. *You think the gods do not care about human beings,* the friend said. *Look at all these pictures. Look how many people made vows in a storm and were saved.*

Diagoras looked. *Yes,* he said. *But where are the pictures of those who made vows and drowned?*

I want to be careful with this story, because this is not a chapter about whether prayers are answered. We will come to prayer later in the book, with the respect it deserves. Many people who believe deeply in prayer can accept Diagoras's point completely, because his point is not really about the gods. It is about counting.

The tablets on the wall could not tell anyone how often vows were followed by rescue. They could only tell you that survivors paint pictures. Everyone who made the same vow and went down with the ship was absent from the evidence. Not because they did not exist, but because the drowned do not paint pictures.

> *The drowned do not paint pictures. Every wall of success stories is missing its other half.*

* * *

During the Second World War, a group of mathematicians at Columbia University in New York worked on problems for the American military. One of them was Abraham Wald, a Hungarian-born statistician who had fled Europe before the war.

One of the problems they worked on was how to protect bomber aircraft. The story, in the simplified form in which it is usually told, goes like this. The military looked at planes returning from missions and recorded where they had been hit by enemy fire. The damage was concentrated in certain areas, such as the wings and the body of the plane. The obvious conclusion was to add armor to those areas. Armor is heavy, so you cannot put it everywhere; you put it where the holes are.

Wald saw the problem. These were the planes that *came back*. The holes showed where a plane could be hit and still return. The places where the returning planes had few holes, such as around the engines, were not places that were rarely hit. They were places where a hit tended to bring a plane down, so those planes were not in the sample. The armor should go where the holes were not.

Historians have since pointed out that Wald's real work was more technical than the legend suggests; he wrote a series of mathematical memos about how to estimate a plane's weak points from the damage on the survivors. But the heart of the legend is true, and it has become the most famous example of what statisticians call *survivorship bias*: drawing conclusions from the cases that survived some process, while forgetting the ones that did not.

Once you understand survivorship bias, you begin to see votive tablets everywhere.

* * *

The business magazine profiles the founder who dropped out of university and became a billionaire. It does not profile the thousands who dropped out of university, started companies and went bankrupt. The interview with the actor who moved to the city with nothing and refused to give up does not include the many actors who moved to the city with nothing, refused to give up, and are still waiting tables. The successful investor explains her strategy on television. Nobody invites the hundreds of people who used the same strategy and lost.

These stories are not lies. The founder really did drop out; the actor really did persist. The problem is that the stories arrive without their missing half, and so we draw the wrong lesson. We conclude that dropping out, persisting and following that strategy *cause* success, when we have no idea how often they lead to failure.

Think of one of the most loved success stories of our time. J. K. Rowling's first Harry Potter manuscript was rejected by around a dozen publishers before a small London publisher took a chance on it. The story is told and retold to aspiring writers as a lesson: *don't give up.*

It is a beautiful story, and I do not want to take it away from anyone. But Diagoras would ask a simple question. How many manuscripts were rejected twelve times, and then rejected twelve more times, and then fifty more? Their authors did not give up either. They kept sending the pages out, year after year. We do not hear their stories, because the drowned do not paint pictures.

Persistence may well be necessary for most kinds of success. That is very different from saying it is sufficient. The tablets on the wall can teach us the first. They cannot teach us the second.

> *Every success story has a sample size. Ask who was left outside it.*

* * *

There is an even more unsettling question lurking here. Suppose we could see all the failures as well as all the successes. Would the best work always win?

In 2006, the sociologists Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds and Duncan Watts ran an experiment to find out, in an artificial world they built themselves. They created a website where more than fourteen thousand people could listen to and download songs by unknown bands. There were forty-eight songs in all, and none of the participants had heard any of them before.

Some participants were placed in an "independent" world, where they saw only the names of the bands and songs and made their own choices. The rest were divided into eight separate worlds, each of which showed the participants how many times each song had already been downloaded by others in that world. The eight worlds started identically. Each one was a parallel universe in which the same songs, heard by similar people, competed for attention.

The results were striking. In the worlds where people could see what others were downloading, the popular songs became far more popular and the unpopular ones far less, compared with the independent world. Success became more unequal. And it became much less predictable. The same song could become a hit in one world and sit in the middle of the pack in another. Early downloads, which were essentially random, snowballed. A small lead at the beginning tended to grow into a large lead at the end.

Quality was not irrelevant. The songs that did best in the independent world rarely did terribly in the social worlds, and the songs that did worst rarely did brilliantly. But across a wide middle range, which song became a hit depended heavily on what happened to be downloaded first.

I want to be fair to this study. It was one experiment, in one artificial music market, more than twenty years ago. It cannot tell us the exact role of chance in any particular career. But it offers a powerful challenge to the comforting belief that the best work always rises to the top. In a world where people watch each other, and they always do, early luck can be amplified until it looks like destiny.

* * *

The economist Robert Frank, whom I mentioned in Chapter 3, added another piece to this puzzle with a simple computer simulation.

Imagine a competition with thousands of entrants. Each entrant has a score for skill and effort, and a much smaller score for luck. Even when luck counts for only a tiny share of the final result, Frank found, the winner in a large contest is usually *not* the most skilled entrant.

The reason is simple once you see it. When thousands of people compete, the best of them are extremely close together in skill. There is not much daylight between the top hundred. So the small amount of luck, the good day, the favorable judge, the timing, becomes the tie-breaker. It does not take much wind to decide a contest between archers who are all excellent.

This has a strange and important consequence. The bigger and more competitive the prize, the more likely it is that the winner is someone who was *both* very good and very lucky. The many people who were equally good, but less lucky, are invisible. And the winner, looking back, will quite naturally explain their success in terms of their skill and effort, because they really were skilled and they really did work hard. Everything they say will be true. It will just be missing the wind.

> *The winner's method deserves a question before it becomes everyone's rule.*

* * *

None of this is meant to make you cynical about success, or to take anything away from people who have achieved great things. Most of them earned their place among the finalists through years of work. The point is to see clearly what their stories can and cannot teach.

When you hear a success story, it helps to ask a few quiet questions, the way Diagoras did.

*How many people did the same thing and failed?* Usually you will not know the number. But simply asking the question loosens the story's grip.

*What advantages did this person have that are not in the story?* Money, connections, health, timing, a particular place, a particular decade.

*What would have happened with a slightly different roll of the dice?* If the first investor had said no, if the song had been downloaded fourth instead of first, if the plane had been hit two feet to the left.

*Does the person telling the story acknowledge luck?* In my experience, the ones who do are often the most trustworthy guides.

These questions do not make success stories useless. They make them honest. A survivor can still teach you a great deal about how to sail. They just cannot tell you that their way of sailing guarantees reaching shore.

* * *

There is one more place where votive tablets hang, and it is the one most of us visit every day.

Sana, after her rejection, did what many of us do when we are hurting. She opened her phone and scrolled. And there, on the walls of social media, she found tablet after tablet. A school friend announcing a new job at a famous company. A cousin's engagement photos. A former colleague's promotion. An acquaintance's new house. Every one of them a picture of someone who reached land.

What Sana could not see were the drowned. The friend who had applied for forty jobs before this one. The cousin's two broken engagements. The colleague who was turned down for the same promotion twice. The acquaintance's debt. People rarely post their storms, only their rescues. So the wall gives a picture of the world in which everyone else's arrows land on target, and only yours fall into the grass.

That picture is false. Not because the posts are false, but because the wall is missing its other half.

> *Social media is a wall of votive tablets. The drowned do not post.*

So when you look at other people's success and feel your own failure more sharply, remember Diagoras on Samothrace. Look at the wall. Admire the rescues; many of them were hard-won. And then ask, gently, where the other pictures are. You will not find them. But knowing they exist is enough to stop the wall from lying to you about your own life.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*The drowned do not paint pictures.*

*Every success story has a sample size. Ask who was left outside it.*

*It does not take much wind to decide a contest between archers who are all excellent.*

**Practice.** Choose one success story that has shaped how you think, a famous person or someone you know. Write down three things that are missing from it: people who did the same thing and failed, advantages the story does not mention, and moments where a different roll of the dice would have changed the ending. Then ask what lesson is still left standing.

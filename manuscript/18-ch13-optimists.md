# Chapter 13: The optimists who did not come home

In September 1965, an American navy pilot named James Stockdale was shot down over North Vietnam. He ejected, landed in a village, was beaten by the people who found him and was taken to a prison in Hanoi that the American prisoners would later call, with bitter humor, the Hanoi Hilton.

He stayed there for more than seven years.

As the most senior navy officer among the prisoners, Stockdale became a leader of the men held there. He was tortured many times. He spent long periods in solitary confinement, sometimes in leg irons. He had no rights as a prisoner, no date of release and no way of knowing whether he would ever see his wife and sons again. He did not know whether the war would end in one year or ten.

Stockdale was released in 1973. Decades later, the business writer Jim Collins interviewed him for a book about why some companies become great. Collins asked a simple question. Who didn't make it out?

*Oh, that's easy,* Stockdale said. *The optimists.*

Collins was confused. The optimists?

*The optimists. Oh, they were the ones who said, "We're going to be out by Christmas." And Christmas would come, and Christmas would go. Then they'd say, "We're going to be out by Easter." And Easter would come, and Easter would go. And then Thanksgiving, and then it would be Christmas again. And they died of a broken heart.*

Then Stockdale said something that Collins would later call the *Stockdale Paradox*:

*You must never confuse faith that you will prevail in the end—which you can never afford to lose—with the discipline to confront the most brutal facts of your current reality, whatever they might be.*

* * *

I want to be careful with this story, as I have tried to be careful with every story in this book. Stockdale was describing his memories of a terrible place, many years later. He was not running a study. We do not know, and cannot know, exactly why particular men did not survive; illness, injury, torture and despair were all bound up together. One man's observation is not a law of nature.

But Stockdale was not the only person to see this pattern.

Twenty years before Stockdale was shot down, the Austrian psychiatrist Viktor Frankl was a prisoner in the Nazi concentration camps. In *Man's Search for Meaning*, the book he wrote soon after his liberation, he describes something the chief doctor of his camp told him. In the week between Christmas 1944 and New Year 1945, the death rate in the camp rose beyond anything they had seen before. The work had not become harder. The food had not become worse. The weather had not changed in any special way. The doctor's explanation was that a great many prisoners had lived in the naïve hope that they would be home by Christmas. As the day came closer and there was no encouraging news, they lost courage, and the disappointment overcame them.

Frankl also tells the story of a fellow prisoner who confided in him, in February 1945, that he had had a strange dream. A voice had told him that he could ask any question and it would be answered. He had asked when the war would end, for him. The voice had answered: on the thirtieth of March.

The man was full of hope. But as the thirtieth of March approached, the news from the front showed no sign of liberation. On the twenty-ninth, he suddenly became ill and ran a high fever. On the thirtieth, the day the voice had promised, he lost consciousness. On the thirty-first of March, he died.

Frankl was a doctor, and he did not pretend that disappointment alone had killed his friend. The man died of typhus, a disease that was everywhere in the camps. But Frankl believed, from long observation, that the sudden collapse of hope had weakened the body's ability to resist it.

* * *

Two men, in two different wars, in two of the worst places human beings have ever built, saw the same thing. I do not think it is a coincidence. And I think what they saw has something to teach those of us whose struggles are, thank God, much smaller.

What failed, in both stories, was not hope. It was hope with a date.

The prisoners who said *we'll be out by Christmas* were not wrong to want freedom. They were not wrong to believe it would come; for many of them, eventually, it did. Their mistake, if it can be called a mistake in a place of such suffering, was to tie their hope to a specific outcome at a specific time. They made an appointment with the future. And the future did not keep it.

When a hope with a date is broken, it is not broken once. It is broken again and again, every time the next date passes. Each disappointment takes something that cannot easily be replaced. Eventually, there is nothing left to hope with.

> *Hope that sets a date with the universe will one day be stood up.*

* * *

What survived?

Stockdale described two things that he held at the same time, and he insisted that they had to be held together.

The first was faith in the end of the story. *I never lost faith in the end of the story,* he told Collins. *I never doubted not only that I would get out, but also that I would prevail in the end and turn the experience into the defining event of my life.* This faith was not tied to a date. It was tied to an outcome so broad, so far away, that no single Christmas could break it.

The second was a ruthless honesty about the present. Stockdale did not pretend that things were better than they were. Collins later wrote that he carried a mental image of Stockdale turning to the optimists and saying: *We're not getting out by Christmas; deal with it!* Stockdale looked at the brutal facts every day, and he built his life around them. He helped organize ways for the prisoners to communicate secretly, tapping coded messages through the walls. He helped draw up rules for how the men should resist, so that no prisoner had to work out alone what was expected of him. He kept people connected, which kept them alive.

Frankl described something similar from his own camp, though he used different words. He found that the prisoners who held up best were often those who had something to live *for*: a person waiting for them, a task unfinished, a book they intended to write. He liked to quote Nietzsche here, of all people: *He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how.* The *why* was not a date. It was a meaning. A meaning cannot be cancelled by a calendar.

> *Hold faith in the end of the story, and honesty about this chapter. You need both.*

* * *

Most of us will never be prisoners of war. But many of us will spend some part of our lives in a long wait.

Waiting for a job after months of applications. Waiting for a visa. Waiting for a diagnosis, or a recovery. Waiting for a marriage proposal that keeps being postponed. Waiting for a family to accept a choice. Waiting for a debt to be paid off, a court case to finish, a child to come home.

And in these waits, it is very natural to do what the optimists did. *By the end of the month, I'll have heard back. By the summer, I'll be well. By her birthday, he'll have changed his mind. By next year, everything will be different.* We set dates because dates give the fog a shape. They make the waiting feel finite.

There is nothing wrong with planning by dates. A target date is a useful tool: *I will send ten applications by Friday. I will review my strategy at the end of March.* Those are dates you control. They are about your own actions.

The danger comes when the date becomes a prophecy about something you do not control. *I will have a job by March.* *She will come back by Eid.* *I will be cured by summer.* These are not plans. They are appointments with the wind. And when the wind does not show up, the hope that was tied to the appointment takes the blow.

> *A deadline is a tool. A prophecy is a trap.*

* * *

So how do you hold hope through a long wait without being broken by it? Here is what I have learned from Stockdale, from Frankl and from watching people I love go through their own long seasons.

Hope for the end of the story, not for a particular page. *I will work again. I will build a life I am glad to live. I will find people to love who love me back.* These hopes are wide enough that no single disappointment can kill them.

Put your dates on your actions, not on outcomes. You decide when you will apply, call, rest, review and try again. You do not decide when the answer comes.

Look at the brutal facts on a regular schedule. Once a week, perhaps, ask honestly: *What is actually true right now? What has changed? What has not?* Facing reality on purpose, at a set time, is much less frightening than having it ambush you at three in the morning.

Build meaning inside the wait, not only beyond it. Stockdale's men tapped messages through the walls. Frankl, sick with typhus in the camp, began rebuilding on scraps of paper the manuscript that had been taken from him. The wait is not an empty corridor between your old life and your real life. It is your life, right now. What can you learn, give or build inside it?

And do not wait alone. The tapping through the walls was not a small detail. Isolation turns waiting into despair faster than anything else. Find the people who will tap back.

* * *

There is a strange gift hidden in Stockdale's paradox, and I want to end with it.

When you stop tying your hope to a date, the hope becomes harder to break. That much is obvious. But something else happens too. You become free to notice the present: the small kindnesses, the unexpected openings, the people tapping through the walls. The optimists in Stockdale's prison spent so much of their attention on the next Christmas that they had little left for the cell they were actually in. Stockdale, by accepting the cell, could work inside it.

The archer does not know when the wind will drop. If she decides that it must drop by noon, and it does not, she may put her bow down in despair. But if she simply knows that winds change, that they always eventually change, she can keep her bow strung and her eyes open and draw again the moment the air goes still.

Faith in the end of the story. Honesty about this chapter. That is the kind of hope that brings people home.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Hope that sets a date with the universe will one day be stood up.*

*A deadline is a tool. A prophecy is a trap.*

*Hold faith in the end of the story, and honesty about this chapter.*

**Practice.** Write down any hopes you are holding that have a date attached to an outcome you do not control. Rewrite each one in two parts: a wide hope for the end of the story, and a dated plan for your own next actions. Then choose one day each week when you will look at the brutal facts on purpose, and put it in your calendar.

# Chapter 13: The optimists who did not come home

In September 1965, an American navy pilot named James Stockdale was shot down over North Vietnam. He ejected, landed in a village, was beaten by the people who found him and was taken to a prison in Hanoi that the American prisoners would later call, with bitter humor, the Hanoi Hilton.

He stayed there for more than seven years.

As the most senior navy officer among the prisoners, Stockdale became one of their leaders. He was tortured many times. He spent long stretches in solitary confinement, sometimes in leg irons. He had no rights, no release date and no way of knowing whether he would ever see his wife and sons again. He did not know whether the war would end in one year or ten.

Stockdale was released in 1973. Decades later, the business writer Jim Collins interviewed him for a book about why some companies become great, and asked a simple question. Who didn't make it out?

Stockdale's answer surprised him. The optimists, he said.

The optimists, as Stockdale described them, were the men who were sure they would be home by Christmas. Christmas came and went. Then they were sure they would be home by Easter. Easter came and went. Then the next holiday, and then Christmas again. And in the end, Stockdale said, they died of a broken heart.

Then he described what he had done differently, and Collins later named it the *Stockdale Paradox*. Stockdale never lost faith that he would get out, and even that he would one day turn the experience into the defining event of his life. But he kept that faith completely separate from his reading of the present. He refused to lie to himself about how bad things were, or how long they might last. Faith in the ending. Brutal honesty about today. He insisted that you needed both, and that confusing them could kill you.

* * *

I want to be careful with this story, as I have tried to be careful with every story in this book. Stockdale was describing his memories of a terrible place, many years later. He was not running a study. We cannot know exactly why particular men did not survive; illness, injury, torture and despair were all bound up together. One man's observation is not a law of nature.

But Stockdale was not the only person to see this pattern.

Twenty years before Stockdale was shot down, the Austrian psychiatrist Viktor Frankl was a prisoner in the Nazi concentration camps. In *Man's Search for Meaning*, written soon after his liberation, he records something the chief doctor of his camp told him. In the week between Christmas 1944 and New Year 1945, the death rate in the camp rose beyond anything they had seen before. The work had not become harder. The food had not become worse. The weather had not changed in any special way. The doctor's explanation was that many prisoners had lived in the hope of being home by Christmas. As the day came closer with no encouraging news, they lost courage, and the disappointment overwhelmed them.

Frankl also tells of a fellow prisoner who confided in him, in February 1945, that he had had a strange dream. A voice had offered to answer any question, and he had asked when the war would end for him. The voice had named a date: the thirtieth of March.

The man was full of hope. But as the thirtieth of March approached, the news from the front showed no sign of liberation. On the twenty-ninth, he fell suddenly ill with a high fever. On the thirtieth, the promised day, he lost consciousness. On the thirty-first of March, he died.

Frankl was a doctor, and he did not pretend that disappointment alone had killed his friend. The man died of typhus, which was everywhere in the camps. But Frankl believed, from long observation, that the collapse of a hope tied to a date had weakened his body's ability to fight.

* * *

Two men, in two wars, in two of the worst places human beings have ever built, saw the same thing. I do not think that is a coincidence. And I think it has something to teach those of us whose struggles are, thankfully, much smaller.

What failed, in both stories, was not hope. It was hope with a date.

The prisoners who said *we'll be out by Christmas* were not wrong to want freedom. They were not wrong to believe it would come; for many of them, eventually, it did. Their mistake, if such a word can even be used in a place of such suffering, was to tie their hope to a specific outcome at a specific time. They made an appointment with the future, and the future did not keep it.

A hope with a date is not broken once. It is broken again every time the next date passes. Each disappointment takes something that is hard to replace. Eventually there is nothing left to hope with.

Hope that sets a date with the universe will one day be stood up.

* * *

What survived?

Stockdale held two things at once. The first was faith in the end of the story, an outcome so broad and so distant that no single Christmas could break it. Not *I will be home by Easter*, but *I will get out, and I will make something of this*. The second was ruthless honesty about the present. He looked at the facts every day and built his life around them. He helped organize ways for the prisoners to communicate secretly, tapping coded messages through the walls. He helped draw up rules for how the men should resist, so that no prisoner had to work out alone what was expected of him. He kept people connected, and connection kept them alive.

Frankl described something similar in his own camp, in different words. The prisoners who held up best were often those who had something to live *for*: a person waiting for them, a task unfinished, a book they intended to write. A reason to live can carry a person through almost any way of living. It was not a date. It was a meaning, and a meaning cannot be cancelled by a calendar.

* * *

Most of us will never be prisoners of war. But almost all of us will spend some part of our lives in a long wait, and waiting is its own kind of hard.

Waiting for a job after months of applications. Waiting for a visa, or a residence permit, or a court decision. Waiting for test results, and then for the treatment to work. Waiting, month after month, for a pregnancy. Waiting for a business to turn a profit. Waiting for a loan to be paid off. Waiting for a family to accept a marriage, or a child to come home, or a parent to forgive.

In these waits it is very natural to do what the optimists did. *By the end of the month I'll hear back. By summer I'll be well. By her birthday he'll have changed his mind. By next year everything will be different.* We set dates because dates give the fog a shape. They make the waiting feel finite.

There is nothing wrong with planning by dates. A target date for your own actions is a useful tool: *I will send ten applications by Friday. I will review my approach at the end of March. I will ask the clinic about other options after this cycle.* Those are promises you can keep.

The danger begins when the date becomes a prophecy about something you do not control. *I will have a job by March. He will come back by Eid. I will be cured by summer. The visa will come before the baby is born.* These are not plans. They are appointments with the wind. And when the wind does not show up, the hope tied to the appointment takes the blow.

A deadline is a tool. A prophecy is a trap.

* * *

So how do you hold hope through a long wait without letting it break you? Here is what I have learned from Stockdale, from Frankl and from watching people I love live through their own long seasons.

*Hope for the end of the story, not for a particular page.* I will work again. I will build a life I am glad to live. I will find people to love who love me back. Hopes this wide cannot be killed by one disappointment.

*Put your dates on your actions, never on outcomes.* You decide when you apply, call, rest, review and try again. You do not decide when the answer comes.

*Face the facts on a schedule.* Once a week, perhaps, ask honestly: What is actually true right now? What has changed, and what has not? Facing reality on purpose, at a set time, is far less frightening than letting it ambush you at three in the morning.

*Build meaning inside the wait, not only beyond it.* Stockdale's men tapped messages through the walls. Frankl, sick in the camp, began rebuilding on scraps of paper the manuscript that had been taken from him. The wait is not an empty corridor between your old life and your real life. It is your life, right now. What can you learn, give or build inside it?

*Do not wait alone.* The tapping through the walls was not a small detail. Isolation turns waiting into despair faster than anything else. Find the people who will tap back.

* * *

There is a strange gift hidden in Stockdale's paradox, and I want to end with it.

When you stop tying your hope to a date, the hope becomes harder to break. That much is obvious. But something else happens too. You become free to notice the present: the small kindnesses, the unexpected openings, the people tapping through the walls. The optimists in Stockdale's prison spent so much attention on the next Christmas that they had little left for the cell they were actually in. Stockdale, by accepting the cell, could work inside it.

The archer does not know when the wind will drop. If she decides it must drop by noon, and it does not, she may put down her bow in despair. But if she simply knows that winds change, that they always change eventually, she can keep the bow strung and her eyes open, and draw the moment the air goes still.

Faith in the ending. Honesty about today. That is the kind of hope that brings people home.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Hope that sets a date with the universe will one day be stood up.*

**Practice.** Write down any hope you are holding that has a date attached to an outcome you do not control. Rewrite each one in two parts: a wide hope for the end of the story, and a dated plan for your own next actions. Then choose one day each week when you will look at the facts on purpose, and put it in your calendar.

# Chapter 8: If the future is fixed, why try?

The word *destiny* hides three different questions, and much of our confusion comes from mixing them up.

The first question is about the future: *if what will happen is already fixed, does my effort make any difference?*

The second is about the past: *why does everything that happened to me feel, looking back, as though it was always going to happen?*

The third is about the present: *how much room do I actually have to choose, given my money, my time and the people who depend on me?*

This chapter takes the first question. The next two chapters take the others. Keeping them apart is half the work, because an answer that is true for one of them can be poison when applied to another.

So here is the first question in its plainest form, the one that started this whole book. If my future is already written, if I will receive exactly what I was always going to receive, then why work hard? Why pray? Why lie awake hoping, if the ending is already on the page?

I used to think this was my question, or at least the question of a tired and anxious generation. It is not. It is one of the oldest questions human beings ever wrote down. And more than two thousand years ago, someone gave an answer that I still think is one of the cleverest replies in the history of thought.

* * *

The ancient Greeks had a name for the argument. They called it the *lazy argument*, or the *idle argument*. The Roman writer Cicero preserved it in a short book called *On Fate*, written in the year Julius Caesar was murdered. His version is about illness, but it works for anything. Here it is, applied to an exam.

If it is fated that you will pass this exam, you will pass whether you study or not.

If it is fated that you will fail, you will fail whether you study or not.

One of the two is fated.

So studying is pointless.

Cicero pointed out that if anyone took this argument seriously, they would do nothing at all in life. Why plant seeds, if the harvest is fated? Why call a doctor, if recovery is fated? Why look both ways before crossing the road? The argument does not just make effort pointless. It makes every action pointless.

And yet, if you believe the future is fixed, it seems very hard to escape. Each step sounds reasonable. Where exactly does it go wrong?

* * *

The answer came from a philosopher named Chrysippus, who led the Stoic school in Athens in the third century before the common era. The Stoics believed in fate as firmly as anyone ever has. They thought the whole universe was a single chain of causes. So they could not answer the lazy argument by saying fate does not exist. They had to find the mistake inside it.

Chrysippus found it. Some outcomes, he said, are *co-fated* with the actions that produce them. They are bound together like the two ends of the same rope.

It is not fated that you will pass the exam *whether or not you study*. If you are fated to pass, it may be fated that you will pass *by studying*. The result and the effort come as a package. Saying "I will pass whether or not I study" is like saying, in an example Cicero gives, that the great wrestler Milo will wrestle at the Olympic Games whether or not anyone wrestles against him. It is nonsense. The outcome contains the action inside it.

Read that slowly, because it changes everything.

The lazy argument imagines destiny as something that happens *to* us from the outside, a result that arrives regardless of what we do, like a parcel delivered to an empty house. Chrysippus imagines destiny as something that happens *through* us. If there is a written story, our choices are not outside it. They are among its sentences. If your destiny is written, your effort may be part of the ink.

* * *

Let me make this concrete, because there is a modern version of the same worry, and it sounds even more convincing than the ancient one.

Meet Nadia. She is a composite. She is nineteen, and in three weeks she has a final exam that will decide whether she can continue her degree. Tonight she is choosing between her notes and her phone.

Suppose she chooses the notes. Why did she choose them?

Trace it back. She studies because she is afraid of failing. She is afraid of failing because her parents sacrificed a great deal to send her to university, and she knows it. She has the discipline to sit down because her grandmother made her do her homework at the kitchen table every evening when she was eight. She has the energy tonight because she slept well, and she slept well because her flatmate was away. She can concentrate because of the brain she was born with, which she did not design.

Every link in that chain has a cause, and every cause has a cause behind it. Many scientists and philosophers believe this is true of every human choice: that each one is the product of what came before. Some go further and say there is no room left for anything we could call free will.

So here is the modern worry. If Nadia's decision to study was itself caused by things she did not choose, does her studying really matter? Isn't she just a domino falling in a line set up long before she was born?

* * *

Here is the answer, and I want to give it the room it deserves, because it is the single most useful idea in this part of the book.

A caused action can still be a cause.

Picture two versions of the same evening. In the first, Nadia studies for three hours. In the second, she spends the same three hours scrolling. Everything else is identical. Which Nadia walks into the exam better prepared? Obviously the first. Which is more likely to pass? The first.

That is all it means for an action to matter. To say Nadia's studying makes a difference is to say that, if she had not studied, things would probably have gone differently. That is true whether or not her decision to study was itself caused by her grandmother, her fear and a good night's sleep. Her studying has causes behind it. It also has effects in front of it. Both are real at once.

Think of the dominoes again. Every domino in the line was pushed. It did not choose to fall. And every domino, as it falls, pushes the next one. Take one domino out of the line and the chain stops. The fact that it was pushed does not make it unimportant. It is exactly the piece that carries the push forward.

You are a domino that was pushed. You are also a domino that pushes.

This is why the oldest and newest versions of the worry fail in the same way. Whether the future is fixed by fate, by God or by the laws of physics, nothing in that idea says your effort is outside the chain. Your effort is one of the links. Remove it and the outcome changes. Keep it and the outcome changes too.

* * *

It helps to separate two ideas that people often mix up, because the mixing causes a great deal of needless despair.

The first idea is *fatalism*. In its everyday form, it says that certain outcomes will happen *no matter what you do*. The lazy argument is fatalism. So is the belief that you are "destined" to fail whatever you try, or that a particular person is "destined" to be yours whatever happens. Fatalism cuts the line between your actions and your outcomes.

The second idea is *determinism*: the view that everything that happens, including human choices, is the result of earlier causes.

They sound alike, but they point in opposite directions. Determinism does *not* say outcomes happen no matter what you do. It says the opposite: that what you do is one of the causes of what happens. Nadia's studying is part of the chain that produces her exam result. Her studying has causes of its own, but it is still a cause.

Whether determinism is true, and whether it leaves room for free will, is a question philosophers are still arguing over, and we will look at the practical side of it in Chapter 10. But here is what matters now. Neither religious destiny nor scientific determinism supports the conclusion that effort is pointless. Only fatalism does. And fatalism, as the Stoics showed long ago, falls apart under careful thought.

* * *

There is a second answer to the lazy argument, and in practice I find it even more useful, because it does not depend on what you believe about fate at all.

Suppose everything really is written. Do you know what it says?

Nobody has ever read their destiny in advance. Not one person in history. We only read it backward, one page at a time, after the page has been lived. Before the event, the person who believes in destiny and the person who does not are standing in exactly the same place: in front of a choice, without knowing how it ends.

Destiny, then, can never tell you what to do *next*. It cannot tell Nadia whether to study tonight, because she does not know whether passing is written for her, or whether passing is written *together with* studying tonight. It cannot tell you whether to apply for the job, keep the shop open, start the treatment or make the phone call. The only way to discover what was written is to live the page in front of you.

So the lazy argument fails twice. It fails in logic, because it forgets that outcomes can be bound up with the actions that bring them about. And it fails in practice, because even if it were sound, nobody could ever use it. We cannot consult the book. We can only write in it, or be written through it, whichever way you prefer to see it.

* * *

Then why does the lazy argument feel so convincing when we are hurting?

I think it is because destiny does two different jobs in our lives, and we confuse them.

The first job is comfort. When something we wanted is lost, the thought that it was "not meant to be" can bring real peace. It tells us we did not fail through carelessness. It lets us stop replaying the decision at three in the morning. For many people, the words *it was not written for me* are what finally let them sleep after a loss. I would never take those words from anyone.

The second job is guidance. Here destiny is asked to tell us what to do next: whether to try, whether to wait, whether to give up. And this is the job destiny cannot do, because we cannot read it in advance.

The trouble begins when we hand destiny the second job. Then it turns into waiting. *If the job is meant for me, it will come*, so we stop applying. *If my health is meant to recover, it will*, so we skip the appointment. *If she is meant for me, she will come back*, so we stop living and wait by the door. *If success is written, it will happen*, so we stop working, and are surprised when it does not.

I have watched people lose years this way. Not because they believed in destiny, but because they gave destiny the wrong job.

Destiny is a beautiful word for the past and a dangerous word for the future.

* * *

So here is how I have come to hold it, and I offer it whatever you believe.

When you look *backward*, at something already lived, use the language of destiny freely if it brings you peace. *It was not meant to be. It came at the right time.* These can be honest, healing words for a page that has already turned.

When you look *forward*, at the choice in front of you, set destiny gently aside. Not because it is false, but because it is silent. It does not tell you what to do. You must decide, with the best judgment you have, as though your choice matters, because whether you believe in fate, in God's knowledge, in physics or in none of these, it does. It is one of the ways the story gets written.

The archer does not know where her arrow will land. Perhaps somewhere it is already known. But she cannot read that place before she shoots. She can only draw, aim and release. And if the landing place is written, then her drawing and aiming are written too, as part of the same sentence.

There is no version of destiny in which the bow draws itself.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*A caused action can still be a cause.*

**Practice.** Take a sentence you have told yourself that uses destiny to stop you acting, such as "If it's meant to happen, it will happen" or "There's no point, it's already decided." Write it out as a full argument, step by step, like the lazy argument. Find the step that assumes your action cannot be part of what happens. Cross it out. What is left?

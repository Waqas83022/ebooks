# Chapter 8: If it is written, why write?

Here is the question in its plainest form, the one that started this whole book.

If my destiny is already written, if I will receive exactly what I was always going to receive, then what is the point of working hard? What is the point of praying? Why should I lie awake hoping, if the ending is already on the page?

I used to think this was my question, or at least a question of my generation, worn out by competition and uncertainty. It is not. It is one of the oldest questions human beings have written down. And more than two thousand years ago, someone gave an answer to it that I still think is one of the cleverest replies in the history of thought.

* * *

The ancient Greeks had a name for the argument. They called it the *argos logos*, which means "the lazy argument" or "the idle argument." The Roman writer Cicero preserved it in a short book called *On Fate*, written in the year Julius Caesar was assassinated.

It goes like this. Suppose you are ill.

If it is fated that you will recover from this illness, then you will recover whether you call a doctor or not.

If it is fated that you will not recover, then you will not recover whether you call a doctor or not.

One of these two is fated.

Therefore, calling a doctor is pointless.

Cicero points out that if anyone took this argument seriously, they would do nothing at all in life. Why study for an exam, if passing or failing is fated? Why plant seeds, if the harvest is fated? Why look both ways before crossing the street? The argument does not just make effort pointless. It makes every action pointless.

And yet, if you believe in fate, it seems very hard to escape. Each step looks reasonable. Where exactly does it go wrong?

* * *

The answer came from a philosopher named Chrysippus, who led the Stoic school in Athens in the third century before the common era. The Stoics believed in fate as firmly as anyone ever has. They thought the whole universe was a single chain of causes, unfolding according to a rational plan. So they could not reply to the lazy argument by saying that fate does not exist. They had to find the mistake inside it.

Chrysippus found it. Some events, he said, are simple. But many events are *co-fated*: they are fated together with other events, as a package. They cannot happen without them.

It is not fated, he said, that you will recover "whether or not you call the doctor." If you are fated to recover, it may be fated that you will recover *by calling the doctor*. The recovery and the call are joined. To say "I will recover whether or not I call the doctor" is like saying "I will have children whether or not I marry," or, in an example Cicero gives, "the great wrestler Milo will wrestle at the Olympic Games whether or not anyone wrestles against him." It is nonsense. The outcome contains the action inside it.

Read that again slowly, because I think it changes everything.

The lazy argument imagines destiny as something that happens *to* us from outside, a result that arrives regardless of what we do, like a package delivered to an empty house. Chrysippus's reply imagines destiny as something that happens *through* us. If there is a written story, our choices are not outside it. They are among the sentences.

> *If your destiny is written, your effort may be part of the ink.*

* * *

There is a story from the early history of Islam that makes the same point so beautifully that I cannot leave it out. I share it not as theology, but because it shows that people in very different places, with very different beliefs, have reached for the same answer to the same question.

In the year 639, the caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab was travelling with his army toward Syria. At a place called Sargh, his commanders met him with bad news. A plague had broken out in the land ahead. Umar consulted his companions, and after much discussion he decided to turn back rather than lead his people into the epidemic.

One of his most respected commanders, Abu Ubaidah, challenged him. *Are you running away from what God has decreed?*

Umar's reply has been remembered ever since. *Yes,* he said. *We are running from the decree of God to the decree of God.*

Then he gave an example. Suppose you had camels, he said, and you came to a valley with two sides, one green with grass and the other bare. If you grazed your camels on the green side, would you not be doing so by God's decree? And if you grazed them on the bare side, would that not also be by God's decree?

In other words, the choice to act wisely is not a rebellion against destiny. It is one of the ways destiny unfolds. Turning back from the plague was not an escape from what was written. It was what was written.

Chrysippus, in Athens, and Umar, in the Syrian desert, were separated by nine centuries and two very different views of the world. Yet they arrived at the same place. For anyone who believes in destiny, effort is not the opposite of fate. It may be how fate does its work.

* * *

There is a second answer to the lazy argument, and in practice I find it even more useful, because it does not depend on what you believe about fate at all.

Suppose everything really is written. Do you know what it says?

No one has ever read their destiny in advance. Not one person. We only ever read it backward, one page at a time, after the page has been lived. Before the event, the person who believes in destiny and the person who does not are standing in exactly the same place: in front of a choice, without knowing how it will turn out.

This means that destiny, whatever it is, can never tell you what to do *next*. It cannot tell you whether to apply for the job, because you do not know whether the job is written for you. It cannot tell you whether to keep trying in a relationship, because you do not know whether the relationship is written. It cannot tell you whether to study tonight, because you do not know whether passing the exam is written, or whether passing the exam is written *together with* studying tonight.

The only way to find out what was written is to live the page in front of you.

> *Nobody has ever read their destiny in advance. We only read it backward.*

So the lazy argument fails twice. It fails logically, because it forgets that outcomes can be fated together with the actions that bring them about. And it fails practically, because even if it were sound, we could never use it. We cannot consult the book. We can only write in it, or be written through it, however you prefer to see it.

* * *

I want to say a word about the difference between two ideas that people often mix up, because the mixing causes a great deal of unnecessary despair.

The first idea is what philosophers call *fatalism*. In its everyday form, it says that certain outcomes will happen *no matter what you do*. The lazy argument is fatalism. So is the belief that a particular person is "destined" to be with you whatever happens, or that you are "destined" to fail whatever you try. Fatalism cuts the line between your actions and your outcomes.

The second idea is *determinism*. This is the view, held by many scientists and philosophers, that everything that happens, including human choices, is the result of prior causes. Your decision to read this book was caused by your experiences, your brain, your situation. It did not appear from nowhere.

These two ideas sound similar, but they are very different. Determinism does *not* say that outcomes happen no matter what you do. It says the opposite: that what you do is one of the causes of what happens. If you study, the studying is part of the chain of causes that leads to the exam result. That studying may itself have causes, your parents, your habits, your fear of failure. But it is still a cause. A caused action can still be a cause.

> *A caused action can still be a cause.*

Whether determinism is true, and whether it leaves any room for what we call free will, is a question philosophers are still arguing about, and we will look at it more closely in Chapter 10. But here is what matters for now. Neither of the great ideas about a "fixed" future, religious destiny or scientific determinism, actually supports the conclusion that effort is pointless. Only fatalism does. And fatalism, as the Stoics showed long ago, does not survive careful thought.

* * *

Then why does the lazy argument feel so convincing when we are hurting?

I think it is because destiny is doing two different jobs in our lives, and we confuse them.

The first job is comfort. When something we wanted is lost, the idea that it was "not meant to be" can bring enormous peace. It tells us we did not fail because we were careless. It tells us the universe, or God, had a reason, even if we cannot see it. It lets us stop replaying the decision over and over. For many people, the words *it was not written for me* are what finally allow them to sleep after a loss. I would never take those words away from anyone.

The second job is guidance. Here, destiny is asked to tell us what to do next: whether to try, whether to wait, whether to give up. And this is the job that destiny cannot do, because, as we have seen, we cannot read it in advance.

The trouble begins when we ask destiny to do the second job. Then it turns into waiting. *If the job is meant for me, it will come.* So we stop applying. *If he is meant for me, he will return.* So we stop living, and wait by the door. *If success is written, it will happen.* So we stop working, and are surprised when it does not.

I have watched people lose years this way. Not because they believed in destiny, but because they used destiny for the wrong job.

> *Destiny is a beautiful word for the past and a dangerous word for the future.*

* * *

So here is how I have come to hold it, and I offer it to you whatever you believe.

When you look *backward*, at something you have already lived, you may use the language of destiny freely, if it brings you peace. *It was not meant to be. It was written. It came at the right time.* These can be honest and healing words for a page that has already been turned.

When you look *forward*, at the choice in front of you, set destiny gently aside. Not because it is false, but because it is silent. It does not tell you what to do. You must decide, with the best judgment you have, as though your choice matters, because, whether you believe in fate or causes or neither, it does. It is part of how the story gets written.

The archer does not know where the arrow will land. Perhaps somewhere it is already known. But the archer cannot read that place before she shoots. She can only draw, aim and release. And if the arrow's landing place is written, then her drawing and aiming are written too, as part of the same sentence.

There is no version of destiny in which the bow draws itself.

**Practice.** Take a sentence you have said to yourself that uses destiny to stop you acting: "If it's meant to happen, it will happen," or "There's no point, it's already decided." Write it out as a full argument, step by step, like the lazy argument. Then find the step that assumes your action cannot be part of what is meant to happen. Cross it out. What is left?

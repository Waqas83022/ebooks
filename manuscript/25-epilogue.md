# Epilogue: Why try? {.unnumbered}

We began with a question, and I promised you a careful answer. Here it is, as plainly as I can give it.

*If hard work cannot guarantee the result, if luck can undo the best preparation, if destiny may already be written and if prayers are sometimes answered with a no, then why try at all?*

Try because effort changes the odds. It was never a contract with the outcome, but it was never nothing either. No farmer believes that plowing guarantees rain. Every farmer plows, because an unplowed field guarantees there will be no harvest at all.

Try because work builds a place for luck to land. The wind can only carry arrows that have been released. The newspaper message is printed on page two for everyone; only the people who are looking up from their counting will see it.

Try because if your destiny is written, your effort is not outside it. It may be part of the ink. Chrysippus said so in Athens, and Umar said so at the edge of a plague. And whether or not anything is written, you cannot read the page before you live it. Nobody ever has.

Try because the arrow that misses is a loss you can grieve and recover from, while the arrow you never release becomes a question you carry for the rest of your life.

Try because of what the trying makes of you. The skills, the courage, the room you widen, the people you meet on the way, the self you discover when you want something badly enough to go after it. The company may keep the job. It does not get to keep what you built while trying to earn it.

Try because hope, at its deepest, was never a prediction. It was the certainty that something makes sense, regardless of how it turns out.

And try, finally, because the trying is the part that is yours. The outcome was never yours to own. The aim always was.

* * *

At the beginning of this book, I mentioned a song whose chorus confesses that all the trying, all that distance covered, came to nothing in the end. I understand why people sing it. I have felt it. On the night the email arrives, or the café conversation ends, or the scan comes back, it feels like the truest thing anyone has ever said.

But I do not believe it anymore.

It mattered. It mattered not because it guaranteed anything, but because it was real. You prepared, and the preparation became part of you. You loved, and the love was not wasted because it was not returned. You hoped, and the hope kept you walking through days that would otherwise have stopped you. You prayed, perhaps, and the prayer steadied your hands. None of it bought the result. All of it was yours.

In the end, it does matter. Not because of where the arrow landed, but because of who drew the bow.

* * *

I want to leave you with a picture.

It is early morning in a wide field. The grass is wet. Somewhere far down the field there is a target, small from this distance, and you are not sure you can reach it. You have missed it before. Some of your arrows are still lying in the grass where they fell.

You walk to the line. You set your feet. You feel the wind on your face, and you know you cannot stop it; you can only learn to read it. You do not know what is written about this arrow, and you will not know until it lands. You take a breath, and whether you send it upward or simply let it go into the air, it steadies you.

Then you draw. Not because the arrow is guaranteed to hit. Because you are an archer, and this is what archers do.

The wind is blowing. The page is open. The target is far away.

Breathe, and draw.

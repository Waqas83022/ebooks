# Chapter 10: The room you have to act

In the spring of 1870, a twenty-eight-year-old American named William James was in serious trouble.

He had trained as a doctor but did not want to practice. His health was poor. He was living in his parents' house in Cambridge, Massachusetts, unsure what to do with his life, and he had sunk into a depression so deep that, by his own later account, he had thought about ending it.

Part of what was crushing him was an idea. James had been studying science and philosophy, and he had come to find the case for determinism overwhelming. If every event was the product of earlier causes, then so was every human thought and every human choice. A person was a kind of machine, running out a program set in motion long before birth. And if that was true, what was the point of striving? His own will, which he desperately needed to climb out of his despair, seemed to him an illusion.

Then he read an essay by a French philosopher, Charles Renouvier, who described free will as the act of holding on to a thought because you choose to, when you could be thinking something else. James was struck by it. On the thirtieth of April, 1870, he wrote in his diary that the previous day had been a turning point in his life. He had decided, he wrote, that his first act of free will would be to believe in free will.

It was not an argument, and James knew it. He had not proved determinism false. He had simply decided to live as though his choices mattered, and to see what happened.

What happened was that he slowly recovered. Not overnight, and not completely; he struggled with low moods all his life. But he never sank so deep again. He went on to become one of the founders of modern psychology and one of the warmest, most readable philosophers America has produced.

* * *

I tell you this story not because it settles the question of free will. It does not. Philosophers and scientists are arguing about it more fiercely than ever.

Some argue that free will is an illusion. The neuroscientist Robert Sapolsky, in a book called *Determined*, published in 2023, makes the case that every human action is the product of biology and circumstance: genes, hormones, brain chemistry, childhood, culture and the events of the previous few seconds, all the way down. There is no self floating free of all that and choosing on its own.

Interestingly, Sapolsky does not conclude that we should stop trying. He concludes that we should become far more compassionate, toward others and toward ourselves, because nobody chose the forces that shaped them. The person who failed did not freely choose to fail. The person who succeeded did not freely choose to succeed either.

Many philosophers disagree with him. A large group, often called *compatibilists*, argue that the freedom that matters to us was never about escaping causes. It is about acting on your own reasons and values without being forced or tricked. A woman who studies medicine because she cares about healing is acting freely in this sense, even though her caring has causes. A woman who studies medicine because her father will disown her if she does not is less free, even though both choices have causes.

We saw in Chapter 8 that a caused action can still be a cause, so the practical question in front of you stays the same whichever side is right: what should I do? And underneath it is a second question that, for most people, matters far more than the philosophy.

Not *am I free?* but *how much room do I have to act?*

* * *

Room is a better word than freedom for most of real life, because room comes in different sizes, and three things shape its size more than any others: money, time and obligations.

Imagine two people who both want to leave jobs they hate and retrain for a new career.

The first is single, has no children, has savings that would cover a year without income and has parents who would let her move home if things went badly. Her room is large. She can take a risk, because failure would be survivable.

The second has two young children, a mortgage, a mother who depends on her for daily care and no savings to speak of. Her room is much smaller. She has the same wish, and perhaps the same courage. But a choice that would be bold for the first woman could be reckless for her.

If we told both of them simply to *follow your dreams* or *take responsibility for your life*, we would be talking nonsense to one of them. They do not have the same amount of room. Pretending they do is not inspiring. It is cruel.

* * *

Money does more than limit our options. It reaches inside our heads.

In 2013, a team of researchers including Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir published a study of sugarcane farmers in India. These farmers are paid once a year, at harvest. In the months before harvest, money is tight; just after harvest, it is plentiful. The researchers gave the same farmers a set of mental tests at both times. The farmers performed noticeably worse before the harvest, when they were short of money, than after it, when they were not. They were the same people with the same intelligence. The difference was the weight of their worries.

The researchers' explanation was that scarcity takes up mental space. When you are worried about rent, school fees or the next meal, part of your mind is always busy with that worry, and less of it is free for planning, learning and choosing well. Time pressure and heavy caring duties seem to work in a similar way. A person juggling three jobs and a sick parent is not only short of hours. She is short of the calm attention that good decisions require.

This matters enormously when we judge ourselves, or others, for "not trying hard enough." Sometimes what looks like a lack of effort is a lack of room. The bow is being drawn in a very small space, with very little air.

* * *

But notice that the second woman is not without room. Hers is smaller, and its shape is different. Perhaps she can take a course in the evenings. Perhaps she can save slowly for two years. Perhaps she can find a role that bridges her old career and the new one. Perhaps she can ask for help she has been too proud to ask for. Her choices are real. They are harder, and they will take longer.

Taking responsibility begins with an honest account of the room you have to act.

It helps, when the room feels small, to name exactly what is making it small. Constraints are far less overwhelming when they are named one by one than when they are felt all at once as a fog of impossibility.

Some constraints are *outside you and fixed*, at least for now: a law, an illness, a family duty you will not abandon, the state of the economy.

Some are *outside you but negotiable*: an employer who might agree to flexible hours, relatives who might share a responsibility, a bank that might reschedule a loan, a deadline that might move if you ask.

Some are *inside you*: habits, fears, beliefs about what people like you are allowed to do, an old story about your own limits. These are often the hardest to see and, sometimes, the most possible to change.

And some are simply *missing information*. You do not know whether something is possible because you have never asked.

When you sort your constraints this way, the room usually turns out larger than it felt. Never unlimited. But a person who believes they have no room at all is often looking at a mixture of real walls and painted ones, and cannot tell them apart in the dark.

* * *

The psychiatrist Viktor Frankl survived the Nazi concentration camps and wrote about them in a short book called *Man's Search for Meaning*. One of his central observations was that even there, when everything else had been taken, a person kept one last freedom: the freedom to choose the attitude he would take toward his circumstances.

That idea is often misused, so I want to handle it carefully. Frankl was not saying that attitude is all that matters, or that suffering people just need a better outlook. He was a prisoner in a place built to strip human beings of every other kind of room. He survived partly by chance, and he knew it. His point was that even in the smallest room any human being has ever been forced into, some room remained. Not enough to save himself. Enough to remain himself.

If that was true in the camps, it is true in your situation too. Your room may be small. It is almost never zero.

* * *

Here is the most hopeful thing in this chapter. Room is not fixed. You can make it larger.

Every month of savings is room. Every new skill is room. Every person who knows and trusts you is room. Every year of good health is room. Every piece of information about your options is room. None of these guarantee anything. Each one gives your future self more choices, more ways to respond when the wind changes, more doors to try when one closes.

This is one of the most practical answers I know to the questions that began this book. If you cannot control the outcome, cannot read your destiny and cannot be sure your choices are free in the deepest philosophical sense, what *can* you do? You can widen your room, so that whatever happens, you will have more ways to meet it.

The archer did not choose her height, the length of her arms, the first bow she was handed or the wind on the day of the contest. Those were given. Within them she chose where to stand, how to train, which arrows to carry and, finally, where to aim. And over the years, by those choices, she made her own room larger: a stronger draw, a steadier hand, a better eye for the wind.

William James could not prove he was free. He decided to act as though his room was real, and then spent his life making it larger. That is as good an answer to the question as anyone has found.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Your room may be small. It is almost never zero.*

**Practice.** Think of a choice you feel stuck on. List every constraint you can think of, then sort each one into four groups: outside me and fixed, outside me but negotiable, inside me, and missing information. Pick one item from the negotiable or missing-information groups and take one step on it this week: ask the question, have the conversation, look up the answer.

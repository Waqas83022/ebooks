# Chapter 10: The room you have to act

In the spring of 1870, a twenty-eight-year-old American named William James was in serious trouble.

He had trained as a doctor but did not want to practice. His health was poor. He was living in his parents' house in Cambridge, Massachusetts, unsure what to do with his life, and he had sunk into a depression so deep that, by his own later account, he thought about ending his life.

Part of what was crushing him was an idea. James had been studying science and philosophy, and he had come to find the case for determinism overwhelming. If every event was the result of earlier causes, then so was every human thought and every human choice. A person was a kind of machine, running out a program set in motion long before they were born. And if that was true, James wondered, what was the point of striving? What was the point of anything? His own will, which he desperately needed in order to climb out of his despair, seemed to him to be an illusion.

Then he read an essay by a French philosopher, Charles Renouvier, who defined free will in a way James found striking: the sustaining of a thought *because I choose to* when I might have other thoughts. And on the thirtieth of April, 1870, James wrote in his diary:

*I think that yesterday was a crisis in my life... My first act of free will shall be to believe in free will.*

It was not an argument. James knew it was not an argument. He had not proved that determinism was false. He had simply decided that he would live as though his choices mattered, and see what happened.

What happened was that he slowly recovered. Not overnight, and not completely; he struggled with low moods for the rest of his life. But he never again sank as deep. He went on to become one of the founders of modern psychology and one of the most humane and readable philosophers America has produced. Much of his later work was about the practical difference that beliefs make in the way people live.

* * *

I tell you this story not because it settles the question of free will. It does not. Philosophers and scientists are still arguing about it, more fiercely than ever.

Some argue, as James feared, that free will is an illusion. The neuroscientist Robert Sapolsky, in a book called *Determined*, published in 2023, makes the case that every human action is the product of biology and circumstance: of genes, hormones, brain chemistry, childhood, culture and the events of the last few seconds, all the way down. There is no room, he says, for a self that floats free of all that and makes choices on its own.

Interestingly, Sapolsky does not conclude that we should all stop trying. He concludes that we should become far more compassionate, toward others and toward ourselves, because nobody chose the forces that made them who they are. The person who failed did not freely choose to fail. Neither, for that matter, did the person who succeeded freely choose to succeed.

Many other philosophers disagree with him. A large group, often called *compatibilists*, argue that the kind of freedom that matters to us was never about escaping causes. It is about acting on your own reasons, in line with your own values, without being forced or tricked. A person who chooses to study medicine because she cares about healing people is acting freely in this sense, even if her caring has causes. A person who studies medicine because her father will disown her if she does not is less free, even if both choices have causes.

I am not going to settle this debate in a chapter, and I do not need to. Here is the thing I find most useful about it: almost nothing about how you should live tomorrow depends on how it is settled.

* * *

Think about what actually happens when you face a hard decision. Should you leave your job? Should you apply to study abroad? Should you have a difficult conversation with someone you love?

You deliberate. You weigh reasons. You imagine outcomes. You feel the pull of different possibilities. Whatever is going on in your brain underneath, and whatever philosophers eventually decide it means, you cannot step outside of this process. You cannot sit back and watch yourself decide like someone watching a film. Even the decision to "let fate decide" is a decision, and it has consequences like any other.

As we saw in Chapter 8, a caused action can still be a cause. Your deliberation is part of the chain that produces the result. So even if James's worst fear were true, even if every choice is the product of causes, the practical question in front of you is still the same one: what should I do?

And there is a second practical question underneath it, one that I think is much more important than the metaphysical one. It is not *am I free?* It is *how much room do I have to act?*

* * *

Room is a better word than freedom for most of real life, because room comes in different sizes.

Imagine two people who both want to leave jobs they hate and train for a new career.

The first is single, has no children, has savings that would cover a year without income and has parents who would let her move back home if things went badly. Her room to act is large. She can take a risk, because the cost of failure is survivable.

The second has two young children, a mortgage, a parent who depends on her for care and no savings to speak of. Her room to act is much smaller. She has the same wish, and perhaps the same courage. But a choice that would be bold for the first woman might be reckless for her.

If we told both of them simply to *follow your dreams* or *take responsibility for your life*, we would be speaking nonsense to one of them. They do not have the same amount of room. Pretending they do is not inspiring. It is cruel.

But notice that the second woman is not without room. Hers is smaller, and its shape is different. Perhaps she can begin a course in the evenings. Perhaps she can build savings slowly for two years. Perhaps she can find a role that bridges the old career and the new. Perhaps she can ask for help she has been too proud to ask for. Her choices are real. They are just harder, and they will take longer.

> *Taking responsibility begins with an honest account of the room you have to act.*

* * *

It helps, when the room feels small, to name exactly what is making it small. Constraints become much less overwhelming when they are named one by one, instead of felt all at once as a vague weight of impossibility.

Some constraints are *outside you* and *fixed*, at least for now: a law, an illness, a family duty you are not willing to abandon, the state of the economy.

Some are *outside you* but *negotiable*: an employer who might agree to flexible hours, a family that might share a responsibility, a bank that might restructure a loan, a deadline that might be moved if you ask.

Some are *inside you*: habits, fears, beliefs about what people like you are allowed to do, an old story about your own limits. These can be the hardest to see and, sometimes, the most possible to change.

And some are simply *missing information*. You do not know whether something is possible because you have never asked.

When you sort your constraints this way, the room often turns out to be larger than it felt. Not unlimited. Never unlimited. But a person who believes they have no room at all is usually looking at a mixture of real walls and painted ones, and cannot tell them apart in the dark.

> *A choice becomes clearer when its constraints are named.*

* * *

There is a sentence by the psychiatrist Viktor Frankl that is quoted so often it has almost been worn smooth. He wrote it after surviving the Nazi concentration camps, in a book called *Man's Search for Meaning*:

*Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms—to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one's own way.*

I want to treat this sentence carefully, because it is often misused. Frankl was not saying that attitude is all that matters, or that people who suffer simply need a better attitude. He was a prisoner in a place designed to strip human beings of every other kind of room. His point was that even there, in the smallest room any human being has ever been forced into, he found that some room remained. Not enough to save himself; he survived partly by chance, and he knew it. But enough to remain himself.

If that was true in the camps, then it is true in your situation too. Your room may be small. It is almost never zero.

* * *

There is one more thing I want to say about room, and it is the most hopeful thing in this chapter.

Room is not fixed. You can make it larger.

Every month of savings is room. Every new skill is room. Every person who knows and trusts you is room. Every year of good health is room. Every piece of information you gather about your options is room. None of these guarantee anything. But each one gives your future self more choices, more ways to respond when the wind changes, more doors to walk through when one closes.

This is one of the most practical answers I know to the questions that began this book. If you cannot control the outcome, and you cannot read your destiny, and you cannot be sure your choices are free in the deepest philosophical sense, what *can* you do? You can widen your room. You can make sure that, whatever happens, you will have more ways to respond to it.

The archer did not choose her height, the length of her arms, the bow she was first handed or the wind on the day of the contest. Those were given. But within them, she chose where to stand, how to train, which arrows to carry and, finally, where to aim. And over years, by her choices, she made her own room larger: a stronger draw, a steadier hand, a better eye for the wind.

William James could not prove that he was free. He decided to act as though his room was real, and then he spent his life making it larger. That, I think, is as good an answer to the question as anyone has found.

> *Freedom is not only something to believe in. It is a room you can make larger.*

List every constraint you can think of, then sort each one into four groups: outside me and fixed, outside me but negotiable, inside me, and missing information. Pick one item from the "negotiable" or "missing information" group and take one step on it this week: ask the question, have the conversation, look up the answer.

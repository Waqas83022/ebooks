# Chapter 1: The contract nobody signed

Let me introduce you to Sana. She is a composite, built from many people I have known and many more I have heard about, but you will recognize her.

Sana is twenty-seven. For two years she has wanted one job. It is an analyst position at the company in her city that everyone talks about, the one with the glass building and the good salary and the reputation that opens other doors. She does not want it casually. She wants it the way people want things at night.

So she prepares. She reads the company's annual reports until she can quote them. She takes an online course in the software they use. She finds two former employees and asks them, nervously, over coffee she pays for, what the interviewers like to hear. She practices her answers in front of the bathroom mirror until her younger brother bangs on the door. On the morning of the final interview, her mother prays for her, and Sana prays too, more seriously than she has prayed in a long time.

The interview goes well. She knows it goes well. She answers the hard question about the failed project with honesty and a little humor, and the senior manager smiles. On the bus home she lets herself imagine, just for a few minutes, the first day: the badge on the lanyard, the call to her father, the life that would start from there.

Nine days later, the email arrives. It is short. *After careful consideration, we have decided to move forward with another candidate. We were impressed by your preparation and wish you every success.*

She reads it standing in the kitchen. Then she reads it again, as though the words might rearrange themselves.

The email gives her a decision. Within the hour, her mind has written a verdict.

*You were never good enough. You fooled yourself. All that work was for nothing. Maybe there was no point in hoping. Maybe there was no point in praying. Maybe effort doesn't matter at all.*

A single result has started to claim far more authority than it has earned.

* * *

Now let me introduce you to the other candidate. His name is Daniel, and he is a composite too.

Daniel also prepared for months. He also read the reports, practiced the answers, lay awake the night before. Daniel's grandmother also prayed. On the morning of his interview, Daniel was just as frightened as Sana.

Perhaps Daniel was a slightly better fit. Perhaps he had used the company's particular software in a previous role. Perhaps his last answer happened to touch on a project the manager was worried about that week. Perhaps the manager went to the same university and felt, without quite knowing it, a small warmth of recognition. Perhaps it was simply that Daniel's interview was at ten in the morning and Sana's was at four in the afternoon, when everyone in the room was tired.

Sana will never know. That is one of the cruelest features of disappointment: it so rarely comes with reasons attached.

Here is what we do know. There was one job. Two people prepared seriously for it. Only one could receive it. And the result, whatever it was based on, could tell the world who got the job. It could not tell the world which of them was worth more as a human being. It could not even tell the world, with certainty, which of them was the better candidate. It could only tell us what happened.

> *A result tells you what happened. It does not tell you what you are worth.*

* * *

Why does a moment like Sana's hurt so much? Not just disappoint, but wound, as if something has been broken?

I think it is because most of us carry a contract we have never read.

It goes something like this. *If I work hard enough, and want it sincerely enough, and do things the right way, then I will get what I am working for.* Some of us add a clause: *and if I pray, and if I am a good person, then the universe, or God, will make sure of it.*

Nobody hands us this contract to sign. We absorb it. We absorb it in school, where effort is rewarded with marks and stars and the teacher's approval. We absorb it from our parents, who tell us to work hard because they love us and because, in their own lives, it was the best advice they had. We absorb it from every story where the underdog trains, struggles and wins in the final scene. We absorb it from religious teachings, too, though often in a simplified form that the teachings themselves never quite promised.

And it is not a bad contract to teach children. It is mostly true, in the way that many useful things are mostly true. Children who work harder usually do learn more. People who prepare usually do perform better. Effort usually does improve the odds.

The problem is the word *will*. The contract says that if you do your part, the result *will* follow. Life has never agreed to that clause. Life speaks the language of odds, not guarantees. And when we meet the gap between the two for the first time, as an adult, with something enormous at stake, we do not experience it as a lesson about probability. We experience it as betrayal.

Sana is not only sad that she did not get the job. She feels cheated. She kept her side of the bargain. Where is the other side?

* * *

In the 1960s, a psychologist named Melvin Lerner began to notice something unsettling about the way people respond to suffering. He saw that people often blame victims for misfortunes they clearly did not cause: that we look at someone who has been hurt by pure bad luck and quietly decide they must have done something to deserve it.

To test this, Lerner and his colleague Carolyn Simmons ran an experiment, published in 1966. Women watched what they believed was a fellow student receiving painful electric shocks as part of a learning task. (She was an actor; there were no real shocks.) The observers could not stop it. And when they could do nothing to help her, many of them did something strange. They began to think less of her. They rated her as less attractive and less admirable than observers who had been able to change her situation, or who learned she would be compensated.

Lerner's explanation became famous as the *just-world hypothesis*. Most of us, he argued, need to believe that the world is basically fair, that people get what they deserve and deserve what they get. This belief is so precious to us that when we see undeserved suffering, we would rather rewrite the victim than give up the belief. If she is suffering, she must somehow have earned it. Otherwise the world is a place where suffering can fall on anyone, including us, at any time, for no reason at all. And that thought is almost unbearable.

The research on this has been debated and refined for decades, as good research always is. But the core insight has stayed with me, because I think it explains what happens inside a person like Sana.

When the result goes against us, the just-world belief does not disappear. It turns around and points at us.

If the world is fair, and I lost, then I must have deserved to lose. If I had really been good enough, I would have won. If I had prayed with more sincerity, it would have been answered. If I had wanted it more, worked harder, been better, it would be mine. So the failure is mine, entirely, and it says something about me, entirely.

That is the voice that wrote Sana's verdict in her kitchen. It is not the voice of truth. It is the voice of the contract, refusing to admit it was never real.

* * *

There is a second way people respond when the contract breaks, and it is the opposite of the first.

Instead of blaming themselves, they blame the whole idea of effort. *I did everything right and it didn't matter. So nothing matters. Hard work is a lie they tell you to keep you running. Luck is everything. Or destiny is everything. Either way, I am done trying.*

This is the chorus of that song: all the trying, all the distance, and in the end it did not even matter. It feels like hard-won wisdom. It feels like you have finally seen through the trick.

But notice that it is the same contract in disguise. Both responses assume that effort is supposed to guarantee the outcome. The first says: the guarantee is real, so I must have failed. The second says: the guarantee is fake, so effort is worthless. Neither one considers the third possibility, which happens to be the true one.

Effort was never a guarantee. It was a contribution.

> *Effort changes the odds. It does not sign a contract with the outcome.*

* * *

The people who understand this best have always been farmers.

No farmer has ever believed that ploughing guarantees rain. A farmer knows the whole list of things that can ruin a season: drought, flood, locusts, hail, disease, a bad market at harvest time. Every one of them is outside the farmer's control. Some years the farmer does everything right and the crop fails anyway. Farmers in every culture have stories, songs and prayers about exactly this.

And every farmer ploughs.

They do not plough because ploughing guarantees a harvest. They plough because not ploughing guarantees there will not be one. Effort does not decide the outcome. It decides whether an outcome is possible at all. The rain may or may not come; but if it comes, it will fall on a prepared field or an empty one, and that part belongs to the farmer.

This is what the contract got wrong. It treated effort as a price we pay for a result, like money handed over at a shop counter. But effort is more like planting. You put something real into the ground. You give it every chance you can. And then you wait under the same sky as everyone else.

* * *

I want to be careful here, because there is a cheap version of this idea that does more harm than good.

The cheap version says: *Don't be sad. You can't control the outcome, so let it go.* That is not what I am saying. Sana is allowed to be sad. She is allowed to be angry. She wanted something good, she worked hard for it and she did not get it. Grief is the correct response to that. It would be strange if she felt nothing.

What I am saying is narrower, and I think more useful. Sana's sadness is appropriate. Sana's verdict is not.

She is right to grieve the job. She is wrong to conclude that her preparation was worthless, that she is not good enough, that hope was foolish or that effort does not matter. Those conclusions go far beyond what the evidence can support. One email from one company about one position cannot carry that much weight. It was never built to.

There is a difference between what we deserve and what we receive, and it helps to keep the two words apart. *Deserve* is a moral word. It belongs to the world of fairness and character and effort. *Receive* is a word about events. It belongs to the world of odds, competition, timing and other people's decisions.

Sana deserved respect for how she prepared. She deserved to be taken seriously, and she was. She did not receive the job. Both of these things are true at the same time, and the second does not cancel the first.

> *Hard work deserves respect, even when it does not receive applause.*

* * *

So what did Sana's effort actually do, if it did not get her the job?

I do not want to rush into consolation here, because consolation offered too quickly sounds like dismissal. But it is worth looking honestly at the ledger.

Her effort got her into the final round, out of what was probably a very large pile of applications. That is not nothing. It moved the odds from tiny to real. Her effort taught her the software, which she now knows and will carry to the next application. It taught her how to talk about her own failures with honesty, which is a rare skill. It introduced her to two people who work in the industry she wants to enter, who now know her name. And it showed her something about herself that she did not know before: that when she wants something, she is capable of going after it with discipline and courage.

None of this is the job. I am not pretending it is. If you had offered Sana all of these things in exchange for the job, she would have said no in a heartbeat.

But none of it is nothing, either. And all of it is still hers. The company kept the position. It did not get to keep what she built while trying to earn it.

* * *

Here is where I want to leave the contract.

You were taught a deal in which effort buys outcomes. It was taught to you by people who loved you, and it was mostly true, and it got you a long way. But it was never the whole truth, and there is no shame in discovering that as an adult. Everyone who aims at anything that matters discovers it sooner or later.

The truth is less tidy and, I think, more honest. Your effort is real. It matters. It is the one part of the story that is entirely yours. And it is also one force among many. The wind is real too. So are other people's choices. So, if you believe in it, is whatever is written.

The rest of this book is about how to live inside that truth without becoming either the person who blames herself for every loss or the person who stops trying altogether. There is a third way to stand. The archer knows it.

She draws with everything she has. She lets go. And whatever happens to the arrow, she does not hand her worth to the wind.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Effort changes the odds. It does not sign a contract with the outcome.*

*A result tells you what happened. It does not tell you what you are worth.*

*No farmer has ever believed that ploughing guarantees rain. Every farmer ploughs.*

**Practice.** Write down three things you once expected life, work or love to give you if you did your part. Next to each, write which kind of promise it really was: a real commitment someone made to you, a reasonable expectation, or a deep and honest wish. Notice how many of your heaviest disappointments came from the third kind being treated as the first.

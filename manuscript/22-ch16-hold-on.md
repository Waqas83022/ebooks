# Chapter 16: Hold on, or let go

On the twenty-ninth of October 1941, in the darkest year of the Second World War, Winston Churchill visited his old school, Harrow, and gave a short speech to the boys. One part of it has been repeated ever since, on posters, in locker rooms and in a thousand motivational talks: his call never to give in, never, never, never, in anything great or small.

Almost nobody remembers how the sentence ended. Churchill added an exception. Never give in, he said, except to convictions of honor and good sense.

I find that ending wonderful. The most famous speech about never giving up in the English language contains an exception clause. Even Churchill, at a moment when stubbornness was saving his country, knew that persistence and wisdom are not the same thing. There are times when honor and good sense tell you to stop.

The trouble is that nobody tells you exactly when those times are.

* * *

We are surrounded by two sets of advice that flatly contradict each other, and both are delivered with complete confidence.

One set says: never give up. Winners never quit. The moment you want to stop is the moment just before the breakthrough.

The other says: know when to walk away. Don't throw good money after bad. Let go of what isn't working. Doing the same thing and expecting a different result is a kind of madness.

Both are true sometimes. Neither is true always. And most of the people who give this advice decide which version to give based on how things turned out, which, as we saw in Chapter 7, is exactly the wrong way to judge a decision. If you persisted and succeeded, you were a hero who never gave up. If you persisted and failed, you were a fool who could not let go. If you quit and something better came along, you were wise. If you quit and regretted it, you were weak.

So let us think about this more carefully than the posters do.

* * *

The first thing to know is that our minds lean toward continuing, even when continuing makes no sense.

In 1985, the psychologists Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer ran a neat experiment at a university theater. People buying season tickets for a series of plays were randomly charged one of three prices: full price, or one of two discounts. The tickets were otherwise identical. Then the researchers simply counted how many plays each group attended.

Over the first half of the season, the people who had paid full price went to significantly more plays than those who had paid less.

Why? The money was already spent. It could not be recovered by going, or lost by staying home. The only sensible question was *do I want to see a play tonight?* But the people who had paid more felt they had more invested, and did not want to "waste" it. They kept going to justify what they had already put in.

Economists call this the *sunk cost fallacy*. Money, time and effort that have already been spent are gone, whatever we do next. Yet we treat them as a debt that obliges us to continue. *I've put five years into this degree. I've sunk so much into this shop. I've given this relationship half my life.* The more we have invested, the harder it becomes to stop, even when stopping is plainly right.

That is why I have learned to distrust persistence when it is the only argument I have for continuing. Before you call persistence a virtue, ask what it is preserving.

* * *

The second thing to know is that letting go of a goal that has truly moved out of reach is not weakness. Often it is health.

The psychologist Carsten Wrosch and his colleagues have spent years studying people who face goals they cannot reach: parents caring for a seriously ill child who must give up career plans, older people who can no longer do work they once loved, young people whose path is suddenly blocked. In studies published in 2003, they found that people who were better able to let go of goals that had become unattainable tended to report better well-being: less distress, fewer signs of depression. People who could not only let go but also *re-engage*, putting their energy into new goals, tended to do better still.

These studies show associations, not strict causes, and they cannot tell any one person when to stop. But they do challenge the idea that refusing to let go is always the most admirable thing a person can do. Sometimes the strongest move is to open your hand around one goal so that it is free to hold another.

* * *

The third thing to know is that *give up* is not one choice. It is several.

We usually talk as if there are only two options, continue or quit. In real life there are at least four.

You can *continue as you are*, trusting that the same approach will work given more time.

You can *change the method*, keeping the goal but trying a different way to reach it.

You can *pause*, setting the goal aside for a season while you rest, save, heal or deal with other duties, with a plan to return.

And you can *leave the goal*, choosing to put your energy somewhere else.

Many people who feel trapped between "never give up" and "let it go" actually need the second or third option. They do not need to abandon the goal. They need to stop doing what is not working, or to rest.

* * *

How do you know which you need? There is no formula, and I will not pretend there is. But some questions clear the fog.

*Is there evidence of progress?* Not success, which can take a long time, but progress: getting closer, getting better, getting more specific feedback, more interest, more replies. If there is progress, even slow progress, that is a strong reason to continue. If there has been none for a long time despite real effort, that is a strong reason at least to change the method.

*Is the road hard, or is it closed?* This is the most important distinction, and it is often blurred. A hard road is one where success is possible but difficult: you need more skill, more attempts, more time, more luck. A closed road is one where success is no longer possible, or no longer possible for you: a clear and final no from another person, an age limit, a rule that will not change, an opportunity that has gone. Hard roads need persistence. Closed roads need the courage to turn around. The cruelest mistakes come from confusing the two: giving up too soon on a hard road, or spending years pushing at a closed one. A hard road and a closed road need two different kinds of courage.

*What is it costing?* Every goal has a price, in money but also in health, relationships, time and the people who depend on you. Some prices are worth paying. Some are not. Often the people around you can see the cost more clearly than you can. Ask them.

*Is the value still alive?* Over the years we change. A goal that once expressed who we were may no longer do so, and we keep chasing it out of habit, pride or a promise made long ago. It is worth asking honestly: if I were starting today, knowing what I know now, would I choose this?

*Can the value be reached another way?* This question opens more doors than any other.

* * *

The psychologist Angela Duckworth, whose research made perseverance famous under the name *grit*, makes a point that people who quote her often miss. Our goals, she suggests, are arranged in a kind of ladder. At the top sits a single, deep, long-lasting purpose: what we care about most. Below it are many smaller goals, which are only means to that end. The top of the ladder is worth holding stubbornly. The lower rungs should be held more loosely. When one is blocked, it is often wise to swap it for another that serves the same purpose.

Let me give you an example. Maya is a composite.

Maya wanted to be a doctor for as long as she could remember. She applied to medical school three times, and three times she was turned down. Each time she improved her application. Each time the competition was fierce and the places few. After the third rejection she sat with the question we have been asking: is this road hard, or is it closed?

After much grief and much advice, she decided that for her, at least for now, it was closed. Her savings were gone. Her family needed her to work. The odds of a fourth attempt succeeding were real but small.

Then she asked the deeper question. Why did she want to be a doctor? When she was honest, the answer was not the title or the white coat. It was that she wanted to spend her life helping people who were ill and frightened, using science to do it.

Once she named that, she could see that medicine was one road to that place, but not the only one. She trained as a nurse. Years later she specialized in caring for people with cancer. She spends her days doing exactly what she once dreamed of doing: helping frightened, ill people, using science. She is not a doctor, and she still feels a small ache when she sees a white coat. But the reason she wanted the white coat in the first place is alive in her every single day.

The same move appears everywhere once you look for it. The footballer whose knee will not hold up becomes the coach who makes other people's careers. The researcher who cannot find an academic post takes her skills to a hospital, a charity or a government office, and finds her questions are still alive there. The shopkeeper who closes his shop keeps what he loved about it, the conversations, the trust of regulars, the feeling of running his own day, and builds it into something new. Changing direction can protect the value that first made you begin.

Not every story ends this neatly, and I will not pretend otherwise. Some goals have no substitute that feels anywhere near as good. Some values can only be served in one particular way. Sometimes, when you leave a goal, you lose something nothing will fully replace, and that loss deserves real grief. But far more often than we expect, the thing we were really reaching for can be reached by another road.

* * *

There is one more practice that helps more than any other, because it protects you from making this decision in your worst moment.

Decide in advance.

Before you begin a hard goal, or at a calm moment along the way, write a short review rule. What evidence would tell you to keep going as you are? What evidence would tell you to change the method? What costs will you refuse to pay, whatever happens? And when will you review this: after how many attempts, how much time, how much money?

Then, when the hard moment comes, the night after the third rejection, the month the savings run low, you will not be deciding alone and exhausted. You will be deciding with your calmer, clearer self, who saw this moment coming and left you a note.

It will not make the decision painless. Nothing will. But it will make it yours, and not the decision of your fear, your pride or the money you have already spent.

* * *

Churchill was right. Never give in, never, never, except to honor and good sense.

The archer knows both halves of that sentence. There are seasons to keep drawing at a distant target through rain and wind, because she is getting closer and the target is worth it. And there are seasons to lower the bow, look around the field and see that her arrows would fly truer somewhere else. Neither is weakness. The only weakness is refusing to ask the question.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*Quit the road if you must. Keep the reason.*

**Practice.** For one goal you are unsure about, write a review rule. What evidence would tell you to continue? What evidence would tell you to change the method? What costs will you not accept? What deeper value does this goal serve, and what is one other road toward that value? Set a date to read it again, and keep it.

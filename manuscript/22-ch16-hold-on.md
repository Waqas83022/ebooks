# Chapter 16: Hold on, or let go

On the twenty-ninth of October 1941, in the darkest year of the Second World War, Winston Churchill visited his old school, Harrow, and gave a short speech to the boys. One part of it has been quoted ever since, on posters, in locker rooms and in a thousand motivational talks:

*Never give in, never give in, never, never, never, never—in nothing, great or small, large or petty—never give in...*

Almost nobody quotes the rest of the sentence. Churchill went on: *...except to convictions of honour and good sense.*

I find that ending wonderful. The most famous speech about never giving up in the English language contains an exception clause. Even Churchill, at a moment when stubbornness was saving his country, knew that persistence was not the same thing as wisdom. There are times when honor and good sense tell you to stop.

The trouble is that nobody tells you exactly when those times are.

* * *

We are surrounded by two sets of advice that contradict each other completely, and both are delivered with total confidence.

*Never give up. Winners never quit and quitters never win. Keep going. The moment you're ready to quit is usually the moment right before the miracle happens.*

And: *Know when to walk away. Don't throw good money after bad. Let go of what isn't working. The definition of madness is doing the same thing and expecting different results.*

Both sets of sayings are true sometimes. Neither is true always. And most of the people who give this advice decide which version to give based on how things turned out, which, as we saw in Chapter 7, is exactly the wrong way to judge a decision. If you persisted and succeeded, you were a hero who never gave up. If you persisted and failed, you were a fool who could not let go. If you quit and something better came along, you were wise. If you quit and regretted it, you were weak.

So let us try to think about this more carefully than the posters do.

* * *

The first thing to know is that our minds are biased toward continuing, even when continuing makes no sense.

In 1985, the psychologists Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer ran a simple and clever experiment at a university theater. People buying season tickets for a series of plays were randomly given one of three prices: full price, or one of two discounts. The tickets were otherwise identical. Then the researchers simply counted how many plays each group attended.

Over the first half of the season, the people who had paid full price went to significantly more plays than those who had received a discount.

Why? The money was already spent. It could not be recovered by attending, or lost by staying home. Rationally, the only question should have been: *do I want to see this play tonight?* But the people who had paid more felt they had more invested, and they did not want to "waste" it. They kept going to justify what they had already put in.

Economists call this the *sunk cost fallacy*. Money, time and effort that have already been spent are gone, whatever we do next. But we treat them as though they create a duty to continue. *I've already put five years into this degree. I've already spent so much on this business. I've already given this relationship so much of my life.* The more we have invested, the harder it becomes to stop, even when stopping is clearly right.

This is why I have come to distrust persistence when it is the only argument I have for continuing.

> *Before you call persistence a virtue, ask what it is preserving.*

* * *

The second thing to know is that letting go of a goal, when it is truly out of reach, is not a sign of weakness. Often it is a sign of health.

The psychologist Carsten Wrosch and his colleagues have spent years studying what happens to people who face goals they cannot attain: a parent caring for a child with a serious illness who must give up career plans, an older person who can no longer do the work they once did, a young person whose path is suddenly blocked. In a set of studies published in 2003, they found that people who were better able to disengage from goals that had become unattainable tended to report better well-being: less distress, fewer symptoms of depression. And people who could not only let go but also *re-engage*, finding new goals to put their energy into, tended to do better still.

These studies show associations, not strict causes, and they cannot tell any individual when to stop. But they do challenge the idea that the most admirable thing a person can ever do is refuse to let go. Sometimes the strongest thing is to release your grip on one goal so that your hands are free for another.

* * *

The third thing to know is that *give up* is not a single choice. It is several.

We usually talk as if there are only two options: continue or quit. But in real life, there are at least four.

You can *continue as you are*, doing the same thing, trusting that it will work given more time.

You can *change the method*, keeping the goal but trying a different way of getting there.

You can *pause*, setting the goal aside for a season while you recover, gather resources or deal with other things, with a plan to return.

And you can *leave the goal*, choosing to put your energy somewhere else entirely.

Many people who feel stuck between "never give up" and "let it go" are actually in need of the second or third option. They do not need to abandon the goal. They need to stop doing what is not working, or to rest.

* * *

So how do you know which one you need? There is no formula, and I will not pretend there is one. But there are some questions that I have found help to clear the fog.

*Is there evidence of progress?* Not success, which may take a long time, but progress: getting closer, getting better, getting more interest, getting more specific feedback. If there is progress, even slow progress, that is a strong reason to continue. If there has been no progress at all for a long time, despite real effort, that is a strong reason at least to change the method.

*Is the road hard, or is it closed?* This is the most important distinction, and it is often blurred. A hard road is one where success is possible but difficult: you need more skill, more time, more attempts, more luck. A closed road is one where success is not possible, or not possible for you: a clear and final refusal from another person, an age limit, a rule that will not change, an opportunity that no longer exists. Hard roads need persistence. Closed roads need the courage to turn around. And the cruelest mistakes come from treating one as the other: giving up on a hard road too soon, or spending years pushing at a closed one.

> *A hard road and a closed road need two different kinds of courage.*

*What is it costing?* Every goal has a price, not just in money, but in health, in relationships, in time that could have gone to other things, in the people who depend on you. Some prices are worth paying. Some are not. And it is often the people around you who can see the cost more clearly than you can. Ask them.

*Is the value still alive?* Sometimes, over years, we change. The goal that once expressed who we were no longer does. We keep pursuing it out of habit, or pride, or because we said we would. It is worth asking, honestly: *if I were starting today, knowing what I now know, would I choose this?*

*Can the value be served another way?* This is the question that opens the most doors, and I want to spend a moment on it.

* * *

The psychologist Angela Duckworth, whose research on grit made perseverance famous, makes an observation that is often missed by people who quote her. Our goals, she points out, are arranged in a kind of hierarchy. At the top is a single, deep, long-lasting purpose: what we care about most. Below it are many smaller goals, which are means to that end. The top of the hierarchy, she suggests, is worth holding onto stubbornly. The lower goals should be held more loosely. When one of them is blocked, it is often wise to swap it for another that serves the same purpose.

Let me give you an example. She is a composite.

Leila wanted to be a doctor for as long as she could remember. She applied to medical school three times, and three times she was rejected. Each time, she improved her application. Each time, the competition was fierce and the places few. After the third rejection, she sat with the question we have been asking: is this road hard, or is it closed?

She decided, after much grief and much advice, that for her, at least for now, it was closed. Her savings were gone. Her family needed her to work. The odds of a fourth attempt succeeding were real but small.

Then she asked the deeper question. Why did she want to be a doctor? When she was honest, the answer was not the title or the white coat. It was that she wanted to spend her life helping people who were ill and frightened, using science to do it.

Once she named that, she could see that medicine was one road to that place, but not the only one. She trained as a nurse. Years later, she specialized in caring for patients with cancer. She spends her days doing exactly what she once dreamed of doing: helping frightened, ill people, using science. She is not a doctor. She still feels a small ache when she thinks of the white coat. But the thing that made her want the white coat in the first place is alive in her every day.

> *Changing direction can protect the value that first made you begin.*

Not every story ends this neatly, and I do not want to pretend otherwise. Some goals have no substitute that feels anywhere near as good. Some values can only be served in one specific way. Sometimes, when you leave a goal, you lose something that nothing else will ever fully replace. That is a real loss, and it deserves real grief.

But far more often than we expect, the thing we were really reaching for can be reached by another road.

> *Quit the road if you must. Keep the reason.*

* * *

There is one more practice that I think helps more than any other, because it protects you from making this decision in your worst moment.

Decide in advance.

Before you begin a difficult goal, or at a calm moment along the way, write down a short review rule. What evidence would tell you to keep going as you are? What evidence would tell you to change your method? What costs are you not willing to pay, whatever happens? And when will you review this: after how many attempts, how much time, how much money?

Then, when the hard moment comes, the night after the third rejection, the day the money runs low, you are not making the decision alone and exhausted. You are making it with your calmer, clearer self, who saw this moment coming and left you a note.

It will not make the decision painless. Nothing will. But it will make it yours, and not the decision of your fear, or your pride, or the money you have already spent.

* * *

Churchill was right. Never give in, never, never, never, except to convictions of honour and good sense.

The archer knows both halves of that sentence. There are days to keep drawing at a distant target through rain and wind, because she is getting closer and the target is worth it. And there are days to lower the bow, look around the field and recognize that her arrows would fly truer somewhere else. Neither choice is weakness. The only weakness is refusing to ask the question.

# Chapter 9: Reading the page backward

In June 2005, Steve Jobs stood in front of the graduating class at Stanford University and told them three stories from his life. The first was about dropping out of college.

He had left Reed College after one semester, he said, because he could not see the point of spending his working-class parents' savings on courses he did not care about. But he stayed around the campus for another eighteen months, sleeping on friends' floors and sitting in on classes that interested him. One of them was calligraphy. He learned about typefaces, about the spaces between letters, about what makes beautiful printing beautiful. None of it had any practical use in his life.

Ten years later, when he and his colleagues were designing the first Macintosh computer, it all came back. The Mac, he said, became the first personal computer with beautiful typography, and if he had never wandered into that one class, it would not have.

Then he made the point that people have repeated ever since. You cannot join the dots of your life looking forward, he told the students. You can only join them looking back. So you have to trust that somehow they will connect in your future.

It is a lovely idea, and partly true. I want to look at it carefully, because inside it is the most important thing to understand about destiny as we actually feel it.

* * *

Notice what makes the calligraphy story work. It works because the Macintosh succeeded and Apple became one of the most famous companies on earth. Suppose instead that Jobs's first company had failed in its second year, as most new companies do. Would the calligraphy class still be a dot that connected? Almost certainly not. It would be a curious detail in the life of a man who dropped out of college, an interesting waste of time that nobody would think to mention.

The dots we connect, looking back, are the ones that lead to wherever we happen to be standing. They look like a path because we are at the end of it. If we had ended up somewhere else, a different set of dots would have lit up, and they would have looked like a path too.

That is not a criticism of Jobs. It is something every human being does. We are story-making creatures. When we look back over our lives, we cannot help seeing a shape, a direction, a sense that things were leading somewhere. And when the shape is strong enough, we give it a name. We call it destiny.

* * *

In the early 1970s, a young psychologist named Baruch Fischhoff ran a set of experiments that showed how powerfully knowing the ending changes the way we see the past.

In one of them, he gave people a short account of a historical conflict they knew nothing about: a war in the early nineteenth century between the British and the Gurkhas of Nepal. He described the background and asked how likely various outcomes had been. Some participants were told nothing about how the war ended. Others were told the British had won. Others that the Gurkhas had won. Others that it ended in stalemate, or in a peace settlement.

Whatever ending people were told, they judged that ending to have been more likely. They looked at the same evidence and found in it the seeds of whatever outcome they believed had happened. And they did not realize that knowing the ending had changed their judgment. They believed they would have seen it coming anyway.

In another study, Fischhoff and his colleague Ruth Beyth asked people to predict the results of President Nixon's historic visits to China and the Soviet Union in 1972, before the trips took place. Afterward, they asked the same people to recall what they had predicted. People tended to remember giving higher odds to the things that actually happened and lower odds to the things that did not. Their memories had moved toward the truth, and they had not felt them move.

Fischhoff gave this tendency a name I find almost poetic. He called it *creeping determinism*. Once we know how something turned out, the past begins to look as though it could only have turned out that way. The feeling of inevitability creeps backward into moments that, at the time, were genuinely open.

The past looks inevitable only because you are standing at the end of it.

* * *

Why does this matter in a book about destiny?

Because much of what we feel as destiny is creeping determinism. It is the natural feeling that comes from looking at our lives from the end of the road. It does not prove the road was fixed. It does not prove it was open, either. It simply tells us that the human mind, looking back, draws paths.

People who believe in destiny for deep reasons, religious or philosophical, need not be troubled by this. Their belief does not rest on a feeling; it rests on their faith or their reasoning. But it helps to know that the *feeling* of "it was always going to happen" will arrive whether or not anything was ever written. It arrives for everyone, about everything, once the ending is known.

There is a word in Arabic, *maktub*, which means "it is written." It has traveled far beyond the Arabic-speaking world. People say it with a certain peace after something has happened. I love the word. But notice the tense. It is almost always said about the past.

* * *

This matters most when we look back at our failures.

When Sara replayed her final interview in the weeks after the rejection, it began to seem obvious that she had never really had a chance. Of course they chose Daniel. She had been too nervous. Her answer about teamwork was weak. She should have known from the manager's face. A month earlier, walking out of that same interview, she had been sure it had gone well. Nothing about the interview had changed. Only the ending had arrived and started rewriting the story backward.

The same thing happens to the founder whose business closes (*it was obviously a bad idea from the start*), to the patient whose treatment did not work (*I should have known*), to the couple whose marriage ended (*the signs were always there*). Some of those judgments may be true. But many of them are creeping determinism wearing the clothes of wisdom. The signs look obvious only after you know which ones turned out to matter.

Be careful with the sentence *I should have known*. Very often, you could not have.

* * *

There is something else worth noticing: we do not use the language of destiny evenly.

Listen to how people talk about their lives and you will often hear a pattern. When something goes well, it was *meant to be*, or it was the reward of effort. When something goes badly, it was *bad luck*, or somebody else's fault. Psychologists call one version of this the *self-serving bias*: crediting our successes to ourselves and our failures to circumstances. It is very common, and in modest doses it probably helps us; it protects our confidence and keeps us trying.

But many people have the opposite pattern, and they are often exactly the people who pick up books like this one. When something goes well, it was luck. *I got lucky. Anyone could have done it.* When something goes badly, it was their fault. *I wasn't good enough. I should have known.* Success is luck; failure is character. This pattern is common in people who are anxious or depressed, and it is a recipe for misery, because nothing good ever counts and everything bad always does.

Both patterns have the same structure. They take events that were a mixture of effort, luck and things nobody could see, and sort them with different words depending on how they ended. The words are doing something to us. They are turning uncertainty into rules about who we are.

So try a small, honest discipline. When you tell the story of something that happened to you, good or bad, include three things: what you did, what you did not control, and what you still do not know. Do it for your successes as carefully as for your failures.

*I prepared hard for that interview, I was lucky that the manager had a problem I had solved before, and I don't know how close the other candidates were.* That is a true story. It is also a sturdy one. It will not collapse the next time things go badly.

* * *

The psychologist Dan McAdams has spent decades studying the stories people tell about their own lives. He has found that people who tell what he calls *redemption* stories, in which something bad eventually leads to something good, tend on average to report greater well-being and to invest more in caring for others and for the next generation.

This is encouraging, and I believe it. How we tell our story matters. But there is a way of using this finding that does damage, and I want to warn you about it.

A redemption story cannot be forced. If you insist, in the first raw weeks after a loss, that this must be leading somewhere wonderful, you put a weight on yourself and on the future that neither can carry. You start searching every day for the hidden gift and feel like a failure when you cannot find it. Some losses do not get redeemed in any clear way. Sometimes the dots do not connect, or not for a very long time, or not into the picture you would have chosen.

The healthy form of a redemption story is not *this was secretly good*. It is closer to *this was bad, and I built something after it*. The meaning comes from what you do next, not from pretending the loss was a present.

* * *

So what is destiny, in the way most of us actually live it?

Whatever is or is not written into the deep structure of the universe, what we experience as destiny is a reading: the story we tell when we look back at the pages we have lived. It is a real story, and we cannot live without it. But it is always written after the fact, in the light of how things turned out, by a mind that naturally draws paths.

That has two consequences, and I want you to hold both.

The first is mercy toward your past self. When you look back at a failure and feel it was inevitable, remember creeping determinism. At the time, it was open. Your past self stood in front of a genuinely uncertain choice, exactly as you stand in front of one now. Be as fair to them as you would want someone to be to you.

The second is freedom toward your future. The page you are on has not been read yet. Whatever you believe about who or what holds the pen, you are where every human being has always been: in front of an open choice, with a story to live and no way to read the ending before you get there.

Jobs was right that you have to trust something. You do not have to trust that the dots will join into a beautiful picture. You can trust something smaller and sturdier: that what you do today will become one of the dots, and that you will be the one who decides, later, what it meant.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*The past looks inevitable only because you are standing at the end of it.*

**Practice.** Choose one important event in your life. Write it three times: once as a story of destiny, once as a story of luck and once as a story of effort. Then write it a fourth time, honestly including all three, plus one thing you still do not know. Read the four versions aloud. Which one would you want to believe on your worst day? Which one is true?

# Chapter 9: Reading the page backward

In June 2005, Steve Jobs stood in front of the graduating class at Stanford University and told them three stories from his life. The first was about dropping out of college.

He had left Reed College after one semester, he said, because he could not see the value of spending his working-class parents' savings on courses he did not care about. But he stayed around the campus for another year and a half, sleeping on friends' floors and sitting in on classes that interested him. One of them was a class in calligraphy. He learned about typefaces, about the spacing between letters, about what makes beautiful printing beautiful. None of it had any practical purpose in his life.

Ten years later, when he and his colleagues were designing the first Macintosh computer, it all came back. The Mac was the first personal computer with beautiful typography, he said, and if he had never dropped in on that one class, it would not have been.

Then he said the line that has been quoted ever since: *You can't connect the dots looking forward; you can only connect them looking backwards. So you have to trust that the dots will somehow connect in your future.*

It is a lovely idea, and I think it is partly true. I want to look at it carefully, because hidden inside it is the most important thing to understand about destiny as we actually experience it.

* * *

Notice what makes the calligraphy story work. It works because the Macintosh succeeded and Apple became one of the most famous companies on earth. Suppose instead that Jobs's first company had failed in its second year, as most new companies do. Would the calligraphy class still be a dot that connected? Almost certainly not. It would be a curious detail in the life of a man who dropped out of college, an interesting waste of time that nobody would ever think to mention.

The dots we connect, looking backward, are the ones that lead to wherever we happen to have ended up. They look like a path because we are standing at the end of it. If we had ended up somewhere else, a different set of dots would have lit up, and they would have looked like a path too.

This is not a criticism of Jobs. It is something every human being does. We are story-making creatures. When we look back over our lives, we cannot help seeing a shape, a direction, a sense that things led somewhere. And when the shape is strong enough, we give it a name. We call it destiny.

* * *

In the early 1970s, a young psychologist named Baruch Fischhoff ran a set of experiments that showed just how powerfully knowing the ending changes the way we see the past.

In one of them, he gave people a short account of a historical conflict they knew nothing about: a war in the early nineteenth century between the British and the Gurkhas of Nepal. He described the background and the situation, and then asked them how likely different outcomes had been. Some participants were told nothing about how the war ended. Others were told that the British had won. Others were told that the Gurkhas had won. Others that there was a stalemate, or a peace settlement.

Whatever ending people were told, they judged that ending to have been more likely. They looked at the same evidence and found, in it, the seeds of whatever outcome they believed had happened. And they did not realize that knowing the ending had changed their judgment. They believed they would have seen it coming anyway.

In another study, Fischhoff and his colleague Ruth Beyth asked people to predict the results of President Nixon's historic trips to China and the Soviet Union in 1972, before they happened. Afterwards, they asked the same people to remember what they had predicted. People tended to remember having given higher odds to the things that actually happened, and lower odds to the things that did not. Their memories had quietly moved toward the truth.

Fischhoff gave this tendency a name that I find almost poetic. He called it *creeping determinism*. Once we know how something turned out, the past starts to look as though it could only have turned out that way. The feeling of inevitability creeps backward into events that, at the time, were genuinely open.

> *A story can make the past feel inevitable without making the future predictable.*

* * *

Why does this matter for a book about destiny?

Because a great deal of what we feel as destiny is creeping determinism. It is the natural, universal feeling that comes from looking at our lives from the end of the road. It does not prove that the road was fixed. It also does not prove that it was not. It simply tells us that the human mind, looking backward, makes paths.

People who believe in destiny for deep reasons, religious or philosophical, need not be troubled by this. Their belief does not rest on a feeling of inevitability; it rests on their faith or their reasoning. But it is worth knowing that the *feeling* of "it was always going to happen" will arrive whether or not anything was ever written. It arrives for everyone, about everything, once the ending is known.

There is a word in Arabic, *maktub*, which means "it is written." It has travelled far beyond the Arabic-speaking world, partly through a famous novel about a shepherd boy following his dream, where it becomes a kind of refrain. People say it with a certain peace after something has happened: *maktub*, it was written. I love the word. But notice the tense. It is almost always said about the past.

* * *

Now here is something stranger. We do not use the language of destiny evenly.

Listen carefully to how people talk about their lives, and you will often hear a pattern. When something goes well, it is described as *meant to be*, or as the reward of effort. *I worked so hard for this. It was destiny.* When something goes badly, it is described as *bad luck*, or as someone else's fault. *I was unlucky. The timing was wrong. They were biased.*

Psychologists call one version of this the *self-serving bias*: the tendency to credit our successes to ourselves and our failures to circumstances. It is very common, and in modest doses it is probably good for us; it protects our confidence and keeps us trying.

But some people have the opposite pattern, and they are often the people who read books like this one. When something goes well, they call it luck. *I got lucky. Anyone could have done it. They must have made a mistake in choosing me.* When something goes badly, they call it their fault. *I failed. I wasn't good enough. I should have known.* Success is luck. Failure is character. This pattern is common in people who are anxious or depressed, and it is a recipe for misery, because it means that nothing good ever counts and everything bad always does.

Both patterns have the same structure. They take events that were a mixture of effort, luck and whatever else, and they sort them using different words depending on whether the outcome was good or bad. The words are doing something to us. They are turning our uncertainty into rules about who we are.

> *The words we use for uncertainty quietly become rules for how we live.*

So I suggest a small, honest discipline. When you tell the story of something that happened to you, good or bad, try to include all three: what you did, what you did not control, and what you do not know. Do this for your successes as carefully as for your failures.

*I prepared hard for that interview, I was lucky that the manager had the same problem I had solved before, and I do not know how close the other candidates were.* That is a true story. It is also a stable one. It will not collapse the next time things go badly.

* * *

The psychologist Dan McAdams has spent decades studying the stories people tell about their own lives. He has found that people who tell what he calls *redemption* stories, in which something bad leads, eventually, to something good, tend on average to report greater well-being and to be more engaged in caring for others and for the next generation.

This is encouraging, and I believe it. How we tell our story matters. But I want to add a caution, because there is a way of using this finding that does damage.

A redemption story cannot be forced. If you insist, in the first raw weeks after a loss, that this must be leading to something wonderful, you put pressure on yourself and on the future that neither can bear. You start scanning every day for the hidden gift, and feeling like a failure when you cannot find it. Some losses do not get redeemed in any clear way. Sometimes the dots do not connect, or not for a very long time, or not into the picture we would have chosen.

The healthy form of a redemption story is not *this was secretly good*. It is closer to *this was bad, and I did something with it*. The meaning comes from what you build after, not from pretending the loss was a present.

* * *

So what is destiny, then, in the way most of us actually live it?

I have come to think of it like this. Whatever is or is not written in the deep structure of the universe, what we experience as destiny is a reading. It is the story we tell when we look back at the pages we have lived. It is a real story, and an important one; we cannot live without it. But it is always written after the fact, in the light of how things turned out, by a mind that naturally makes paths.

That has two consequences, and I want you to hold both.

The first is humility about the past. When you look back at a failure and feel that it was inevitable, that you were always going to lose, remember creeping determinism. At the time, it was open. You did not know. You could not have known. Your past self was standing in front of a genuinely uncertain choice, just as you are standing in front of one now. Be as fair to them as you would want to be treated.

The second is freedom about the future. The page you are on has not been read yet, not by you. Whatever you believe about who or what holds the pen, you are in exactly the position that every human being has always been in: in front of an open choice, with a story to live and no way to read the ending before you get there.

Jobs was right that you have to trust something. I would only add that you do not have to trust that the dots will connect into a beautiful picture. You can trust something smaller and sturdier: that what you do today will become one of the dots, and that you will be the one who decides, later, what it meant.

## For your quiver {.unnumbered .quiver}

*A story can make the past feel inevitable without making the future predictable.*

*Maktub, it is written, is almost always said in the past tense.*

*The words we use for uncertainty quietly become rules for how we live.*

**Practice.** Choose one important event in your life. Write it three times: once as a story of destiny, once as a story of luck and once as a story of effort. Then write it a fourth time, honestly including all three, plus one thing you still do not know. Read the four versions aloud. Which one would you want to believe on your worst day? Which one is true?

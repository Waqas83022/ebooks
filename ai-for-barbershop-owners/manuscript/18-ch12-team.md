# Chapter 12: Coordinate your team and manage walk-ins

The worst Saturday in Cedar Street's recent history went like this.

At 11:05, as the appointment-only morning ended, five walk-ins arrived within ten minutes. Nobody had written down who came first. Two of them wanted Tomás, who was running twenty minutes behind. Jaylen, Dre's nephew, told one man "about fifteen minutes", which turned into forty-five. At noon, a client who had been seeing Keisha for a year turned up on Tomás's books; he had booked through the shop's Instagram, which sent him to the shop link instead of hers. Keisha found out when she saw him in Tomás's chair. And at 1 p.m. a woman asked for the "$5 off weekday cuts" she had seen on the shop's Instagram, at Keisha's chair. Keisha had never agreed to it.

Nobody did anything terrible. Everybody was busy, and nobody had written down the rules. That is how most shop arguments start.

This chapter covers the agreements a shop with more than one barber needs: who owns what, how walk-ins work, what happens when someone runs late, how shared promotions work, and how to bring a new barber up to speed. AI is very good at turning loose verbal agreements into clear one-page documents. The agreements themselves have to come from a conversation between the people involved.

## Know which kind of shop you are

The rules depend heavily on how your barbers work with you. Broadly:

**Employees** work hours you set, use your products, follow your prices and policies, and the clients they serve are clients of the shop. You can direct how they work.

**Commission barbers** are often employees too, paid a percentage of what they cut. Same control, different pay.

**Chair renters** pay you rent for the chair and run their own small business inside your shop. In most places they set their own prices, hours and policies, keep their own client lists and book their own clients. You generally should not be directing how they do their work.

That last point matters legally as well as practically. In the US, whether someone is truly an independent contractor depends on how much control the shop has over their work, and the tests vary by state. If you set a renter's hours, prices and services, you may be treating them like an employee in a way that creates tax and employment obligations. If you are not sure which side of the line your arrangements fall on, talk to an accountant or employment lawyer in your state. This book will not make that call for you, and neither should an AI chat.

What this chapter *can* help with is making sure everyone knows what the arrangement is.

## The responsibility chart

Sit down with your barbers, ideally on a day the shop is closed, and agree who owns each of these. Then write it on one page and put it on the wall.

![Figure 13. Cedar Street's responsibility chart. One owner per row. The renter owns her own book; the shop owns the brand.](images/fig13-responsibility-chart.png)

Cedar Street's version, after the bad Saturday:

| Area | Who owns it |
|---|---|
| Shop Google profile, website, shop Instagram | Dre |
| Replies to reviews of the shop | Dre (Tomás drafts if Dre is away) |
| Shop menu and prices (Dre and Tomás) | Dre |
| Keisha's prices, services, policies | Keisha |
| Client records for Dre and Tomás's clients | The shop |
| Keisha's client list and booking page | Keisha |
| Shop-wide promotions | Dre; Keisha opts in or out in writing for each one |
| Messages on shop channels about a specific barber | Sent to that barber's link |
| Walk-in board on Saturdays | Whoever is on front desk (named on the schedule) |
| Complaints | Dre; Keisha handles her own clients' complaints |
| Opening, closing, cleaning | Rota, on the wall |
| Shared stock (towels, neck strips, disinfectant) | Dre orders; everyone records when they open the last box |
| Each barber's own products | That barber |

Two rows on that chart prevented both of the noon and 1 p.m. problems. Shop channels now send any question about a specific barber to that barber's link, and a shop-wide promotion applies to Keisha's chair only if she says yes in writing. When Dre ran a back-to-school kids cut post, he messaged Keisha first. She said no, because she does not cut kids under 10. The post mentioned Dre and Tomás only.

AI can turn your conversation into the chart:

::: prompt
I run a barbershop with [number] barbers: [describe each one's arrangement: employee, commission, renter]. Here are my notes from a meeting where we agreed who's responsible for what: [paste notes]. Turn these into a one-page responsibility chart with two columns, Area and Who owns it, with one owner per row. Then list any area that's usually important in a barbershop but that my notes don't cover, so we can decide it. Flag anything in my notes that sounds like the shop controlling a chair renter's prices, hours or client list.
:::

That last instruction is useful. It will not give you legal advice, but it will point out lines like "Keisha must work Saturdays", which you may want to discuss with a professional.

## Who can contact which clients

This is the question that causes the most bad feeling in shared shops, and it is worth a clear written answer.

At Cedar Street:

- The shop may send service messages (reminders, changes, closures) to any client booked through the shop's system.
- Marketing messages to Dre and Tomás's clients come from the shop, using clients who opted in to the shop's messages.
- Nobody contacts Keisha's clients except Keisha. The shop does not export, copy or message her list.
- If a client of one barber asks to switch to another, that is the client's choice. The new barber tells the old one, as a courtesy.
- If a barber leaves, what happens to "their" clients depends on their arrangement. Agree it in writing at the start, not the week they leave.

AI should never be given a renter's client list by the shop, and the shop's AI drafts should never include a renter's clients.

## Walk-ins without the chaos

Walk-ins are good business, especially in neighborhoods with lots of foot traffic. They are also the main source of waiting-room tension. The fix is a visible, fair system and honest waiting times.

### The walk-in board

A whiteboard by the door, a tablet, or the walk-in queue feature in your booking app. Each walk-in gets a line: name, time arrived, service, preferred barber or "next available". That is all.

The rules, written on or next to the board:

- First come, first served, within each barber's queue.
- "Next available" goes to whichever barber frees up first.
- Booked appointments come first; walk-ins fill the gaps.
- Walk-ins close at a set time before closing, so the last client is not rushed.
- Wait times are estimates. If yours is going to be more than 30 minutes, we'll tell you and offer a booked time instead.

Cedar Street's sign, drafted with AI and edited by Dre:

> **Walk-ins welcome after 11 on Saturdays, any time Tuesday to Friday.** Add your name to the board. Booked appointments go first, then walk-ins in order. Ask us how long the wait is. We'll be straight with you. Don't want to wait? Book your next cut by scanning the code.

### Honest wait times

The single most damaging thing at the walk-in board is an optimistic estimate. "About fifteen minutes" that becomes forty-five turns a satisfied walk-in into a one-star review.

Give a range, based on what is actually ahead: "There are two booked and one walk-in ahead of you with Tomás, so probably 45 minutes to an hour. Dre might be free sooner, around 30." Then offer the alternative: a booked time later today or another day, from the walk-in card.

People are remarkably patient with honest waits and remarkably angry about dishonest ones.

## When a barber runs late

Every barber runs late sometimes. A complicated cut, a client who arrived ten minutes late, a long conversation. The fix is not to never run late. It is to tell the next client early.

The rule at Cedar Street: if a barber is going to be more than 15 minutes behind, whoever is on the desk texts the next client with an honest estimate and a choice.

> Hi [first name], [barber] is running about [minutes] behind today. You're welcome to come at [new time], or if you'd rather, [other barber] can take you at your original time. Just reply and let us know.

Only offer the other barber if that barber is an employee of the shop and is genuinely free, or if a renter has agreed to take overflow. Never offer a renter's time without asking them.

## Closing tasks that actually get done

Closing is where "somebody" does everything and nobody does anything. A rota and a checklist fix it.

::: prompt
Write a closing checklist for a three-chair barbershop. Group it by area: each barber's own station, shared areas, front desk and register, back room. Under 25 items total. Each item starts with a verb and is specific ("Empty and disinfect clipper guards", not "Clean tools"). Add a line at the end for the person closing to sign and note anything that needs restocking.
:::

Print it, laminate it, put a marker next to it. Assign closing on a rota. On the day you notice the trash was not emptied, look at who signed.

## Hiring: define the role before you post the ad

When Cedar Street needed a Saturday front-desk person, Dre's first instinct was to post "Hiring! DM me". He got eleven replies, mostly from barbers looking for a chair, which was not what he needed.

Write down the role before you advertise it. What hours, what tasks, what pay, what skills matter, what does a good first month look like? AI can turn your notes into a role description and an ad:

::: prompt
I need to hire a part-time front-desk assistant for my barbershop, Saturdays 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. Tasks: greet clients, run the walk-in board, answer the phone and DMs using our saved replies, take payments, keep the waiting area tidy, restock towels. Pay: [rate]. Write: 1) a one-page role description, 2) a job ad under 120 words, 3) six interview questions, including two scenarios (an angry walk-in, a client asking for a price we don't have). Keep the ad free of language that could discourage applicants based on age, gender, race or other protected characteristics.
:::

Then check the ad and questions against your local employment rules. For a barber role, add a trial cut with a clear scoring sheet, agreed with your existing barbers in advance, so you are judging everyone on the same things.

## Onboarding a new barber or front-desk hire

A new person absorbs the shop's habits in the first two weeks. If they absorb them by guessing, you will be correcting them for months.

Give every new hire four things on day one:

1. **The shop brief** from Chapter 3.
2. **The responsibility chart** from this chapter.
3. **The front-desk script sheet** from Chapter 8, if they will talk to clients.
4. **A shift checklist** for opening, during the day and closing.

Then use AI for something it is surprisingly good at: practice. Have the new hire open a chat with the shop brief loaded, and ask it to play difficult clients:

::: prompt
You're going to help train a new front-desk assistant at a barbershop. Using the shop brief below, play a client in each of these situations, one at a time. Stay in character, be realistic, not cartoonish. After each conversation, step out of character and give the trainee three points of feedback based only on the shop brief: what they got right, what they got wrong, and what to say next time.\
Situations: 1) a walk-in who's been waiting 40 minutes and is angry; 2) a client who insists the price was $35 last time; 3) a parent who wants to book a kids cut for a 2-year-old; 4) someone asking for a barber who left the shop; 5) a client who wants a refund for a cut he didn't like.

Shop brief: [paste]
:::

Thirty minutes of this, followed by a real conversation with you about what came up, beats a week of learning on live clients. Tomás used it to train Jaylen on the phone; he said it was the first time Jaylen had heard the walk-in policy explained five different ways until it stuck.

## A shop AI policy, in five lines

If several people use AI on the shop's behalf, agree on a few rules. Cedar Street's fit on an index card:

1. Use AI to draft. A person reads everything before it goes to a client.
2. No client names, phone numbers, photos or health details in AI chats. Use placeholders.
3. Prices, times and policies come from the shop brief only.
4. Saved replies and templates are approved by Dre before use.
5. Keisha decides what is said about Keisha.

## The fifteen-minute team meeting

Once a week, on the same day, spend fifteen minutes with the team on the scorecard from Chapter 2. Not a lecture; a look at the numbers together. Rebooking rate per barber, no-shows, walk-ins turned away, one thing that went well, one thing to try next week.

Barbers who rent their chairs can choose whether to join. Many will, especially for the shop-wide parts (walk-ins, the Google profile, reviews), because those affect their business too.

## Before your next shift

- Agree a responsibility chart with your barbers and put it on the wall.
- Write down who can contact which clients, especially if anyone rents a chair.
- Set up a walk-in board with written rules and honest wait times.
- Write the running-late text and agree when to send it.
- Print and laminate a closing checklist, and start a rota.

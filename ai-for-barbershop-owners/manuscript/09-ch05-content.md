# Chapter 5: Turn haircut photos into a local content plan

Tomás posts on his own Instagram every day. His feed is beautiful: sharp fades, hard parts, designs, slow-motion clips of the foil shaver set to music. He has 4,000 followers. In eight months at Cedar Street, the number of clients who booked with him because of a post was, as far as anyone could tell, three.

The trouble was not the quality. It was that the posts did not do the boring parts. They never said where the shop was. They never said what a skin fade cost or how to book. Many of his followers were other barbers, who liked the work and lived in other states. A man two streets away who needed a fade on Thursday could scroll past twenty of Tomás's posts and still not know he could walk to him.

This chapter is about content that fills chairs, as opposed to content that collects likes. It is less glamorous, and it works. AI helps with the part most barbers hate, which is the writing, the planning and the remembering to post at all.

## What local content has to do

A post that brings in clients answers four questions for a local person, quickly:

1. **What is it?** A real cut, by a named barber, in your shop.
2. **Is it for me?** The service name, and who it suits.
3. **Where?** Your shop's name and neighborhood, every time.
4. **How do I get it?** The price or "from" price, and how to book.

Most barber content nails the first one and skips the other three. Fixing that is mostly a caption job, and caption jobs are where AI is strongest.

## Five kinds of post that work for barbershops

You do not need fifty content ideas. You need a handful of formats you can repeat, because repeating them is what makes posting sustainable.

**The real cut.** A photo or short video of a cut you did this week, with the service, barber, price and booking link. This is your bread and butter. Aim for two a week.

**The barber.** Introduce each barber: what they love cutting, how long they have been cutting, which days they work, one human detail. People book people. Repeat these every couple of months; your followers change.

**The question.** Answer something clients ask you every week. "How often should I get a fade touched up?" "What's the difference between a taper and a fade?" "Can you fix a home haircut?" These posts are useful to local people and easy to write.

**The availability post.** "Tomás has three openings Wednesday afternoon." Simple, direct, effective. Chapter 9 covers it in detail.

**The shop moment.** The new chair arriving, the shop's anniversary, the kid getting his first haircut (with parental permission), the charity cut day, a short clip of closing time. These remind people that you are a real place with real people.

Seasonal posts slot into these formats: back-to-school cuts in August, wedding and prom season in spring, holiday hours in December, Father's Day gift cards in June. Note your local events too: the high-school homecoming game, the street fair, the marathon that closes your road.

## Getting the photo right

A caption cannot rescue a bad photo. You do not need a professional camera. You need a few habits.

- Shoot near a window or under a consistent light; avoid the yellow overhead light if you can.
- Use a plain background. A dark cape and a clean wall behind the head beat a busy shop floor.
- Take the same angles every time: side profile, back, three-quarter front. Consistency makes your feed look professional and makes before-and-afters honest.
- Clean the neck and brush off hair before the photo. Obvious, often forgotten.
- Get the client's permission before you shoot, and again before you post. Something like: "Mind if I get a photo of the fade for the shop's Instagram? I won't show your face if you'd rather not." Many clients say yes. Respect those who say no.

AI can help you build a shot list so you are not inventing angles in the moment:

::: prompt
Make a photo and video shot list for a barbershop that wants to post real haircuts on Instagram and Google. For each of these services, list 3 specific shots (angle, distance, what should be in frame, lighting tip): skin fade, cut and beard, kids cut, hot towel shave, textured hair taper. Also list 5 short video ideas under 20 seconds that don't need talking to camera. Keep each item to one line.
:::

Print the list and tape it by the mirror. It removes the "what do I shoot" question for months.

## Writing captions in batches

Here is a workflow that takes about twenty minutes a week and produces a week of posts.

**Sunday or Monday (your day off):** Pick four or five photos from the week. For each, write a rough note: service, barber, anything notable. *"Skin fade, hard part, Tomás, client wanted it to grow out OK for a wedding in 3 weeks."*

Then, in one chat, with your shop brief already pasted:

::: prompt
Write Instagram captions for these four photos, using only facts from my shop brief and my notes. For each caption:

- First line: describe the cut plainly (this shows before "more").
- Mention the barber's name and Cedar Street.
- Include the service price from the menu.
- End with the booking link: [booking link].
- Under 50 words. No emojis except at most one. No hashtags in the caption.
- Sound like a barber talking to a regular, not an advert.\
After the four captions, give me 8 hashtags I could add in the first comment: 4 local (neighborhood/city) and 4 about the service. Don't invent neighborhood names; use only: [your neighborhood], [your city].

Photo 1: [note]\
Photo 2: [note]\
Photo 3: [note]\
Photo 4: [note]
:::

Read each caption with the checklist from Chapter 3. Fix anything that sounds off. Then schedule them. Instagram, Facebook and TikTok all let you schedule posts from the app or from Meta's Business Suite, so you can set up the week in one sitting and forget about it.

Here is what one caption for Tomás's wedding fade looked like after a single edit:

> Skin fade with a hard part, cut to grow out clean for a wedding three weeks from now. Tomás plans the length so it still looks sharp on the day. Skin fades at Cedar Street are $40. Book Tomás: [booking link]

That caption will never go viral. It does not need to. It tells a local man exactly what he can get, from whom, where, for how much, and how to book it.

## Short videos without the cringe

Reels and TikToks reach more people who do not already follow you. They also make many barbers uncomfortable, because they picture themselves dancing or talking to the camera.

You do not have to. The videos that work for barbers are usually quiet and process-focused: the last thirty seconds of a fade, the line-up with the trimmer, the hot towel going on, the before-and-after turn of the chair. Add on-screen text with the service and the price, and the shop name in the caption.

AI can write the on-screen text and a simple structure:

::: prompt
Write a script for a 15-second vertical video showing a cut and beard at Cedar Street Barbers. No talking to camera. Give me: 4 shots with timing (for example, 0 to 3 seconds: ...), the on-screen text for each shot (max 6 words each), and a 30-word caption with the price from my brief and the booking link. Make the first on-screen text something a local person would stop scrolling for.
:::

If one of your barbers *does* like talking to camera, let them answer one client question per week on video. "How long should I wait between fades?" in 30 seconds, filmed in the chair. Those videos age well and keep getting found for months.

## The four-week calendar

Planning a month at a time removes the daily "what do I post" question. Here is the calendar Dre and Tomás used for their first month. It assumes three posts a week, which is enough for a small shop.

**Week 1.** Tuesday: real cut (skin fade, Tomás). Thursday: barber introduction (Dre, eleven years on Cedar Street). Saturday: real cut (cut and beard, Dre). Friday: Google update with this week's openings.

**Week 2.** Tuesday: question post ("Taper or fade? Here's the difference"). Thursday: real cut (kids cut, with a parent's permission). Saturday: before-and-after (a home-haircut rescue, with permission). Friday: Google update.

**Week 3.** Tuesday: real cut (textured taper, Keisha, posted on the shop account with her agreement and her booking link). Thursday: availability post for Wednesday and Thursday afternoons. Saturday: shop moment (the new barber chair). Friday: Google update.

**Week 4.** Tuesday: barber introduction (Tomás). Thursday: real cut (hot towel shave, video). Saturday: seasonal post (back-to-school openings and the kids cut price). Friday: Google update.

Here is the prompt that builds a calendar like this for your own shop:

::: prompt
Build a four-week social media calendar for my barbershop using my shop brief. Three Instagram posts per week plus one Google Business Profile update every Friday. Use these formats: real cut, barber introduction, client question, availability post, shop moment, seasonal. Upcoming local events and dates to include: [list them, e.g. school starts 26 August, street fair 7 September]. For each post, give the day, format, which barber, a one-line idea, and the photo or video I need to capture this week. Put it in a table. Don't invent events or barbers.
:::

## Openings and announcements

New barber starting? Moving to a new location? Adding Sunday hours? These are your biggest posting moments, and they deserve a little more planning.

For a new barber, plan three posts: an announcement a week ahead (who they are, their specialty, their start date, how to book), a "first week" post with a real cut, and a barber introduction after they settle in. Ask the assistant to draft all three at once so the details stay consistent, and make sure the new barber approves anything that describes them.

For a shop opening or move, list everything a client needs to know: new address, parking, what changes and what stays the same, the date of the change, and what happens to existing bookings. Then ask AI to produce a short version for Instagram, a Google update, a text for clients who have opted in to messages, and a sign for the door. Read them all side by side before posting; you want the same date and details everywhere.

## Track bookings, not likes

Here is the honest truth about social media metrics: views and likes tell you whether people enjoyed a post. They do not tell you whether anyone booked.

To find out what posts actually do, you need two habits.

**Use a link that shows where the booking came from.** Many booking platforms let you create a separate link for Instagram, or show the source of each online booking. If yours does not, at least put your booking link in your Instagram bio and ask "How did you find us?" at booking or checkout.

**Count completed first visits from social, monthly.** Not followers, not likes. First-time clients who booked because of a post and actually sat in the chair.

Tomás changed his own captions to include the shop, the price and his booking link. He also started posting the shop's cuts on the shop account, where most followers were local. Over the next two months, eleven first-time clients told the front desk they had found the shop on Instagram, up from three in the previous eight months. His own feed still gets more likes. The shop's feed gets more clients.

## A word on AI-generated content

You will see advice to "let AI run your social media". Be careful with that. AI is excellent at the writing and planning. It is not good at being your shop.

What works: real photos and videos of your real work, with captions drafted by AI and edited by you.

What does not: AI-generated images of haircuts you did not do, AI "models" in your chairs, fake client quotes, stock photos of barbershops that are not yours, or a feed of generic tips that could belong to any shop in the country. Apart from being misleading, it does not work. People choosing a barber are looking for evidence that *this* barber can cut *their* hair. Only your real work provides that.

## Before your next shift

- Print a shot list and tape it by the mirror.
- Shoot four real cuts this week, with permission.
- Batch-write captions for all four in one sitting, using your shop brief.
- Build a four-week calendar and schedule the first week.
- Add a way to track bookings from social: a separate link, a source field, or the "How did you find us?" question.

# Chapter 14: Your first 30 days

You have now read about a dozen things you could change in your shop. The mistake to avoid is trying all of them next week.

When owners finish a book like this, they often go home fired up and, on Monday, set up new reminders, a new Instagram plan, a referral program, an ad, a membership and a new walk-in rule, all at once. For about ten days it is exciting. Then the shop gets busy, half of it slips, and by the end of the month nobody can tell what worked, because everything changed at the same time.

This chapter is a slower plan that works better. One baseline, one problem, one change a week, and an honest look at the results before you add more. It is the plan Dre followed, and it is the one I would give any owner starting on Monday.

## Before day one: the shop brief

If you have not written your shop brief (Chapter 3) and passed the ten test questions, do that first. Every other step in this plan depends on AI working from your real facts. Allow an hour.

## Week 1: baseline

The goal this week is to know where you are, not to change anything.

- Work out sellable minutes for each barber (Chapter 2).
- Start the tally sheet by the register: missed calls, messages waiting more than two hours, walk-ins turned away.
- Pull the last two weeks of appointments from your booking system, remove names, and calculate utilization, no-show rate, and checkout rebooking rate. Use the prompt in Chapter 2 if you want help.
- Check your Google Business Profile against your shop brief. Note what is wrong, but leave fixing it for a later week unless it is something urgent like the wrong opening hours.
- Fill in the first weekly scorecard.

At the end of the week, pick **one** problem. Use the three questions from Chapter 2: how many bookings it costs, how cheap it is to fix, how close it is to the chair.

Typical choices:

- Too many missed calls or slow replies → Chapter 8.
- A big recurring gap in the calendar → Chapter 9.
- A high no-show rate or low rebooking → Chapter 10.
- Hard to find on Google, few recent reviews → Chapters 4 and 6.

Write it at the top of the scorecard: *This month we are fixing* ___.

## Week 2: one change

Make the change that addresses your chosen problem. Only that change. Before you start, decide four things and write them on a test card:

- **What exactly changes**, and from which day.
- **Who approves** any message that goes to clients.
- **What you will count**, using the definitions from Chapter 2.
- **What counts as success**, decided now, not after you see the numbers.

Then do it. Run it for the whole week, record sources at booking, and resist the urge to tweak it halfway.

Dre's week 2: switch on the missed-call text-back, record a new voicemail greeting, and load ten saved replies into Instagram and his phone. What he counted: missed calls, missed calls recovered (by the text or a callback), and bookings from those callers. Success: recovering at least 75% of missed calls.

## Week 3: follow one client all the way through

This week, keep the week 2 change running and add no new ones. Instead, walk through your shop as a new client would, from start to finish, and look for friction.

Pick a real inquiry, or pretend to be one. Search for your shop on Google from your phone. Look at the photos, prices and reviews. Click the booking link. Try to book the service you advertise most. Call the shop during a busy hour and see what happens. Send a DM and time the reply. If you can, have a friend who has never been in book a real cut and tell you everything that annoyed them, from the parking to the checkout.

Look for these in particular:

- A price that is different in two places.
- A broken or confusing booking link.
- A service that will not show available times online when the barber is free.
- A call or message that took too long to answer.
- A checkout where nobody offered to book the next visit.
- A reminder that is confusing or arrives at a bad time.

Fix the single worst one. Write down the rest for later months.

Dre's friend, who had never been to Cedar Street, found that the booking page listed the skin fade under "Specialty services" on page two, where she did not see it. Moving it to the top of the list took two minutes.

## Week 4: results and decision

Now compare. Use the same weekdays and windows as your baseline, and the same definitions.

Count completed services, not bookings. Note direct costs, including any discounts and any money spent. Note the time it took you and the team. Note any complaints or awkward moments.

Then make one of three decisions about the change you made:

- **Keep it** if it met the success line you set in week 2.
- **Change it** if it nearly worked, or worked in a way you did not expect. Pick one thing to adjust and run it again next month.
- **Stop it** if it did not work and you cannot see a clear reason it would with a tweak.

All three are good outcomes. Stopping something that does not work frees your time for something that might, which is worth as much as keeping a change that does.

Dre's numbers at the end of week 4: missed-call recovery at 86%, against 39% in the baseline. At least eleven bookings traced to the text-back in the two weeks it ran. Decision: keep. Next month's problem: checkout rebooking.

## The weekly owner review

Every week of the 30 days, and every week after, spend about 30 minutes on a short review, alone or with the team. Same day, same time. The agenda:

1. **Scorecard.** Fill in this week's numbers. Compare with last week and the baseline. (10 minutes.)
2. **This month's change.** Is it running as planned? Anything to fix that is not a change to the test itself? (5 minutes.)
3. **Client voice.** New reviews, complaints and comments from the week. Anything that repeats? (5 minutes.)
4. **Next week.** One quiet-slot plan (Chapter 9), staffing, stock, anything coming up locally. (5 minutes.)
5. **One note** for the scorecard: what changed, and anything unusual about the week. (5 minutes.)

If you want AI to help, paste in the scorecard and the week's notes and ask for a two-paragraph summary with one question you should be asking. Keep the decisions to yourself.

## After the first month

At the end of 30 days, you have a baseline, one fix that is working (or a clear lesson), a weekly review habit, and a list of other problems you noticed along the way. That is a strong position.

Start month two the same way: pick the next problem from the list, one change, measured against the same scorecard. Over six months, one change a month, you will have worked through most of this book and know which parts actually move the numbers in your shop.

Here is roughly how Cedar Street's first six months went, one problem a month:

1. Missed calls and slow replies (Chapter 8).
2. Checkout rebooking (Chapter 10).
3. Google Business Profile, photos and reviews (Chapters 4 and 6).
4. Quiet Tuesday and Wednesday afternoons (Chapter 9).
5. Skin fade price and the menu (Chapter 11).
6. A first paid ad test for Tomás (Chapter 7).

By month six, compared with the same weeks a year earlier, Tomás's chair utilization had gone from 53% to 67%, Dre's from 64% to 71%, and checkout rebooking was close to 50%. New Google reviews were arriving every week. Some of that was the season, some was Tomás building his own book of regulars, and some was the work in this book. Dre is honest that he cannot split it exactly. But the scorecard, kept the same way every week, showed the direction clearly, and that was enough to keep going.

## Before your next shift

- Write your shop brief if you have not already.
- Put the weekly owner review in your calendar as a repeating appointment.
- Start week 1 on your next working day: baseline only, no changes.

# Chapter 9: Fill quiet weekday slots

It is Monday morning, the shop is closed, and Dre is checking next week's calendar at his kitchen table. Saturday is almost full. Friday after 3 is filling. But Tuesday and Wednesday, between noon and 4, Tomás's chair has twelve half-hour openings.

Dre's first thought is the one every owner has: post "20% off all cuts this week". It feels like action. It would probably bring a few people in.

Before he does that, it is worth asking a different question. Who could use those times at the full price, if they only knew the times were open?

The regular who works evenings and has been meaning to get a cut. The man who asked last week to be told if a Wednesday slot came up. The guy who works from home two streets over and has never thought of getting his hair cut at lunchtime on a Tuesday. All of them can fill a chair at $35. A public discount is a separate decision, one you can test later once you know what plain availability does on its own.

This chapter walks through a one-week campaign to fill those gaps, step by step, with the messages, the tracking and the arithmetic. It is the most hands-on chapter in the book. Run it once, and you will have a routine you can repeat whenever the calendar has holes.

## Find the gaps that repeat

One empty Tuesday is bad luck. Empty Tuesdays every week are a pattern, and patterns can be worked on.

Pull eight weeks of appointment data (names removed) and look at which hours are empty most often. You can do it by eye on a printed calendar, or let AI do the counting:

::: prompt
Here are 8 weeks of appointments from my barbershop, names removed. Columns: date, weekday, start time, end time, barber, service, status. Each barber's sellable hours are [list per barber, minus lunch].

For each barber, make a table of weekdays (rows) by hour blocks (columns: 10-12, 12-2, 2-4, 4-7 on weekdays; 8-11, 11-2, 2-5 on Saturday) showing the average share of sellable time that was booked and completed. Then list the three emptiest blocks for each barber, with the average number of empty hours per week in each. Show your method.
:::

What you get is a heatmap, even if it is just numbers in a table. Cedar Street's looked like this.

![Figure 10. Cedar Street's quiet hours, averaged over eight weeks. Darker means busier. The midweek early afternoon is the obvious gap.](images/fig10-quiet-slots-heatmap.png)

The midweek gap between noon and 4 was there every single week, for both barbers, and worst for Tomás, who has a smaller base of regulars. Friday evening and all of Saturday were close to full. That pattern is very common. Weekday afternoons are hard because most people with nine-to-five jobs cannot come.

Which raises the question: who *can*?

## Who could use a weekday afternoon?

Make a list of the kinds of people in your area who are free in your quiet window. For a midweek early afternoon, Cedar Street's list was:

- Regulars who work evenings, nights or shifts (bartenders, nurses, warehouse and restaurant workers).
- People who work from home or have flexible hours.
- Retirees.
- Students with afternoons free.
- People who work nearby and could come on a lunch break, if the cut is quick.
- Anyone already waiting for a weekday opening (the waitlist).

You probably know which of these groups your clients belong to. If you want help thinking it through, describe your neighborhood and ask the assistant for more ideas, then cross out the ones that do not fit.

Notice what is not on the list: kids. School is in session. Kids' cuts belong after 3:30, and promoting them for 1 p.m. on a Tuesday wastes a message.

## Count the openings you can actually sell

Before you tell anyone about an opening, make sure it is real.

Block out lunch, cleaning, breaks and any time the barber is busy with something else. Then count only gaps long enough for the service you are promoting. A 20-minute gap between two appointments looks free on the calendar, but it cannot hold a 30-minute cut. If two barbers share the calendar, write the barber's name next to each opening.

Then open your live booking page as a client would, choose the service you are promoting, and confirm the openings show up there. It is surprisingly common for a gap to exist on the barber's calendar but not appear online, because of a buffer setting, a service length mismatch, or the barber being marked as unavailable. If it does not show online, clients cannot book it, and your campaign will fail for a reason that has nothing to do with marketing.

If you make a list of openings to plan your outreach, write the time on it ("as of Monday 9 a.m."). The calendar will change while you are writing messages.

For Cedar Street's campaign week, the target was Tomás's chair, Tuesday and Wednesday, noon to 4. After blocking his lunch, there were twelve half-hour openings for a standard cut, all visible on the live booking page.

## Match each opening to the right audience

There are three ways to tell people about an opening. Each suits a different audience, and they work best together.

**The waitlist.** Clients who asked to be told about a specific day, time or barber. They have already said yes; they just need the message. Offer a specific opening, and use your live booking link or a clear hold-and-release process so two people cannot claim the same slot.

**Overdue regulars.** Clients who are past their usual return interval and have agreed to receive marketing messages from you. Leave out anyone who already has a booking coming up, anyone who has opted out, and anyone who has not given you permission to send them promotional texts or emails. (Chapter 10 explains why permission matters, and the rules on texting.)

**The public.** Everyone who follows you or finds you: an availability post on Instagram and a Google Business Profile update, with the service, price, barber and live booking link. Keep the post accurate as openings fill.

Cedar Street had a waitlist of three people for weekday slots with Tomás. Looking at clients who had opted in to texts, had not been in for longer than their usual gap, and had nothing booked, there were 24 eligible regulars. Every appointment could fill only one opening, even when several channels pointed people at it.

## Write the messages

Give the assistant the verified price, the window, the tone and the placeholders. Keep the actual client list in your booking system; the AI only needs to know what kind of message to write.

::: prompt
Draft three messages for Cedar Street Barbers.

1. A short text for a regular who opted in to marketing and is overdue for a visit.
2. A reply to someone on the waitlist for a Wednesday opening with Tomás.
3. A public availability post for Instagram and Google.\
Facts: a standard cut is $35 and takes 30 minutes. The window is Tuesday and Wednesday, noon to 4 p.m., with Tomás. Use these placeholders: [first name], [barber], [verified slot], [booking link], [opt-out wording if required].\
Keep each message under 45 words. Relaxed, plainspoken tone, like a barber texting. Leave missing facts in brackets. Don't invent prices, deadlines, availability or offers.
:::

Here is what came back, after one round of editing:

**Overdue regular**

> Hey [first name], [barber] at Cedar Street has a few weekday openings this week if you're due for a cut. Standard cut is $35. See what's still free: [booking link]. [Opt-out wording if required]

**Waitlist opening**

> Hey [first name], the Wednesday opening you asked about just came up. [Barber] has [verified slot] for a standard cut. You can grab it here: [booking link]. The calendar shows if it's still open.

**Public availability post**

> Got half an hour Tuesday or Wednesday? Tomás has a few openings between noon and 4 at Cedar Street. Standard cuts are $35. Pick a time: [booking link]

Notice what none of them say. No "limited time", no "only 3 left", no "don't miss out". Just what is available, what it costs, and how to book.

## Before you press send

Read each message as if you were the client. Can you tell who it is from, what it costs and how to book? Then check the practical things:

- The link opens the right service, with the right barber, and shows the openings.
- The shop (or the barber) has permission to contact each person on the list in this way.
- Every placeholder is filled.

Send the first batch yourself, not through an automation, so you can handle replies and notice awkward wording. If two people reply asking about the same time, you will know immediately.

As slots fill, stop promoting those times. Edit the public post or add a comment: "Tuesday's gone, two left Wednesday." An availability post that is out of date is worse than none.

One more thing. If someone replies asking for a cut and beard, check that the longer service fits. A 30-minute opening fits a standard cut, not a 45-minute cut and beard. Offer a time that works for both services, and quote the correct price from the menu, $50, not $35.

## Keep track while the day is busy

The point of the week is to learn what works, and you cannot learn without records. The record-keeping needs to be quick, because you are cutting hair.

At booking, record the source once: the campaign link, the client's reply to a text, or a quick "How did you find this opening?" at the chair. If you cannot tell, write "Unknown". Do not guess later; guesses always flatter the campaign.

Keep four columns: booked, completed, cancelled, no-show. If a client who already had an appointment moved it into one of the target slots, mark it as a reschedule. That matters, because moving an existing booking into a quiet slot does not fill a gap; it just moves one.

A paper sheet by the register is fine. So is a note in the booking system.

## Review the week before you repeat it

On Friday, pull the records for the twelve target openings. At Cedar Street, eight were booked, seven cuts were completed, and one client did not show up. Nobody had moved an existing appointment into these slots.

| Booking source | Booked | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Outreach to 24 eligible regulars | 4 | 3 |
| Waitlist replies | 2 | 2 |
| Local search or Google profile | 1 | 1 |
| Public availability post | 1 | 1 |
| Total | 8 | 7 |

The shop booked 8 of 12 openings (67%) and completed 7 of 12 (58%). Three of the 24 regulars in the outreach group completed a cut, or 12.5%. The waitlist and the public post reached different audiences, so their raw counts answer a different question; you cannot say one "beat" the other. Always keep the numbers next to the number they came from.

### What the completed cuts were worth

The seven cuts brought in $245 in service revenue (7 × $35). At $5 in direct costs per cut, that leaves $210 before Tomás's commission, owner labor, fixed overhead and the time spent on the campaign. If your shop pays commission per cut or pays card fees, include those in your version. For Cedar Street, after Tomás's 50%, the shop's share was $87.50, and Tomás earned $122.50 for chair time that would otherwise have been empty.

That $210 describes the completed cuts. It does not prove the campaign caused all of them. Some of those regulars might have booked anyway that week. To estimate the campaign's real effect, compare with similar weeks (the same Tuesday and Wednesday window over the previous four weeks), or run the outreach on alternate weeks and compare. Cedar Street's previous four weeks had averaged three completed cuts in that window for Tomás. So a fair estimate of the extra cuts is about four, not seven.

### Test a discount only if the gap is still there

Now, and only now, is it worth thinking about a discount, and only for the openings that are still empty.

Here is the arithmetic. With $5 in direct costs, a $35 cut contributes $30. A $5 discount drops that to $25. If all seven completed cuts in this example had received the discount, the shop would need nine discounted cuts to beat the original $210. Eight would contribute $200 (8 × $25). Nine would contribute $225 (9 × $25).

In other words, the discount has to bring in at least two extra completed cuts just to break even, and there have to be openings left to hold them. Check that before you try it. And remember that a discount offered to everyone also goes to the regulars who would have booked at full price.

Use the same arithmetic for a referral reward or a bundle. The question is always the same: how many extra completed visits does this need to pay for itself, and is there room in the chair for them?

## Choose what to do next week

Cedar Street's decisions after the first week:

- Keep using the waitlist for openings that match. It cost nothing and both people came.
- Check whether the four people who booked through outreach included anyone who was already about to book. (Two of them had been in five weeks earlier, inside their usual interval. Next time, the "overdue" filter would be a week longer.)
- Repeat the test the following week, same window, same messages, with one change: post the availability update at 8 a.m. on Monday instead of Tuesday morning.

Change one thing at a time. That way, when you look at the numbers next week, you will know what made the difference.

## Make it a routine

After two or three runs, this stops being a campaign and becomes a weekly habit that takes about 20 minutes:

1. On Monday, check the coming week's calendar and count real openings in the quiet windows.
2. Also on Monday, message the waitlist for matching openings.
3. On Monday or Tuesday, send the overdue-regular message to eligible clients who have not had one in the last month. Do not text the same people every week.
4. On Tuesday morning, post the availability update on Instagram and Google.
5. During the week, update the post as slots fill and record sources at booking.
6. On Friday or Saturday, count booked, completed, cancelled and no-shows in the target slots, and write one line on the scorecard.

Some shops also offer regulars a standing appointment: "same time every three weeks". A client who works evenings and always comes at 1 p.m. on a Tuesday is the most reliable filler of a quiet slot there is.

## Before your next shift

- Pull eight weeks of appointments and find your emptiest recurring windows.
- Check that those openings show on your live booking page for the service you want to promote.
- Start a waitlist if you don't have one. Most booking apps support it; a note works too.
- Run the one-week campaign: waitlist, eligible overdue regulars, public post.
- Record sources and the four columns, then review on Friday before repeating.

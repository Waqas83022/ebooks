# Chapter 3: Give AI the facts about your shop

On a busy Friday, Tomás got an Instagram message: *Do yall take walk ins tomorrow?* He was between clients, so he asked a chat assistant to write a friendly reply. It came back in seconds: *Absolutely! Walk-ins are always welcome at Cedar Street. Come by anytime and we'll get you in the chair!*

He sent it. It sounded great. It was also wrong. On Saturdays, Cedar Street takes appointments only until 11 a.m., because the morning rush is booked solid weeks ahead. The client showed up at 9:15 with his son, waited forty minutes, and left. His Google review the following week used the word "liars".

The assistant had not lied. It did not know the walk-in rule because nobody had told it. Asked to be friendly about walk-ins, it wrote the friendliest thing it could.

The fix is a document that takes about an hour to write and saves you from this kind of mistake for years: a one-page shop brief.

## What a shop brief is

A shop brief is a single page that holds every fact an AI assistant (or a new employee) needs to answer routine questions correctly. Prices, times, hours, barbers, booking rules, how you talk, and what never to promise.

You paste it at the start of a chat, or save it in the assistant's settings, and every draft that follows is built on your facts instead of guesses. When a price changes, you change it in one place.

It also turns out to be the best training document you will ever give a new hire. Writing it forces you to decide things you have been deciding on the fly for years. ("Do we charge for a line-up on a kid's cut? Do we let people be fifteen minutes late or ten?")

## What goes in it

Keep it to one page if you can, two at most. The assistant does not need your life story. It needs facts it can rely on.

**The basics.** Shop name, address, the cross street or a landmark, parking, phone number, booking link, Instagram handle, opening hours for each day, days closed, and holiday closures coming up.

**The barbers.** Who works which days, any specialties, who cuts kids, who does designs or color, and whether each barber is an employee or rents their chair (this matters for who handles their bookings).

**The menu.** Every service with its price, its booked length, and what is included. Write "starting at" if a price varies, and say what makes it vary.

**Booking rules.** How people book. Whether walk-ins are accepted and when. Deposit rules, if any. The cancellation cutoff. The late-arrival grace period and what happens after it. The no-show rule.

**Payments.** Cash, card, tap, tips on card, whether you sell gift cards or memberships.

**How we talk.** Three or four lines describing your voice, plus a short list of words you use and words you never use.

**Never promise.** The list of things the assistant must not do: confirm a time slot, offer a discount, quote a price not on the menu, comment on a client's skin or scalp condition, or make up anything not in the brief.

**Hand it to a human.** Topics that always go to the owner: complaints, refunds, injuries, anything involving a minor without a parent, press, and anything not covered in the brief.

## Cedar Street's brief

Here is the brief Dre wrote after the walk-in mess. Use it as a model. Yours should sound like your shop, not like his.

![Figure 4. Cedar Street's one-page shop brief. Every fact in one place, dated, with one owner.](images/fig04-shop-brief.png)

> **Cedar Street Barbers: shop brief** (updated 4 November; owner: Dre)
>
> *Basics.* 412 Cedar Street, next to Lopez Hardware. Free parking behind the building, entrance on Pine Alley. Phone [shop phone]. Book online at [booking link]. Instagram @[handle].
>
> *Hours.* Tuesday to Friday 10 a.m. to 7 p.m. Saturday 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. Closed Sunday and Monday. Closed Thanksgiving Day and the Friday after.
>
> *Barbers.* Dre (owner): Tuesday to Saturday, fades, beard work, hot towel shaves. Tomás: Tuesday to Saturday, skin fades, designs, kids. Keisha: rents her own chair, Wednesday to Saturday, textured hair, women's short cuts and tapers; she takes her own bookings at [Keisha's link] and sets her own prices.
>
> *Menu (Dre and Tomás).* Standard cut $35, 30 min. Skin fade $40, 45 min. Beard trim and line-up $20, 15 min. Cut and beard $50, 45 min. Kids cut, under 12, $25, 30 min. Hot towel shave $45, 45 min. Full service (cut, beard, hot towel, wash) $75, 75 min. Designs from $10 extra; price depends on detail, confirmed in the chair.
>
> *Booking rules.* Book online or by phone. Walk-ins welcome Tuesday to Friday when a chair is free. Saturday is appointment only until 11 a.m.; walk-ins after 11 if there is space. No deposit for regular services. Cancel or move up to 12 hours ahead with no charge. Late arrivals: 10-minute grace; after that we may shorten the service or rebook. Two no-shows in six months means future bookings need a $10 deposit.
>
> *Payments.* Cash, card and tap. Tips on card welcome. Gift cards sold in the shop.
>
> *How we talk.* Like a barber talking to a regular: relaxed, direct, friendly, short. First names. No corporate language. Words we use: cut, fade, line-up, book, slot, chair. Words we avoid: experience, elevate, pamper, luxurious, gentlemen's grooming, any emoji except an occasional thumbs-up.
>
> *Never promise.* A specific time slot (always send the booking link or say we'll confirm). Any discount or free service. A price not listed here. That a walk-in will be seen at a particular time. Anything about medical, skin or scalp conditions.
>
> *Always hand to Dre.* Complaints, refund requests, injuries or cuts, questions about Keisha's prices or bookings (send her link), media, job applications, and anything not covered above.

Notice what Dre did with Keisha. She rents her chair, so the shop brief does not quote her prices or promise her time. It points to her link. That one line prevents a whole category of arguments.

## How to use the brief

There are three ways to put the brief to work, from simplest to most convenient.

**Paste it each time.** Keep the brief in a note on your phone. When you start a new chat, paste it first, then add: *Use only the facts in this brief. If a question needs a fact that is not in it, write [CHECK] instead of guessing.* Then ask for what you need. This works with any free assistant.

**Save it in the assistant's settings.** Most paid assistants let you save standing instructions or create a project with files attached. ChatGPT has custom instructions and projects; Claude has projects; Gemini has saved instructions and "Gems". The names change but the idea is the same: you upload the brief once, and every chat in that project starts with it. This is the setup most owners end up with.

**Load it into your booking tool's AI.** If your booking platform has an AI assistant or receptionist, it will have a settings page for business information and frequently asked questions. Copy the relevant parts of the brief there. Then test it (see below), because each platform uses that information a little differently.

Whichever you choose, keep one master copy, date it, and give it an owner. When Tomás raises his skin fade price, the brief gets updated the same day, and the date at the top changes. Old copies floating around in three different apps are how wrong prices end up in client messages.

## Teach it your voice

The "How we talk" section of the brief gets you most of the way. To get closer, show the assistant how you actually write.

Scroll back through your texts and DMs and find five or six messages you sent to clients that sound like you at your best. Remove the names. Then:

::: prompt
Here are six messages I've sent to clients at my barbershop, with names removed. Describe my writing style in five short bullet points: sentence length, punctuation habits, greetings and sign-offs, words I use often, and anything I never do. Then write a 40-word reminder message for a Saturday appointment in that same style, using [first name], [time] and [barber].

[paste the six messages]
:::

Read the description. If it is accurate, paste those five points into your brief under "How we talk". If it got something wrong ("you use exclamation marks a lot"), correct it there. From then on, drafts will sound more like you and less like a bank.

## Test before anyone uses it

This is the step most people skip, and it is the one that catches the walk-in disaster before it happens.

Write ten test questions, the kind clients actually ask. Include at least three about prices, three about rules (late arrivals, walk-ins, cancellations), two about closed days or holidays, and two trick questions where the right answer is "I'm not sure, let me check" or "we don't offer that".

Here are Cedar Street's:

1. How much is a cut and beard?
2. Is a skin fade still $35? (It is $40. The right answer corrects the client politely.)
3. Do you do kids cuts? How old?
4. Can I walk in Saturday at 9?
5. I'm running 15 minutes late, is that OK?
6. Are you open Monday?
7. Are you open the day after Thanksgiving?
8. How much does Keisha charge for a taper?
9. Do you do perms? (Not on the brief. The right answer: we don't list that service; let me check with the team.)
10. Can you fit me in at 3 today? (The right answer: I can't confirm a time here; here's the booking link, or we'll check and reply.)

Paste the brief, then ask all ten. Mark each answer right or wrong. Look hardest at the trick questions. A good setup will say it does not know. A bad one will cheerfully invent a perm price.

When an answer is wrong, do not just fix the answer. Fix the brief, or add a line to the instructions, and run all ten questions again. When all ten pass, the setup is ready for drafting. Run the test again every time you change the brief, and every few months anyway, because the assistants themselves get updated.

## The review checklist

Even with a good brief and passing tests, a person reads every client-facing message before it goes out, at least until you have used a template for a while and trust it. Here is the checklist to run in your head, which takes about ten seconds once it becomes habit:

- Facts: Do the price, time, service, barber and day match the brief and the live calendar?
- Promises: Does it promise a slot, a discount, or anything we did not approve?
- Link: Is the booking link the right one, for the right barber and the right service?
- Placeholders: Are all the [brackets] filled in? Nothing like "[first name]" left in the message?
- Privacy: Does it mention anything personal that should not be in a text that someone else might see on a lock screen?
- Permission: Has this person agreed to hear from us this way (see Chapter 10 for marketing messages)?
- Tone: Would I say this out loud to this client?

Print it and tape it by the register if you have staff drafting messages. It looks fussy for about a week. After that it becomes automatic.

## For chair-rental shops: one shop brief, several barber briefs

If your barbers rent their chairs, write two kinds of brief.

The **shop brief** covers what the shop owns: the address, hours the building is open, parking, the shop's Instagram and Google listing, shared rules (cleanliness, music, kids in the shop), and a list of barbers with a link to each one's booking page.

Each **barber brief** belongs to that barber. It covers their services, prices, booking rules, deposit policy and voice. They write it, they keep it updated, and they decide whether to share it with the shop. Keisha's brief, for example, has her prices, her 24-hour cancellation rule (stricter than the shop's), and her note that she does not take walk-ins at all.

Then agree on one rule for shared channels: when a question about a particular barber's prices or availability arrives on the shop's Instagram or phone, the reply sends the client to that barber's link and does not answer for them. Chapter 12 goes further into this.

## What to do with the brief this week

You can write the first version in under an hour. Here is a prompt that does most of the typing for you:

::: prompt
I own a barbershop. I'm going to describe it in a messy way. Turn what I say into a one-page shop brief with these headings: Basics, Hours, Barbers, Menu (price and booked length for each service), Booking rules, Payments, How we talk, Never promise, Always hand to the owner. Use short lines. Where I haven't given a fact, write [MISSING] instead of guessing. At the end, list the five most important questions I haven't answered.

Here's my shop: [talk it through in your own words, or record a voice note and paste the transcript]
:::

The list of missing questions at the end is the useful part. It is usually things like "What happens if a client arrives 20 minutes late?" or "Do you charge extra for long hair?", rules you apply every week but have never written down.

## Before your next shift

- Write your shop brief using the prompt above. Fill every [MISSING] gap.
- Run the ten test questions. Fix the brief until all ten pass.
- Save the brief where you will use it: a phone note, your assistant's project settings, or both.
- Put the review checklist by the register if anyone else sends messages for the shop.

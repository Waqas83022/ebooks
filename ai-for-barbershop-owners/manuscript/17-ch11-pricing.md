# Chapter 11: Price for chair time, not just per cut

Ask a barber which service makes the most money and you will usually hear "the full service" or "the cut and beard", because those have the biggest price. Dre would have said the same. Then he worked out what each service earned per hour of chair time, and the answer surprised him. His $40 skin fade, one of the most popular services in the shop, earned less per hour than a plain $35 cut.

Your chair is the one thing you cannot make more of. There are only so many hours in a week, and on a Saturday, every one of them is already spoken for. So the useful question is not "how much does this service cost?" It is "how much does this service leave behind for each hour it occupies the chair?"

This chapter shows you how to work that out, how to use it to set prices and bundles, and how to check the arithmetic on discounts, memberships and gift cards before you commit to them. AI is handy for the calculations and for the wording of any price change. The decisions are yours.

## Contribution per chair hour

Here is the measure, in one line:

**Contribution per chair hour = (price − discounts − direct costs) ÷ booked minutes × 60.**

*Direct costs* are the costs that come with each service: product used, disposable supplies, laundry, card fees, and commission if the barber is paid per service. Not rent, not the electricity bill, not the owner's salary. Those are overhead; they are there whether you cut one head or forty.

*Booked minutes* are the time the service blocks in the calendar, including any buffer you build in. If a skin fade is booked for 45 minutes, it costs the chair 45 minutes, even if Tomás finishes in 38.

Here is Cedar Street's menu, worked out for the owner's chair (no commission):

| Service | Price | Direct costs | Minutes | Per chair hour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Beard trim and line-up | $20 | $3 | 15 | $68.00 |
| Standard cut | $35 | $5 | 30 | $60.00 |
| Cut and beard | $50 | $7 | 45 | $57.33 |
| Full service | $75 | $12 | 75 | $50.40 |
| Hot towel shave | $45 | $8 | 45 | $49.33 |
| Skin fade | $40 | $5 | 45 | $46.67 |
| Kids cut | $25 | $4 | 30 | $42.00 |

Take the skin fade: $40 − $5 = $35, divided by 45 minutes, times 60 = $46.67 per chair hour. The standard cut: $35 − $5 = $30, divided by 30, times 60 = $60.

Estimate direct costs roughly; you do not need precision to the cent. Look at what you spend on product and supplies in a month and divide by the number of services. Add card fees (often around 3% of the price). That gets you close enough.

## What the numbers tell you, and what they don't

The table told Dre three things.

**The skin fade was underpriced for its time.** It took 50% longer than a standard cut but cost only 14% more. Every Saturday slot that went to a skin fade earned less than the same time spent on standard cuts or beard trims.

**The kids cut was the lowest earner.** That was a choice Dre was happy with. Kids cuts bring families, and families bring fathers who book their own cuts. Some services earn their place in other ways. Just make that choice on purpose.

**The beard trim earned the most per hour,** because it is short. That did not mean he should only do beard trims. It meant a short add-on slotted into a 15-minute gap earned real money.

And one thing the table does *not* tell you: contribution per chair hour matters most when the chair would otherwise be full. On a Saturday morning with a waiting list, every hour is precious and the highest-earning services deserve priority. On a Tuesday afternoon with empty slots, a $21 kids cut is $21 more than an empty chair. The measure is for setting prices and guarding peak hours, not for turning clients away when you have nothing else to do.

## Three ways to fix an underpriced service

Once you spot a service that earns too little per hour, you have three options.

**Raise the price.** If the skin fade went to $45, its contribution would be $40 per 45 minutes, or $53.33 an hour. Still below the standard cut, but much closer.

**Shorten the booked time, honestly.** If Tomás consistently finishes skin fades in 35 to 40 minutes, maybe the booked time should be 40. But do not squeeze it so hard that clients feel rushed or the next client waits. Look at real finishing times for a couple of weeks before changing.

**Charge more at peak times.** Some shops charge a few dollars more on Saturday or after 5 p.m., or for a specific barber with a long waitlist. This rewards clients who can come off-peak and protects the hours everyone wants. It needs clear explanation on the booking page, and it works better in some neighborhoods than others.

Dre chose the first: skin fades went to $45, with four weeks' notice.

## How many clients can you lose after a price rise?

The fear with any price rise is that clients leave. The arithmetic tells you how many could leave before you are worse off.

Take the skin fade. At $40, each one contributes $35 (after $5 in costs). At $45, each contributes $40. To earn the same total contribution, you need $35 ÷ $40 = 0.875 times as many skin fades. In other words, you could lose 12.5% of your skin fade clients and still earn the same. And you would have freed up chair time for other clients.

The general formula: **break-even volume = old contribution per service ÷ new contribution per service.** If the answer is 0.875, you can lose up to 12.5% of volume. Remember to use contribution (after direct costs), not price; the result is quite different.

In practice, a modest price increase on a well-liked service, explained well, rarely loses anywhere near that many. Dre's skin fade numbers over the next two months were down by about 4% compared with the same weeks the year before, well inside the margin.

### Announcing a price change

Tell people ahead of time, in plain language, without apologizing too much or explaining your rent. AI is good at this:

Update the price everywhere on the same day: booking page, Google Business Profile services list, website, shop brief, saved replies, and the sign. A price that changed on the sign but not on Google is a recipe for an awkward conversation at the register.

## Bundles: do they help?

A bundle combines services at a price below their separate total. Cedar Street's cut and beard is $50, against $35 + $20 = $55 separately, a $5 saving. Is it a good deal for the shop?

Separately, a standard cut (30 minutes) plus a beard trim (15 minutes) takes 45 minutes and contributes $30 + $17 = $47. The bundle takes the same 45 minutes and contributes $43 (with $7 in costs). So the bundle earns $4 less than selling the two separately.

That is fine *if* the bundle persuades some clients to add a beard trim they would otherwise have skipped. For those clients, the shop earns $43 instead of $30. If most bundle clients would have booked both anyway, the bundle is just a discount.

You can test it the same way as anything else: look at how many cut-only clients started adding the beard after the bundle was introduced. AI can help you estimate from your appointment data. If the bundle is not changing behavior, consider pricing it at the full $55, or making the bundle include something small that costs you little and clients value (a hot towel finish, for instance).

## Discounts: count the clients who would have come anyway

Chapter 9 showed the arithmetic for a $5 weekday discount: with $30 contribution per cut, a $5 discount drops it to $25, so you need 20% more completed cuts just to break even. Here is the general formula, worth writing on the inside of a cabinet door:

**Extra visits needed to break even = old contribution ÷ new contribution.**

If the result is 1.2, you need 20% more visits. If it is 1.5, you need 50% more.

And the hidden cost: a public discount goes to everyone who books in that window, including the regulars who would have come at full price. The more of your discounted visits come from people who would have booked anyway, the worse the deal.

That is why the order in this book is: fill gaps with availability messages first, discounts last, and only where there is still room.

## Add-ons and retail

Products on the shelf and small add-ons (a hot towel finish, an eyebrow tidy, a scalp massage, a design) can add meaningful money without much chair time. They are worth looking at with the same arithmetic.

A pomade that costs the shop $9 and sells for $20 contributes $11 in about a minute of conversation. A $10 design add-on that takes 10 minutes contributes about $10, or $60 an hour, the same rate as a standard cut.

The trick is to recommend, not sell. Clients can tell the difference instantly. "I used the matte clay today. It's what keeps it from looking flat by the afternoon. It's on the shelf if you want it" works. A hard pitch at the register does not. AI can help you write short product cards for the shelf: what it is, what hair it suits, how to use it, the price.

## Memberships

Memberships, where clients pay a monthly fee for a set number of services, have become popular in barbershops. Zenoti's 2026 benchmark reported that membership sales at the barbershops on its platform grew 20% in 2025. For the shop, a membership means predictable income and more regular visits. For the client, it means a small saving and, often, priority booking.

Before launching one, work through four questions. AI can help with the arithmetic, but these are judgment calls.

**What does it include, and what does it cost?** Example: $65 a month for two standard cuts, against $70 at full price, plus priority access to weekday evening slots.

**What does it do to contribution per visit?** Two cuts at $32.50 each, minus $5 costs, gives $27.50 per visit instead of $30. A small discount, in exchange for visits that are already booked and paid.

**Can the calendar hold it?** If 40 members each use two cuts a month, that is 80 cuts, or 40 hours of chair time a month. Where will those hours come from? If they all want Saturday mornings, they will crowd out your other clients. Many shops steer membership visits toward weekdays, which is fine if it is clear at sign-up.

**What happens with unused visits and cancellations?** Do not build your plan on members paying for visits they never use. It feels bad to members when they notice, and they will. Decide whether unused visits roll over, expire monthly or carry over for one month. Make cancelling easy and the renewal terms clear. Many US states have automatic-renewal laws that require clear disclosure of terms and an easy way to cancel, and other countries have similar rules; check yours, and use a booking or payment platform that handles recurring billing properly.

Start small: offer it to regulars who already come every three weeks, cap the first round at a number your calendar can absorb, and review after three months.

## Gift cards

Gift cards sell well before Father's Day and the December holidays. They bring cash in early and often bring in new clients, since the person using the card may never have visited.

Two things to plan for. First, capacity: many cards get used in the weeks after the holiday that prompted them. If you sell 60 cards in December, a good number of those visits may land in January. That is useful if January is usually quiet; check your calendar. Second, the rules: in the United States, the federal Credit CARD Act generally does not allow gift cards to expire within five years of purchase, and some states go further. Do not count on unused cards as profit.

Write gift card terms plainly and keep a record of every card sold and redeemed. Your booking or payment system almost certainly does this already.

## The offer break-even calculator

Every offer in this chapter (a discount, a bundle, a referral reward, a membership) can be checked with the same four lines. Appendix C has a worksheet version. Here it is in words:

1. **Contribution per visit now** = price − direct costs.
2. **Contribution per visit with the offer** = price − discount − direct costs.
3. **Visits needed to break even** = current visits × (line 1 ÷ line 2).
4. **Room in the calendar?** Are there enough open slots of the right length, at times clients will book, to hold the extra visits?

If line 3 asks for more visits than line 4 can hold, the offer cannot pay for itself, however popular it is.

## Before your next shift

- Estimate direct costs per service and work out contribution per chair hour for your whole menu.
- Spot the service that earns the least per hour and decide whether that is a choice or an accident.
- Before your next discount, bundle, membership or gift card promotion, run it through the four-line break-even check.
- If you change a price, update every listing on the same day.

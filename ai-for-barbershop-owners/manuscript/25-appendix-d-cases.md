# Appendix D: Three worked cases {.unnumbered}

These three shops are fictional, like Cedar Street, but their problems are among the most common I hear about. Each case follows the same pattern as the book: baseline, one problem, one change at a time, an honest review.

## Case 1: A solo barber with quiet afternoons

**The shop.** Sam Okafor rents a small private suite in a salon studio building. One chair, appointment only, no front desk. Sam works Tuesday to Saturday, 10 a.m. to 7 p.m. (Saturday 9 to 4), and charges $40 for a cut, booked at 40 minutes. Direct costs are about $5 a cut, plus the weekly suite rent, which is overhead.

**The baseline.** Sam's sellable time, after a 30-minute lunch and 20 minutes of cleaning and admin a day, came to about 2,330 minutes, or just under 39 hours, a week. Over two normal weeks, Sam completed 50 hours of cuts out of about 78 sellable hours: 64% utilization. Saturdays and weekday evenings were full. Tuesday to Thursday between 1 and 4 p.m. averaged 11 empty hours a week. Missed calls were low, because nearly all clients booked online. Checkout rebooking was already 55%; Sam had always asked.

**The problem chosen.** The weekday afternoon gap. Eleven hours a week is about 16 cuts, or roughly $640 in revenue that the chair was not earning.

**Who could come then?** Sam knew the client base well and listed: nurses and technicians from the hospital ten minutes away, who work 12-hour shifts on rotating days; two restaurant managers; a handful of remote workers; and retirees from the apartment building across the road.

**What Sam did, week by week.**

*Week 1.* Wrote a shop brief (one page, ten minutes with AI), tested it, and started a waitlist in the booking app with a "weekday afternoons" tag.

*Week 2.* Messaged the 31 opted-in clients whose last visit was past their usual interval and who had nothing booked, using a plain availability text (no discount). Posted a weekday availability update on Instagram and Google every Monday morning.

*Week 3.* Started offering standing appointments to clients with fixed off-days: "Want me to lock in every third Wednesday at 1:20?" Six clients said yes in the first two weeks, mostly shift workers who were tired of hunting for slots.

*Week 4.* Left a short stack of cards at the hospital staff coffee shop, with the owner's permission, offering nothing but the facts: weekday afternoon openings, price, booking link with a code.

**The results.** Over six weeks, compared with the six weeks before:

- The 1 to 4 p.m. weekday window went from an average of 5 completed cuts a week to 10, filling about half the gap.
- Of the extra completed cuts, the standing appointments accounted for about 2 a week, outreach to overdue regulars about 1.5, the waitlist about 1, and the hospital cards and posts together about half a cut a week. (Sam kept a source column and compared like-for-like weeks; the numbers are small and rough.)
- Five extra cuts a week at $35 contribution each is about $175 a week, or a little over $9,000 a year if it held, with no discounts.
- Time spent: about 30 minutes a week on messages and posts once the routine settled.

**What Sam decided.** Keep the standing appointments and the Monday availability post. Stop leaving cards at the coffee shop after one more month unless they started producing visits. Try a modest "weekday afternoon" price only if the gap was still there after three months. It wasn't, quite: by then the window was about 70% full, and Sam decided to use the remaining time for a monthly education day instead.

**The lesson.** The biggest single gain came from the least technical change: offering a regular time to people whose schedules suited the quiet hours. AI's role was small but useful: it made the brief, the messages and the posts quick enough that Sam actually did them every week.

## Case 2: A busy shop with a no-show problem

**The shop.** Northside Cuts has six chairs, all barbers employed on 50% commission, and a front-desk person on Friday and Saturday. Prices are $38 for a standard cut (30 minutes) and $48 for a fade (40 minutes). Clients book mainly online, many through a booking marketplace that sends new clients for a fee.

**The baseline.** Friday and Saturday were packed, but the barbers were angry. Across those two days, the no-show rate was 12% over four weeks: 58 no-shows out of 483 appointments due. Most were first-time clients who had booked through the marketplace. Late cancellations added another 4%. On a Saturday with 120 appointments, that was about 14 empty slots, many in prime time, and barbers who had turned down walk-ins to keep those slots open.

**The money.** At an average ticket of about $43 and $5 in direct costs, each no-show cost the shop its share (half of $43, minus $5, or about $16.50) and cost the barber about $21.50. Fifty-eight no-shows in four weeks meant roughly $960 lost to the shop and $1,250 to the barbers, before counting the walk-ins turned away.

**The problem chosen.** Friday and Saturday no-shows.

**What the owner did, one change a week.**

*Week 1.* Read the existing reminders. There was only one, sent 24 hours ahead, with no reschedule link and a generic message from the software. The owner had AI rewrite it, added a reschedule link, and added a second reminder 2 hours before.

*Week 2.* Started sending the same-day no-show recovery message ("we missed you today, no worries, here's the link") so clients who skipped had an easy path back, and recorded every no-show against the client profile.

*Week 3.* Introduced a $10 deposit, applied to the service, for first-time online bookings on Friday and Saturday only, and for any client with two no-shows in six months. The booking page, reminders and shop brief all explained it in one plain sentence, written with AI and edited by the owner.

*Week 4.* Gave front-desk staff a short script for regulars who asked about the deposit: "It's only for first-time online bookings on our busiest days. You won't see it, because you always show up."

**The results.** Over the following eight weeks, compared with the four-week baseline on the same days:

- The no-show rate on Friday and Saturday fell from 12% to 5% (24 of 498 appointments due).
- Late cancellations stayed about the same, around 4%. Many of those clients now moved their booking using the reschedule link rather than vanishing.
- First-time online bookings on Friday and Saturday fell by about 6%. Some people did not want to pay a deposit to a shop they had never visited. The owner judged that acceptable: the slots filled with regulars and walk-ins instead.
- Barbers stopped holding chairs empty for clients who were not coming, and walk-ins turned away fell.

**What the owner decided.** Keep both reminders and the recovery message permanently. Keep the deposit for first-time online bookings on peak days, and review it every quarter. Look at the marketplace's fee against how many of its clients came back (Chapter 7, worksheet 4), which became the next month's problem.

**The lesson.** Most of the improvement came from the deposit, but the reminders and reschedule link mattered too, because they turned some no-shows into reschedules. And the deposit worked because it was narrow (busy days, new online bookers, repeat offenders) and explained plainly, rather than applied to everyone.

## Case 3: A chair-rental shop sharing one brand

**The shop.** Union Avenue Barber Co. has five chairs. The owner, Lena, cuts on one of them. The other four are rented by independent barbers, each with their own prices, booking page, Instagram and client list. The shop has one sign, one Google Business Profile and one shop Instagram account.

**The baseline.** The shop's Google profile listed prices that only applied to Lena. Two renters had no link from the profile at all. The shop Instagram posted cuts by whoever remembered, sometimes with the wrong barber tagged. A "$5 off for students" post the previous autumn had caused a row when students asked for the discount at renters' chairs. Review replies were sporadic, and once a renter had replied, angrily, to a review about Lena. When asked, three of the five barbers said they were not sure who was allowed to reply to reviews on the shop's profile.

**The problem chosen.** Not a booking leak, exactly. The problem was that the shared brand was confusing to clients and causing friction between barbers, and the shop's local visibility (Chapter 4) was suffering for it.

**What Lena did.**

*Week 1.* Called a one-hour meeting on a Monday, with food, and talked through a responsibility chart (Chapter 12). Lena brought a draft generated from her notes by AI, with a list of areas her notes had missed. The barbers changed six lines. The final chart said: the shop owns the Google profile, the shop Instagram, shop-wide rules and review replies on the shop profile; each renter owns their prices, services, booking, client list, policies and own social accounts; shop-wide promotions apply to a renter's chair only if they opt in, in writing, each time.

*Week 2.* Wrote a shop brief covering only what the shop owns (address, parking, building hours, shared rules, and a list of barbers with a one-line specialty and booking link for each). Each renter wrote their own barber brief, using prompt 6 in Appendix A, and decided whether to share it with the shop. Three did.

*Week 3.* Rebuilt the Google profile around the shop brief. The services list showed the range of services offered in the shop without quoting individual barbers' prices; the description named the specialties; the booking link went to a simple page listing all five barbers with their own links. New photos included each barber at their station, with their agreement.

*Week 4.* Set up a shop-wide content rota: one shop Instagram post a week featuring a different barber in turn, written with AI from the barber's own notes, approved by that barber before posting, with their own booking link. Lena took over review replies on the shop profile, using the prompts in Chapter 6, and agreed to pass any review about a particular barber to that barber before replying, so they could add context or ask her not to reply.

**The results.** After three months:

- No more pricing disputes at the chair. Questions on the shop channels about a specific barber went to that barber's link, every time.
- The shop profile went from 61 reviews, mostly old, to 104, with a reply on every one. Reviews increasingly mentioned barbers by name, which helped each of them.
- Renters reported more new clients saying they found them "on Google" or "on the shop's Instagram". Since each renter tracked their own sources, Lena did not have exact numbers for the whole shop, and she did not ask for them. Two renters chose to share theirs: first visits citing Google or the shop account went from 4 to 11 per month between them.
- One renter opted out of the shop content rota entirely, preferring to run her own feed. That was fine; the chart said she could.

**The lesson.** In a chair-rental shop, the first job is not marketing. It is agreeing who owns what, so that marketing does not create arguments. Once that was settled, AI made the shared work (the brief, the profile, the weekly post, the review replies) quick enough for one person to do without resentment, and each barber kept full control of their own business.

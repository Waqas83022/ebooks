# Chapter 10: No-shows, rebooking and the regulars who drifted away

Every barber has a client like Marcus. For three years he came in every third Thursday at 5:30. Same cut, same chair, same conversation about the Lakers. Then one month he did not come. Dre noticed, sort of, in the way you notice that a song you used to hear on the radio has stopped playing. Six months later, Dre saw Marcus at the gas station with a fresh cut from somewhere else.

Nothing went wrong. Marcus changed jobs, his new office was across town, and one Thursday he got a cut near work because it was easier. Then the next one. Nobody from Cedar Street ever asked him to come back.

This chapter is about the end of the journey from Chapter 2: making sure booked clients show up, that they leave with their next visit booked, and that the ones who drift get a friendly nudge before they become someone else's regular. It is the cheapest growth in the book, because every one of these people already knows you and likes your work.

## Why this is where the money is

Look again at the numbers from Zenoti's 2026 benchmark. New-guest visits at barbershops in its data fell 17% in 2025; existing-guest visits grew 2%. The same report looked at rebooking and cancellations across the salon businesses on its platform, barbershops included. Among clients who had rebooked twice or more, the cancellation rate was 4%. That is a vendor's data from its own customers, so treat it as a signal rather than a law. But it points at something most barbers know by instinct: once a client is in a rhythm of booking the next visit, they tend to stay.

Your own numbers will tell you more. At Cedar Street, only 22% of eligible clients left with the next appointment booked. Imagine raising that to 50%. Those are clients whose next visit is already in the calendar, who get a reminder, and who are far less likely to drift to the place near their new job.

## Part one: getting booked clients to show up

Start with no-shows, because they are the most annoying and, for most shops, the easiest to reduce.

First, know your number. Use the definition from Chapter 2: no-shows divided by appointments that were due. Count late cancellations separately. Published figures for barbershops vary hugely, from the low single digits in some booking-software data to well over 10% in surveys of individual shops, partly because everyone counts differently. Your own rate, measured the same way every week, is the only one that matters.

If yours is low (Cedar Street's was 3.6%), do not spend a month on it. If it is high, here are the fixes, roughly in order of cost.

### Reminders that are actually useful

Most booking systems send reminders automatically. Most shops never look at what those reminders say. Check yours. A good reminder:

- Arrives at a useful time. A common pattern is one the day before and one a couple of hours before. Test what works for your clients.
- Says the day, time, barber and service clearly.
- Makes rescheduling easy, with a link, so clients move the appointment instead of skipping it.
- States your late and cancellation policy in one short, friendly line.

::: prompt
Rewrite my barbershop's appointment reminders. I need two: one sent the day before and one sent 2 hours before. Use [first name], [day], [time], [barber], [service], [reschedule link]. Include our policy in one friendly line: cancel or move up to 12 hours ahead at no charge; 10-minute grace for late arrivals. Under 40 words each. Tone: from my shop brief. No emojis.
:::

Cedar Street's day-before reminder became:

> Hi [first name], see you tomorrow at [time] with [barber] for your [service]. Need to move it? Use this link up to 12 hours before: [reschedule link]. Cedar Street Barbers

Reminders about an appointment the client booked are service messages, not marketing. Keep them that way: no promotions bolted on the end.

### Deposits and card-on-file, used carefully

A deposit or a card on file, with a clear fee for no-shows, reduces no-shows in most shops that use it. It also puts off some clients, particularly new ones and walk-in-minded regulars, and it adds friction to booking. Many booking platforms report big drops in no-shows after shops turn deposits on, but those figures usually come from the platforms selling the feature, without much detail on how they were measured.

A middle path that works well for many shops is a deposit only where the risk is: first-time online bookings, long services (a 75-minute full service), peak Saturday slots, and clients who have no-showed before. Cedar Street's rule is in its brief: two no-shows in six months means future bookings need a $10 deposit, applied to the service.

If you change your policy, write it out plainly, put it on the booking page, in the brief and in the reminders, and explain it in person to regulars before it bites them. AI can help with the wording:

::: prompt
Write a short, friendly explanation of a new no-show policy for my barbershop's booking page and a 2-sentence version for reminder texts. Policy: [describe it]. Explain why (empty chairs mean lost income for the barber and lost time for clients who wanted that slot) without guilt-tripping. Under 70 words for the booking page.
:::

### The no-show recovery message

When someone does not show, send a single message that same day. Not a scolding. A door left open.

> Hey [first name], we missed you today at [time]. No worries, life happens. Want to rebook? Here's the link: [booking link]

Most people who no-show feel slightly embarrassed. A friendly message makes rebooking easy; a cold one or silence makes it easier to try another shop. Record the no-show in your booking system so the deposit rule applies if it becomes a habit.

## Part two: book the next visit before they leave

This is the single biggest habit change in the chapter, and it costs nothing.

When the cut is finished and the client is looking in the mirror, happy, that is the moment to book the next one. Not at the register while they are reaching for their card, and not in a text three weeks later. In the chair, while they are pleased.

The line Dre and Tomás settled on:

> "Want me to put you in for the same time in three weeks? I can see what's open."

Three things make it work. It is a question, not a sales pitch. It suggests a specific interval, which is a helpful bit of expertise (a skin fade grows out faster than a standard taper). And "I can see what's open" means the barber picks up the phone or tablet and books it right there, rather than asking the client to remember.

If they say no, that is fine. "No problem, I'll send you a reminder when you're due, if that's OK." Which leads to the next part.

### Know each service's return interval

Different services have different natural rhythms. Write yours down, because it drives the rebooking question, the reminders and the win-back messages:

| Service | Typical return | Overdue after |
|---|---|---|
| Skin fade | 2 to 3 weeks | 5 weeks |
| Standard cut | 3 to 5 weeks | 7 weeks |
| Beard trim and line-up | 1 to 2 weeks | 4 weeks |
| Kids cut | 4 to 6 weeks | 8 weeks |

These are Cedar Street's, from its own data; yours may differ. AI can find yours:

::: prompt
Here's a year of appointment data, names replaced with client IDs. For each service, calculate the median number of days between visits for clients who came at least 3 times. Then suggest an "overdue" threshold for each service: roughly the point where most clients who are going to return already have. Show the counts behind each number.
:::

### Measure it, every week

**Checkout rebooking rate** = eligible completed visits that left with a future appointment ÷ eligible completed visits.

Decide in advance what "eligible" means. A tourist, a wedding guest from out of town or a client who says they are moving away is not eligible. Then put the number on the scorecard and talk about it with your barbers weekly. Cedar Street's went from 22% to 41% in the first month after both barbers started asking at the mirror, and to 48% by the third month. Tomás, who had felt awkward asking, found it easier once he had a single line he said the same way every time.

## Part three: bring back the regulars who drifted

Some clients will always say no at the mirror, and some who rebook will cancel. That is where a win-back message comes in: a friendly note to a regular who has gone past their usual return interval.

### Permission first

This is the part people skip, and it can be expensive. In the United States, the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) and the Federal Communications Commission's rules under it govern marketing texts. As a general rule, sending marketing texts with an automated system requires the client's prior express written consent, a clear opt-out (replying STOP must work and must be honored promptly), and respect for quiet hours, which under the federal rules means no telephone solicitations before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. in the recipient's time zone. Some states add their own rules, and lawsuits over marketing texts are common.

A win-back message ("we've got openings, come back") is marketing. A reminder about an appointment the client already booked is not. Treat them differently.

In practice:

- Ask for marketing consent clearly and separately at booking, or on a paper or tablet form at checkout. A checkbox like "Text me about openings and offers from Cedar Street. Reply STOP any time" works if it is not pre-ticked, and your booking system should record it.
- Keep a record of who agreed, when and how.
- Include opt-out wording in marketing texts, and honor it immediately.
- Send marketing texts during reasonable daytime hours.
- Most texting platforms that send business messages in the US also require you to register your business and use case with the carriers (often called 10DLC registration). Your booking or texting provider will usually walk you through it.

Email has its own rules (in the US, the CAN-SPAM Act), which are less strict on consent but require an unsubscribe link and your business address. Rules in Canada, the UK, the EU and Australia are generally stricter. If you are not sure, keep win-back messages for clients who have clearly opted in, and ask your booking provider what their compliance features cover. For anything high-stakes, ask a lawyer.

This sounds like a lot. In practice, once your booking system records consent and handles opt-outs, it is a checkbox.

### The win-back message

Once a month, pull the list of clients who are past their "overdue" threshold, have opted in to marketing, have nothing booked, and have not had a win-back message in the last couple of months. Your booking system can usually filter this; if not, AI can help you sort an export (names replaced with IDs, as always).

Then send a short, personal-sounding message. It should not sound like a campaign, even though it is one.

::: prompt
Write three versions of a win-back text for regulars who haven't been to my barbershop in longer than usual and have opted in to marketing texts. Use [first name], [barber], [booking link] and [opt-out wording]. Under 35 words. Warm, low-pressure, from the barber who usually cuts them. No discount, no guilt ("we miss you!!"), no fake urgency. One version should mention weekday openings.
:::

The one Dre uses most:

> Hey [first name], it's Dre at Cedar Street. It's been a while, hope all's good. If you're due for a cut, I've got openings this week: [booking link]. [Opt-out wording]

Notice there is no discount. Start without one. Most lapsed regulars do not need a bribe; they need a reminder and an easy link. Measure what plain messages do before you add an offer, exactly as you did in Chapter 9.

### Measure it honestly

**Win-back rate** = clients who completed a visit within, say, 30 days of the message ÷ clients messaged.

Some of those clients would have come back anyway. For a fairer picture, hold back a small random group each month (every fifth eligible client, for example) and do not message them. Compare how many of each group returned. If the messaged group returns more often, the difference is roughly what the message did. If not, rewrite the message or reconsider the timing.

When Dre ran this for three months, 61 eligible lapsed regulars got the message, and 14 completed a visit within 30 days (23%). Of the 15 held back, 2 came back on their own (13%). So the messages were bringing back roughly one extra client in every ten messaged. Small numbers, but each of those clients was worth several visits a year, and the whole thing took twenty minutes a month.

### The ones who left for a reason

Some lapsed clients left because something went wrong: a bad cut, a long wait, a rude moment. A win-back text will not fix that. If a client replies with a complaint, treat it as one (Chapter 6): thank them, pass it to the owner, and call.

And some clients moved, changed jobs or simply found somewhere closer. That is life. Marcus, it turned out, was happy with his new barber near work. He still books with Dre in December, though, when he is home for two weeks. That December visit happens because Dre texted him in November.

## Before your next shift

- Check your reminder messages, and rewrite them so rescheduling is one tap.
- Write a no-show recovery message and send it the same day, every time.
- Agree on one rebooking line with your barbers, and start counting the checkout rebooking rate.
- Set a return interval and an "overdue" threshold for each service.
- Check how your booking system records marketing consent. Fix it before you send any win-back message.

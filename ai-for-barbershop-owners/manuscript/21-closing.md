# Closing: the chair is still the point {.unnumbered}

A few months after Cedar Street started the work in this book, a regular named Earl came in for his usual Thursday cut. Earl is 71, has been coming to the shop since the week it opened, and does not own a smartphone. He has never booked online and never will. He calls the shop, and if nobody answers, he calls again.

That Thursday, Earl mentioned that his wife had been in hospital. Dre stopped cutting for a minute, put the clippers down, and listened. They talked for most of the cut. Dre ran eight minutes over. The next client got a text from Jaylen at the front desk saying Dre was running a little behind, and was fine with it.

That is the whole idea of this book, in one Thursday afternoon. The missed-call text, the saved replies, the scorecard, the reminders, the walk-in board: none of it is the point. They exist to take the small, repetitive, forgettable work off your hands so you have more time and attention for the part no software can do. The part where a man who has sat in your chair for eleven years tells you something he has not told anyone else.

AI tools will keep changing. By the time you read this, some of the products I named will have new features, new prices, or new owners. That is why the methods in this book do not depend on any one of them. Give the assistant your real facts. Let it draft; you decide. Count completed visits, not likes. Change one thing at a time. Keep client details out of chat windows. Ask everyone for an honest review and reply to every one. Never fake anything.

Do those things with any tool, and your shop will be easier to find, easier to book, and easier to come back to. And you will have a few more hours each week to spend on the cut in front of you and the person under the cape.

That is a good trade.

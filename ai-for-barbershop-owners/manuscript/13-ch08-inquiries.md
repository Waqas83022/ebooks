# Chapter 8: Never lose a booking to a missed call

Dre's two-week count in Chapter 2 found 31 missed calls, and 19 of those callers never heard back. He thought about that number on the drive home. Nineteen people had picked up a phone, found the shop's number and dialed it, which is a lot of effort for a haircut. They were ready to book. And the shop had answered them with silence.

He knew why. When you are cutting, you cannot answer the phone. When you finish, there is a client at the register, another in the chair, and a guy asking about Saturday. By the end of the day, a missed call from 2:40 p.m. feels too old to return. So you don't.

This chapter is about making sure every person who reaches out, by phone, text, DM or walking in, gets a fast, accurate reply that ends with a way to book. Some of it is automation. Most of it is a few templates and a simple rule about who answers what.

## One rule: every reply ends with a way to book

Clients reach a barbershop through more channels than any other small business I can think of: the shop phone, the barber's personal phone, texts, Instagram DMs, Facebook messages, WhatsApp, the booking app's chat, a comment under a post, and the front door.

You cannot make all of those go away, and you should not try. People will contact you the way they prefer. What you can do is make every channel lead to the same place: your booking link, or a confirmed slot entered in your calendar by a person.

That is the rule for every template in this chapter. No reply ends with "let me know!" or "hit me up". It ends with the link, or with "I've booked you for 3:30 Thursday with Tomás, see you then", entered in the calendar before the message is sent.

## The missed-call text-back

This is the single most effective fix in this book for most shops, and it is nearly free.

When a call to the shop goes unanswered, the caller automatically gets a text within a minute or two. Many business phone services, VoIP apps and booking platforms can do this; check the settings of whatever you use, or ask your provider. If yours cannot, a cheap business phone app that can is worth switching to.

The text should do three things: tell them who it is, give them the fastest way to book, and invite them to reply if they have a question.

> Hi, this is Cedar Street Barbers. Sorry we missed your call, we're probably mid-cut. You can book any time here: [booking link]. Or reply to this text and we'll get back to you between clients.

That is all. Short, friendly, useful. It is a reply to someone who just called you, about the thing they called about, so it is not a marketing message. Keep it that way: no offers, no promotions, no "follow us on Instagram".

Then, for anyone who replies by text instead of booking, answer between clients using the saved replies below.

Dre set this up through his phone provider in about twenty minutes. In the next two weeks, the shop missed 28 calls. Eleven of those callers booked online straight from the text. Another nine replied with a question and were answered within the hour. Of the remaining eight, Dre called back four at the end of the day. Missed-call recovery went from 39% to 86%, and at least eleven bookings came from people who in the old system would have heard nothing.

## A voicemail that helps

Some people still leave voicemails, and most voicemail greetings waste their time. Rewrite yours:

::: prompt
Write a voicemail greeting for my barbershop, under 20 seconds when spoken aloud. Say the shop name, that we're probably cutting, that the fastest way to book is the link we'll text them, and that they can leave a message if they have a question. Mention our hours from the brief. Natural, not corporate. Read it aloud and count: under 50 words.
:::

Record it in a quiet moment, in your own voice. A real barber's voice beats a robotic one.

## Saved replies for the questions you answer every day

Look at your DMs and texts from the last month. You will find that about ten questions make up most of them. Cedar Street's were:

1. How much is a cut / fade / beard?
2. Do you have anything today / tomorrow / Saturday?
3. Do you take walk-ins?
4. Where are you / where do I park?
5. Can I book with [barber]?
6. Do you cut kids? How old?
7. How long does a [service] take?
8. Do you cut textured or curly hair?
9. I need to cancel / move my appointment.
10. Are you open on [holiday]?

Write one reply for each, built from your shop brief, and save them where you will use them. Instagram and Facebook both have saved replies in their business tools; your phone's keyboard can store text shortcuts; your booking app may have message templates.

::: prompt
Using only my shop brief, write saved replies for these 10 questions clients send my barbershop by text and DM. Each reply under 40 words. Each ends with the booking link [booking link] or, for questions about a specific barber who rents a chair, that barber's own link. For questions about availability, never name a specific time; send the link or say we'll check and reply. Write [CHECK] where the brief doesn't give the answer.

Questions: [paste your ten]\
Shop brief: [paste]
:::

Read every reply, fix the tone, fill the [CHECK] gaps, and test them with a friend who texts you the questions. Then use them. Replying to "how much for a fade" now takes three seconds instead of thirty, and the answer is always right.

## What to do with "Do you have anything today?"

This is the question a chat assistant must never answer on its own unless it is connected to your live calendar, because the honest answer changes every few minutes.

There are two good replies. Either send the link ("Here's what's open today, it's live: [link]"), or check the calendar yourself and offer a real slot. If you offer a slot, put it in the calendar before or immediately after sending, so nobody else books it.

> Hey, Tomás has 4:15 today for a skin fade. Want it? I'll hold it for 15 minutes.

And then actually hold it: mark it in the calendar as "held for DM, 15 min", and release it if they do not reply. A slot promised in a DM and booked online by someone else five minutes later is how you lose two clients at once.

## Handing a booking across barbers

Sometimes a client asks for one barber who is full, and another barber is free. Handle this with care, especially in a shop with chair renters, where each barber's client list is their own business.

The rule at Cedar Street:

- **Between Dre and Tomás** (both on the shop's books): the front desk may offer the other barber, by name, saying why. "Tomás is booked until Friday. Dre has 2:30 tomorrow if you'd like it, or I can put you on Tomás's waitlist."
- **With Keisha** (a renter): the shop never moves Keisha's clients to anyone else, and never books into Keisha's calendar. If someone asks for her, they get her link. If someone asks the shop for a textured cut and Dre and Tomás are both full, the shop may say "Keisha specializes in textured hair; here is her booking link", because she agreed to that in advance.

Write your version down. It prevents a surprising number of arguments.

## The walk-in who cannot be seen

On a busy Saturday, someone walks in and there is no chance of a chair for two hours. The worst outcome is that they leave and you never see them again. The second worst is that they wait 40 minutes, give up, and write a review.

Give them a way to book a future time, on the spot. At Cedar Street there is a small card by the door: *We're fully booked right now. Grab your next cut here*, with a QR code to the booking page. Whoever is nearest says: "Sorry, we're booked up until 2. Here's the card, you can book any open time in ten seconds. Tuesday and Wednesday afternoons are usually easy."

That last sentence does double duty: it is honest, and it steers walk-ins toward your quiet hours.

## AI receptionists: worth it, and when

An AI receptionist answers your shop phone (and sometimes your messages) with a natural-sounding voice. Depending on the product, it can tell callers your hours and prices, send a booking link by text, check your live calendar, book an appointment, take a message, or transfer the call.

Some barber and salon booking platforms now offer one as a paid add-on, connected to their own calendar. Independent services also exist that connect to popular calendars. Prices vary widely, usually a monthly fee with some limit on minutes or calls, and they change often enough that I will not quote them. Check current pricing and read the terms on call recording and data.

Are they worth it? It depends on how many calls you miss after the text-back is in place. Do the text-back first; it is far cheaper, and for many shops it solves most of the problem. If you still miss a lot of calls, especially from people who will not book online (older clients, people with questions), an AI receptionist may be worth testing.

If you test one, judge it on these questions:

1. **Does it see your live calendar?** If not, it can only send a link or take a message. That may still be useful, but it is not a receptionist.
2. **What does it say when it does not know?** Call it and ask for a service you do not offer, a price that is not on your menu, and a time you are closed. It should say it does not know, or take a message. It must not invent.
3. **How does it hand off?** Can it transfer to a person, or send you a summary immediately when a caller is upset or asks for something unusual?
4. **What does it cost per month, and per extra minute?** Work out the cost per completed booking it produces, the same way you did for ads.
5. **Does it record calls, and does it tell callers?** Some US states require everyone on a call to consent to a recording. Make sure the greeting discloses it and that you understand the vendor's terms.
6. **Can you switch it off instantly?** You want a way to pause it in seconds if it starts giving wrong answers.

Then test it like you tested your shop brief: twenty scripted calls, including the awkward ones (an angry client, a parent booking for a child, someone asking for a barber who left, someone asking about a scalp condition). Run it after hours only for the first two weeks, review every call summary, and expand only when you trust it.

Remember the Air Canada case from the introduction. If your AI receptionist promises a client a $30 fade, that promise is yours.

## Response time: a simple standard

How fast is fast enough? There is no magic number, but people who contact a barbershop are usually choosing between you and one or two others, and the first useful reply often wins.

Cedar Street's standard is simple:

- Missed calls get the automatic text within two minutes, always.
- Texts and DMs during opening hours get a reply between clients, within 30 minutes.
- Messages after hours get an automatic reply where the channel allows ("We're closed for the night; book any time here: [link]. We'll answer questions from 10 a.m."), and a personal reply when the shop opens.
- One person checks all channels at opening, at lunch and at close. That person is named on the schedule each day.

That last line is the one that makes the rest work. "Everybody checks the DMs" means nobody does.

## The front-desk script sheet

Put your most-used replies on one page, by the register, and in the notes app of everyone who answers for the shop. Appendix B has a full set. Cedar Street's sheet has six entries:

- Missed-call text-back.
- "Do you have anything today?" (send the link, or offer and hold a real slot).
- Walk-in when fully booked (the card and the line about Tuesday afternoons).
- Booking with a full barber (offer the other employee by name, or the waitlist; renters get their own link).
- Running-late client ("Thanks for the heads-up. We can still do a [shorter service] in the time left, or move you to [next slot]. Which works?").
- Reschedule request (the link, or offer two real times).

AI drafted them all from the brief in one go. Dre edited each one until it sounded like him, then had Tomás and his nephew Jaylen read them aloud to check they sounded natural.

## Before your next shift

- Turn on a missed-call text-back on the shop phone.
- Rewrite and re-record your voicemail greeting.
- Write saved replies for your top ten questions and load them into Instagram, Facebook and your phone.
- Decide who checks messages at opening, lunch and close, and put a name on each day of the schedule.
- Print a walk-in card with a QR code to your booking page.

# Chapter 2: Find where your shop is losing bookings

Ask most barbershop owners what their business needs and you will hear the same answer: more clients. More followers, more new faces, more people walking in.

Dre said exactly that. "I need new clients. Tuesdays are dead, Wednesdays are dead, and the young guys all go to the place with the neon sign."

He was half right. Tuesdays and Wednesdays were quiet. But when he sat down and counted, he found something he did not expect. In two weeks, the shop phone had missed 31 calls. Nineteen of those callers never heard back. Only about one in five clients left the shop with their next appointment booked. And of the 26 first-time clients in those two weeks, a later check showed only nine had come back within six weeks.

Dre did not have a new-client problem first. He had a bucket with holes in it. Pouring more water in (ads, posts, a neon sign of his own) would have meant paying to fill a bucket that was leaking at the bottom.

This chapter shows you how to find the holes in your own bucket, using a phone log, your booking calendar and one simple scorecard. It takes about an hour to set up and ten minutes a week after that. It is the least exciting chapter in the book and possibly the most useful.

## The journey from stranger to regular

Every regular in your chair today was once a stranger who did not know your shop existed. Between those two points, they went through a series of steps, and at every step some people drop out.

![Figure 2. The booking leak map. Every stage loses some people. Your job is to find the stage that loses the most.](images/fig02-leak-map.png)

Here are the stages, with the most common leaks at each.

**Found you.** Someone searches "barber near me", sees a post, walks past, or hears your name from a friend. Leaks: your Google listing shows the wrong hours or no photos; you do not appear in search at all; your posts never mention where you are.

**Chose you.** They look at your reviews, photos, prices and booking options and decide whether to try you. Leaks: few or old reviews; photos that do not show the cuts they want; no prices anywhere; no obvious way to book.

**Reached you.** They call, message, click the booking link or walk in. Leaks: calls go unanswered; DMs sit for a day; the booking link is broken or buried; walk-ins see a full shop and leave.

**Booked.** They get an appointment. Leaks: the online calendar shows no availability even though the barber has gaps; the booking form is too long; the deposit rule scares them off without explanation.

**Showed up.** They arrive. Leaks: no reminder; reminder sent too late; no easy way to reschedule, so they just do not come.

**Paid and left happy.** The cut, the conversation, the checkout. Leaks: long waits past the appointment time; a rushed cut; a surprise price.

**Came back.** They become a regular. Leaks: nobody offered them the next appointment; no reminder when they were due; no reason to choose you over the shop that is closer to their new job.

Here is the important part. A leak near the end of the journey is usually cheaper to fix than a leak at the start. A client who has already sat in your chair and liked the cut is the easiest booking in the world. Asking "same time in three weeks?" at checkout costs nothing. Finding a brand-new client through advertising costs real money. So when you look for leaks, start from the chair and work outward.

## Count your sellable chair time

Before you measure anything else, you need to know how much time you actually have to sell. This number is the denominator for half the measures in this book.

Sellable time is the time a barber is in the shop and available to cut, minus time that is not for sale: lunch, cleaning, breaks, meetings, admin blocks. It is not your opening hours, because nobody cuts for nine straight hours without eating.

Here is Cedar Street, for Dre's chair:

- Tuesday to Friday, 10 a.m. to 7 p.m.: 9 hours a day, 36 hours.
- Saturday, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m.: 9 hours.
- Total time in the shop: 45 hours.
- Minus a 30-minute lunch each day (5 days): 2.5 hours.
- Minus 30 minutes a day for cleaning, restocking and the end-of-day close: 2.5 hours.
- Sellable time: 40 hours, or 2,400 minutes a week.

Do this for each barber, because their hours differ. Tomás works Tuesday to Saturday too, so his number is similar. Keisha, as a chair renter, sets her own hours, and her numbers belong to her. If she chooses to share them, great; if not, you track the shop-level things you control, such as the phone and the Google listing.

Once you know sellable time, you can work out the measure most shop owners never calculate:

**Chair utilization = completed service minutes ÷ sellable minutes.**

If Dre completed 51 hours of cuts over two weeks (3,060 minutes) out of 4,800 sellable minutes, his utilization was 64%. Count *completed* minutes: a no-show does not count, and neither does a 45-minute slot where the barber stood around because the client was 25 minutes late.

For a rough sense of scale, Zenoti's 2026 benchmark report put the median staff utilization at barbershops on its platform at 56%, with the top tenth of shops at 75%. Those are one company's figures from its own customers, so do not treat them as a law of nature. They do give you a sense of what "busy" looks like. A barber at 50% has roughly half of their sellable time empty. Moving from 56% to 66% on a 40-hour week is four extra hours of cutting, every week, without a single new chair.

## Seven numbers to count for two weeks

Pick two normal weeks. Avoid the week before a major holiday, school picture day, or the week the street was dug up. Then count these seven numbers. Most can be pulled from your booking system; one or two need a tally sheet by the register.

**1. Completed service minutes, per barber.** From your booking export. If your system does not show minutes, multiply each service by its standard time from the menu.

**2. Calls missed, and calls never returned.** Check your phone's call log at the end of each day. Count calls that rang out while you were busy, then count how many of those you or anyone else called or texted back. The second number is the painful one.

**3. Messages left waiting more than two hours.** Instagram DMs, Facebook messages, Google messages, texts. You do not need precision. A tick on a sheet when you notice one that has waited too long is fine.

**4. Walk-ins turned away or who left without a cut.** A tally by the door. Saturday mornings are where this hides.

**5. No-shows and late cancellations.** From your booking system. A no-show is a client who did not come and did not tell you. A late cancellation is one who cancelled inside your cutoff (for example, less than 24 hours ahead). Count them separately; they have different fixes.

**6. Checkout rebooking.** Of the clients who finished a visit, how many left with their next appointment already booked? Count only clients for whom rebooking makes sense. A tourist passing through or a one-off wedding trim does not count.

**7. First-time clients, and how many came back.** Count first-timers in the two weeks. Then, six weeks later, check how many of them returned. Six weeks works for most standard cuts; if most of your clients come every two to three weeks, use four.

That is it. You do not need ten dashboards. Seven numbers, two weeks, one sheet.

## Turn the numbers into rates

Raw counts are hard to compare. Turn each one into a rate, and always keep the number underneath so you can see how big the sample is.

- **Missed-call recovery rate** = missed calls that got a callback or text ÷ missed calls.
- **No-show rate** = no-shows ÷ appointments due in the period. Do not include appointments cancelled ahead of time in the bottom number, because those were not "due" anymore.
- **Checkout rebooking rate** = eligible completed visits that left with a future booking ÷ eligible completed visits.
- **First-visit return rate** = first-timers who came back within your window ÷ first-timers.

Here is Cedar Street's two-week baseline for the shop as a whole (Dre and Tomás; Keisha tracked her own):

| Measure | Count | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Chair utilization, Dre | 3,060 of 4,800 min | 64% |
| Chair utilization, Tomás | 2,520 of 4,800 min | 53% |
| Missed calls returned | 12 of 31 | 39% |
| No-shows | 9 of 248 due | 3.6% |
| Checkout rebooking | 47 of 214 eligible | 22% |
| First-timers back within 6 weeks | 9 of 26 | 35% |

Look at that table and the story jumps out. No-shows were low; Dre had been worrying about the wrong thing. The real holes were missed calls (19 people who reached out and got silence), rebooking (four out of five clients walking out with nothing booked), and first-timers who never returned.

## Compare like with like

One rule will save you from a lot of bad decisions: compare the same weekdays and the same times of year.

Saturdays are always busier than Tuesdays. The week before school starts is always busier than the week after. December is different from February. If you change something on a Tuesday and compare it with last Saturday, you will learn nothing. If you run a promotion in the week before Christmas and declare it a success, you may be congratulating yourself for the calendar.

So when you check whether something worked, compare:

- Tuesday afternoons with previous Tuesday afternoons, not with the week's average.
- This March with last March, if you have the data, or with the four weeks before.
- The same barber with themselves. Tomás's numbers and Dre's numbers are different businesses in some ways; Tomás has been at the shop eight months and Dre eleven years.

And keep the denominators. "We got 6 rebookings" means nothing until you know whether it was 6 of 10 clients or 6 of 100.

## Let AI read the spreadsheet

You do not need to be good with spreadsheets. Most booking systems let you export appointments to a CSV file, which opens in Excel or Google Sheets. Before you go near an AI tool, remove client names, phone numbers and emails. Replace each client with a number if you need to tell repeat visits apart. Then paste the cleaned data, or upload the file if your assistant supports it, with a prompt like this:

::: prompt
Below is two weeks of appointment data from my barbershop, with client names removed. Columns: date, weekday, start time, barber, service, booked length in minutes, status (completed, no-show, cancelled, late-cancel), client ID, first visit (yes/no), next appointment booked at checkout (yes/no).

Sellable time per barber is 2,400 minutes per week.

Please:

1. Calculate chair utilization for each barber using completed minutes only.
2. List the weekday and time blocks (in 2-hour blocks) with the most unbooked sellable time.
3. Calculate the no-show rate and the checkout rebooking rate, and show the counts behind each rate.
4. Tell me which single problem looks biggest, and explain why in two or three sentences.

Show your arithmetic. If any data is missing or unclear, say so and do not guess.

[paste data here]
:::

Then check its arithmetic on one or two numbers yourself. AI assistants have become much better at math, but they can still slip, especially with long pasted tables. If the answer does not match what you counted by hand, trust your count and ask the assistant to recheck. Asking it to "show your arithmetic" makes mistakes far easier to spot.

What you get back is a plain summary, something like: *Tomás's chair is 47% empty on Tuesday and Wednesday between noon and 4 p.m. The checkout rebooking rate is 22% (47 of 214). Most first-time clients booked a skin fade.* That last line is a clue for Chapter 5: if new clients come for skin fades, your posts should show skin fades.

## Build the weekly scorecard

Once you have a baseline, keep the same measures going every week. A scorecard is just a single page you look at on the same day each week, ideally the day the shop is closed, with a coffee.

![Figure 3. The Cedar Street weekly scorecard. Six numbers, one page, ten minutes a week.](images/fig03-scorecard.png)

The weekly scorecard has six lines:

1. Chair utilization, per barber (completed minutes ÷ sellable minutes).
2. Missed-call and message recovery (contacts answered or returned ÷ total).
3. No-show rate and late-cancel rate (each ÷ appointments due).
4. Checkout rebooking rate (eligible visits with a future booking ÷ eligible visits).
5. First visits completed this week, and where they came from.
6. One note: what we changed this week, and anything unusual (weather, a holiday, a barber off sick).

That sixth line matters more than it looks. Six weeks from now, when Tuesday suddenly jumps, you will want to know whether it was your new waitlist or the fact that the school down the road had a teacher training day. Write it down while you remember.

You can keep the scorecard in a spreadsheet, on paper, or in a notes app. Appendix C has a version you can copy. What matters is that you look at it every week and that the numbers are counted the same way each time.

## Pick one bottleneck

Now the hardest part: choose one problem to work on this month. One.

It is tempting to fix everything at once. Resist that. If you change your reminders, launch an ad, start posting daily and change your prices in the same month, and bookings go up, you will not know why. If they go down, you will not know which change to undo.

To choose, ask three questions about each leak:

- How many bookings does it cost each week? (Nineteen unreturned calls in two weeks is about nine or ten lost contacts a week. If even half would have booked, that is five appointments a week.)
- How cheap is it to fix? (Calling back missed callers costs time and a text template. Ads cost money.)
- How close is it to the chair? (Closer is usually cheaper and faster.)

Dre looked at his table. Missed calls: about five lost bookings a week, almost free to fix. Rebooking: big potential, also free, but needed a habit change at the chair for both barbers. First-timer returns: important, but partly depended on fixing rebooking first. Quiet Tuesdays: real, but some of that empty time might fill itself once missed calls were answered.

He chose missed calls for month one. Rebooking would be month two. Chapter 8 shows what he did.

Your choice may be different. A shop with a 12% no-show rate should start there. A shop with great retention and no online presence should start with Chapter 4. The scorecard tells you; your gut can then argue with it.

## The 15-minute alternative

If counting seven numbers for two weeks feels like too much right now, do this instead. It takes fifteen minutes and gets you most of the way.

1. Open your phone's call log. Count missed calls in the last seven days and how many you returned.
2. Open your booking app. Count appointments next week, and the empty hours on Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday.
3. Think about your last ten clients. How many left with the next appointment booked?

If more than a few calls a week go unreturned, start with Chapter 8. If you have more than ten empty weekday hours next week, read Chapter 9. If fewer than half of your last ten clients rebooked, read Chapter 10. Then come back and do the full count when you have a quieter week.

## Before your next shift

- Work out sellable minutes for each barber, including yourself.
- Put a tally sheet by the register for missed calls, unanswered messages and walk-ins turned away. Run it for two weeks.
- Pull two weeks of appointments from your booking system and remove client names.
- After two weeks, pick one leak to fix this month. Write it at the top of the scorecard.

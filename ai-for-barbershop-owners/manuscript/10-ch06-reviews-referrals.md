# Chapter 6: Turn reviews and referrals into local discovery

The review arrived at 11:48 on a Saturday night. One star. *Showed up with my son at 9:15, they said walk-ins welcome, waited 40 min and nobody even looked at us. Barbers too busy talking to each other. Liars. Never again.*

Dre read it in bed. By 11:55 he had typed a reply that began *Maybe if you'd actually read our policy* and went downhill from there. He did not post it, which is the first good decision in this chapter. In the morning he read the review again, then pasted it into his assistant along with the shop brief and asked for help writing something he would not regret.

Reviews are the closest thing a small shop has to a loudspeaker. Good ones bring in people you will never meet until they sit in your chair. Bad ones, handled badly, can sit at the top of your profile for years. This chapter covers how to earn more honest reviews, how to reply to all of them, and how to turn happy clients and friendly neighbors into a steady trickle of new bookings.

## The rules, briefly, because they matter

Before the techniques, the boundaries. Some common review tactics are against platform rules, and some are against the law.

In August 2024 the US Federal Trade Commission announced a final rule on consumer reviews and testimonials. Among other things, it bans:

- fake reviews, including ones written by AI, by someone who never used the business, or that misrepresent the reviewer's experience;
- offering compensation or other incentives in exchange for reviews that express a particular sentiment, positive or negative, whether that condition is stated or merely implied;
- reviews by owners, managers or staff that do not clearly disclose their connection to the business;
- suppressing negative reviews through threats or intimidation, or presenting a filtered set of reviews as if it were all of them.

The FTC can seek civil penalties against knowing violators. Other countries have similar consumer-protection rules.

Google's own policy goes further than the FTC on one point. Its Business Profile help pages say that offering incentives, such as free or discounted goods or services, in exchange for customers posting reviews, changing reviews or removing negative ones counts as fake and misleading content and is strictly prohibited. That applies even if you never ask for a particular rating. Google has also said it may restrict profiles that repeatedly break its fake-engagement rules, on top of removing the reviews.

One more practice to avoid, even where no rule names it: asking only the clients who seem happy, or sending happy clients to Google and unhappy ones to a private feedback form. This is sometimes called review gating. It produces a rating that is better than your real one, which is exactly the kind of misleading picture both Google and the FTC are trying to stamp out.

In plain barbershop terms:

- **Do** ask every client for an honest review, the same way, at the same moment.
- **Do not** offer a discount, free line-up, raffle entry or anything else for a review. Not even "for any review, good or bad", on Google.
- **Do not** ask only the clients who seem happy, or send unhappy ones to a private form instead of the review site.
- **Do not** write reviews for clients, or let AI write them, even when a client says "you write it, I'll post it". Their words or nothing.
- **Do not** have staff, family or friends review the shop unless the connection is clearly disclosed, and on Google, simply avoid it.
- **Do not** threaten anyone over a negative review. Reply calmly and, if a review is fake or abusive, report it through the platform.

None of this stops you getting lots of reviews. It just means you get them the slow, honest way, which is the only way that lasts.

## Ask at a consistent moment

Most happy clients never leave a review because nobody asked, and the ones who did mean to forgot by the time they got to the car. So build the ask into the visit.

Choose one moment and stick to it. For most shops it is one of these:

- **At checkout, in person.** "Glad you like it. If you have a minute later, we'd really appreciate an honest review on Google. There's a QR code on the card." Hand them a small card with a QR code that opens your review page directly. Google gives you this link in your Business Profile ("Ask for reviews").
- **In a follow-up message, a couple of hours after the visit.** Most booking systems can send an automatic thank-you message after a completed appointment. Add the review link.

Do both if you like, but treat every client the same. The point is consistency: everybody gets asked, regardless of how the cut went.

Here is a follow-up message that does the job:

> Thanks for coming in today, [first name]. If you'd like to share how your visit went, here's our Google review link: [review link]. Honest feedback helps us, good or bad. Cedar Street Barbers

Track two numbers each month: how many review requests went out, and how many new reviews arrived. Do not mix them up. If requests go up and reviews do not, your ask needs work (the link is buried, the message is too long, the timing is wrong). If neither moves, nobody is asking.

When Cedar Street started handing out QR cards at checkout and adding the link to the automated thank-you text, new Google reviews went from roughly one a month to eleven in the first month and nine in the second. The average rating barely moved, which is what you would expect when you ask everyone honestly. What changed was the count and the recency, and it is hard to look established with seven-month-old reviews.

## Reply to every review

Reply to all of them: the five-star ones, the three-star ones, the one-star ones. Replies show new visitors that the shop is run by people who are paying attention, and they give you a chance to tell your side of a bad story politely.

AI is very good at this job, provided you give it rules.

### Replying to good reviews

The danger with positive replies is that they all sound the same. "Thank you for your kind words! We look forward to seeing you again!" forty times in a row looks like a robot, because it usually is. A good reply is short, uses something specific from the review, and sounds like the barber.

::: prompt
Draft a reply to this Google review of my barbershop. Use my shop brief for tone. Under 40 words. Mention one specific thing the reviewer said. Thank the barber they named, if any, by first name. No exclamation marks except at most one. Don't repeat the shop name more than once. Don't mention any client details beyond what's in the review.

Review: "[paste review]"
:::

Batch them. Once a week, paste in all new positive reviews and ask for a reply to each, each with a different opening line. It takes five minutes.

### Replying to bad reviews

This is where AI earns its place, because the hardest part of replying to a harsh review is not the writing. It is not writing while angry.

A good reply to a negative review:

- Thanks them for the feedback, briefly.
- Acknowledges what went wrong, in general terms, without arguing about the details in public.
- States the relevant fact or policy plainly, if there is a misunderstanding.
- Says what you have changed, if anything.
- Offers a way to talk privately: the owner's name and the shop phone or email.
- Never mentions private client details: no "you were late", "you no-showed twice", "you didn't tip", or anything from your booking records. Other people will read it.

Here is the prompt Dre used:

::: prompt
I need to reply to a negative Google review of my barbershop. Here's the review and what actually happened. Draft a reply that is calm and short (under 80 words), takes responsibility for what we got wrong, explains our policy plainly where there was a misunderstanding, says what we've changed, and invites the reviewer to contact me (Dre) directly at [shop phone]. Don't argue, don't blame the client, don't reveal any private details about the visit, and don't offer anything free or a discount in the public reply.

Review: "[paste review]"\
What happened: [your honest account, in a few lines]\
Shop brief: [paste]
:::

And the reply he posted, after one edit:

> I'm sorry about Saturday. You were told walk-ins were welcome, and on Saturday mornings we only take booked appointments until 11, so you waited for nothing. That message was our mistake, and we've fixed how we answer walk-in questions. I'd like to make it right. Please call me at [shop phone]. Dre, owner

Notice what is not there: no "per our policy", no blame, no detail about the client or his son. A new client reading that review now sees a shop that admits a mistake and fixes it. Several people who later booked mentioned it.

When the reviewer called, Dre offered him and his son a booked Saturday slot. That kind of make-good is fine in private, as a response to a genuine problem, so long as it is not conditional on changing or removing the review. Google treats incentives to change a review as fake engagement, and you do not want the conversation to sound like a trade.

### Reviews that are not real

Sometimes a review is plainly fake: a person who was never a client, a competitor, an angry ex-employee, or spam. You can report it to Google from your Business Profile. Google decides; you cannot force it. Meanwhile, a short, factual reply is better than silence:

> We can't find any record of a visit matching this, and we'd like to understand what happened. Please call me at [shop phone]. Dre, owner

Do not accuse anyone publicly. Do not threaten legal action in a reply. You will look worse than the review.

## Turn complaints into a handoff, not a fight

Most complaints never become reviews if they are handled quickly. Agree, in writing, what happens when a client complains in person, by text, by phone or in a DM. Here is Cedar Street's version, drafted with AI from Dre's notes and now pinned by the register:

1. Whoever receives the complaint thanks the client, listens, and does not argue or explain policy in the moment.
2. They write it down: date, client (in the booking system, not in a chat app), which barber, what happened, what the client wants.
3. Dre gets it the same day. If he is away, Tomás.
4. Dre contacts the client within 24 hours.
5. Standard fixes: a free correction within 7 days for a cut the client is unhappy with; a refund at Dre's discretion; an apology in every case.
6. The complaint goes in a log. Once a month, look for patterns.

AI can help at step 6. Once a month, paste the complaint log and your recent reviews (names removed) and ask:

::: prompt
Here are last month's complaints and reviews for my barbershop, with names removed. Group them into themes. For each theme, give the count, two short quotes, and one practical change we could test. Separate problems about the haircut itself from problems about booking, waiting, price or communication.
:::

At Cedar Street, the first monthly summary showed that four of the six complaints were about waiting, not cutting. That pointed straight at the Saturday walk-in rule and at Chapter 12.

## Referrals: your clients are your best ads

A happy regular who tells a friend is worth more than any ad, because the friend arrives already trusting you. A referral program does not create that behavior, but it can make it a little more likely and a lot more trackable.

Keep it simple, and decide three things before you announce it.

**The reward, and who gets it.** A common setup is "give $10, get $10": the new client gets $10 off their first visit, and the regular who referred them gets $10 off their next visit once the friend has *completed* that first visit. Rewards for completed visits, not bookings, keep no-shows from costing you money.

**The cost cap.** Decide the most you will spend in a month. At Cedar Street, the cap was 20 rewards a month, or $200 at most in discounts, reviewed after eight weeks.

**The redemption rule.** How the referral is recorded (the new client gives the regular's name at booking; or a code), when the reward can be used (next visit, within 90 days), and what it cannot be combined with.

And one firm line: referral rewards are for bringing a friend, never for leaving a review. Keep the two completely separate.

Now the arithmetic, because a referral program is a discount, and discounts cost money. A new client's first standard cut is $35. With the $10 welcome discount, the shop takes $25. The referring regular gets $10 off their next visit too. Assuming about $5 in direct product costs per cut, here is what one completed referral leaves behind on the first visit:

- Revenue from the new client's first cut: $35 − $10 = $25.
- Minus direct costs of that cut: $25 − $5 = $20.
- Minus the referrer's $10 reward on their next cut: $20 − $10 = $10.

So the first visit contributes about $10 instead of $30. That is the cost of acquiring the client: roughly $20 in discounts per new client. Compare it with ads in Chapter 7, which can easily cost more. And if the new client comes back four or five times a year at full price, $20 is cheap.

The risk is paying rewards for people who would have come anyway. If the regular's brother was going to book next week regardless, the $20 bought you nothing. You cannot avoid this completely, but the cap limits the damage, and the eight-week review tells you whether the program is creating new visits or just discounting existing ones.

AI can write the in-shop card and the message to regulars:

::: prompt
Write a short referral card for my barbershop's counter and a text message for regulars who've opted in to marketing. The offer: a new client gets $10 off their first visit; the regular who referred them gets $10 off their next visit after the friend completes a visit. New client must give the regular's name when booking. Reward valid for 90 days, one per visit, can't be combined with other offers. Max 20 rewards per month. Card under 40 words, text under 35 words. Include [booking link] and [opt-out wording if required] in the text. Plain, friendly, no hype.
:::

## Partnerships: borrow trust from your neighbors

Your neighborhood is full of businesses whose customers need a barber. Some are obvious: the gym, the boxing club, the tattoo studio, the men's clothing store, the suit rental shop, the wedding planner, the photographer who shoots proms and graduations. Some are less obvious: the dentist whose waiting room has a noticeboard, the youth football club, the coffee shop where your clients buy their morning cup.

A partnership does not need a contract. It needs a simple, fair swap, a way to track it, and a date to review it.

**Pick partners whose customers overlap with yours** and who you would genuinely recommend. If you would not send your own clients to them, do not ask them to send theirs to you.

**Offer something useful to their customers,** not just to them. A gym might put your card at its front desk while you put theirs at your mirror. A wedding photographer might mention you to grooms and include your booking link in their prep guide; you might offer a groom's-party booking block on Saturday mornings.

**Make it trackable.** Give each partner their own booking link or code, and ask "How did you find us?" at booking.

**Try it for a month or two, then review.** Did anyone come? Did the partner get anything back? Be honest; most partnerships fizzle, and that is fine.

Here is the prompt for a pitch that does not sound like spam:

::: prompt
Draft a short message to the owner of [business type, e.g. a boxing gym] three blocks from my barbershop. I know them a little. Propose a simple one-month trial: I put their card at my counter and mention them to clients who ask, they put ours at their front desk with our booking link [link with partner code]. At the end of the month we each check whether we sent or received any customers. Under 90 words. Friendly, direct, no marketing language, no pressure. Sign off as Dre from Cedar Street Barbers.
:::

Dre sent versions to the boxing gym, the tattoo studio and a suit rental shop. The gym said yes; the tattoo studio said maybe; the suit rental shop never replied. Over two months, the gym's code brought in seven completed first visits. Two of those clients now book every three weeks. The cost was a stack of business cards and one conversation.

## Before your next shift

- Set up one consistent review ask: a QR card at checkout, the link in your thank-you message, or both.
- Reply to every review from the last six months, using the prompts above. Start with the negative ones.
- Write down your complaint handoff and pin it by the register.
- If you want a referral program, decide the reward, the cap and the redemption rule before announcing it.
- Pitch one neighboring business on a one-month trial.

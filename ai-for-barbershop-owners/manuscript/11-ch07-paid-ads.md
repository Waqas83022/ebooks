# Chapter 7: Test paid ads with a fixed budget

Two years ago, Dre pressed the "Boost post" button on a photo of a fade. He spent $50. Instagram told him the post reached 3,100 people and got 42 likes. As far as he knows, nobody booked. He decided ads were a scam and never tried again.

Ads are not a scam, but "boost and hope" is a reliable way to lose money. The way Dre ran his first ad had no target audience beyond "people near here", no specific service, no booking link designed for the ad, no budget plan, and no way to know whether anyone who saw it came in. It was a $50 lottery ticket.

This chapter treats an ad as a small, controlled experiment. You decide in advance what you are testing, how much you will spend, how you will count results, and what result means "keep going" or "stop". AI helps you write the ads and read the results. The discipline is yours.

## First, should you run ads at all?

Paid ads make a working system bigger. They do not fix a broken one. Before you spend a dollar, check four things:

1. **You have empty time to sell, in specific windows.** If your chairs are 85% full, you do not need more clients; you might need higher prices (Chapter 11). If the gaps are Tuesday and Wednesday afternoons, that is what the ad should sell.
2. **You answer inquiries.** If calls go unreturned and DMs wait a day, ads pay to send people into the same hole. Fix Chapter 8 first.
3. **Your Google profile and booking page are in shape.** Many people who see an ad will look you up before booking. If they find wrong hours and four-year-old photos, the ad was wasted. Chapter 4 first.
4. **You can tell where a booking came from.** Without a tracking link or a "How did you find us?" habit, you will never know whether the ad worked.

If all four are true, an ad test is worth running. If not, the money is better spent on whichever of those four is missing, which is mostly free.

## Where barbershops advertise

There are three common places, each suited to a different job.

**Meta (Facebook and Instagram).** You show an image or short video to people in a radius around your shop. It reaches people who were not searching for a barber, so it works best with a strong photo and a clear reason to book now: a specific service, a barber with openings, a new barber starting. You set a daily or total budget. Ads Manager will show you the current minimum when you set it up.

**Google search ads.** You pay when someone who searched for something like "barber near me" or "skin fade [your area]" clicks your ad. These people are already looking, which is valuable, but popular local searches can be expensive per click in big cities. Your Google Business Profile, which is free, often does much of this job already.

**Booking marketplaces.** Some booking platforms list your shop in a consumer marketplace and charge a fee or commission for new clients they send you. That fee is advertising spend, even if it is not called that. Count it the same way: what does each completed first visit cost?

For most small shops, a Meta ad aimed at a radius around the shop is the simplest first test. The method below works for any of them.

## The ad test card

Before you open an ad manager, fill in a test card. It is a short list of decisions, and writing it down is what separates a test from a boost.

![Figure 8. The ad test card. Write it before spending, review it on the date you set.](images/fig08-ad-test-card.png)

**One service.** Not "the shop". A skin fade, a cut and beard, a kids cut before school starts. One clear thing a person can picture.

**One audience.** Who the ad is for and where they are. A radius around the shop that matches how far people realistically travel for a haircut, often a couple of miles in a city and more in a suburb or small town. Age range only if it truly matters for the service.

**One booking page.** A link that goes straight to booking that service, ideally with that barber, and that lets you see bookings that came from the ad. A separate link, a source tag, or at minimum the "How did you find us?" question.

**Two versions, one difference.** Two ads that differ in one thing only: the photo, or the first line, or the offer. If you change three things at once and one ad wins, you do not know why.

**A spend cap and an end date.** The total you are willing to lose if it does not work. For a first test, an amount you would not lose sleep over, perhaps $60 to $150, over one to two weeks.

**The primary measure.** Completed first visits attributed to the ad. Not clicks, not views, not bookings.

**The decision rule.** Written in advance: "If cost per completed first visit is $25 or less, run it again with one change. If it is over $40 or there are fewer than two completed first visits, stop. In between, try one more version."

**The review date.** When you will sit down and look at the numbers, which should be a few days after the end date, so people who booked late in the test have had time to come in.

## How much can you afford to pay for a new client?

The decision rule needs a number, and here is how to get one.

Start with what the first visit leaves behind after direct costs. For a $35 standard cut with about $5 in direct costs (products, the share of supplies, card fees), that is $30. If your barbers are paid commission per cut, include that too. If Tomás earns 50% of the service price, the shop's share of a $35 cut is $17.50, minus $5 in costs, leaves $12.50.

So, for the shop:

- If the barber is the owner (or you are solo), a new standard-cut client acquired for less than $30 pays for themselves on the first visit, before overhead and your own time.
- If the barber is on 50% commission, the shop breaks even on the first visit only below about $12.50.

That second number shocks people. It means that for a commissioned barber, an ad almost never pays back on the first visit. It pays back only if the client returns. Which is why Chapter 10, keeping clients, comes before you scale up ads.

Now add the return visits. Suppose, from your Chapter 2 numbers, about a third of first-time clients become regulars and come back roughly six more times in the year. On average, each new client then brings about two more visits in the year (a third of six). At $30 contribution per visit for an owner's chair, that is around $60 more. So a new client is worth about $90 in contribution in their first year, on average, and you could pay more than $30 for one and still come out ahead. Over the year, not on the first visit.

Be careful with this kind of arithmetic. It is easy to talk yourself into a high acquisition cost by assuming everyone becomes a regular. Use your own return rate, not a hopeful one. And set your decision rule with some room: if your break-even is $30, aim for $20.

## Writing the ads with AI

Ads for a barbershop should look and sound like the shop: a real photo, plain words, the service, the price, the place.

::: prompt
Write two versions of a Facebook and Instagram ad for my barbershop. Use only facts from my shop brief.\
Service: skin fade with Tomás, $40, 45 minutes.\
Goal: book a weekday appointment, Tuesday to Thursday.\
Audience: men within about 3 miles of the shop.\
Both versions must use the same photo and the same booking link. They should differ only in the first line: version A leads with the cut, version B leads with weekday availability.\
For each: primary text under 40 words, headline under 8 words, call-to-action button suggestion.\
No exaggerated claims ("best in town"), no fake urgency, no discount.
:::

Then read both against the checklist from Chapter 3. Ads add one extra check: would a stranger understand, in two seconds, what you are selling and where? If not, cut words.

A few extra cautions:

- **Use real photos of your own work,** with the client's permission for ad use specifically. Advertising is a bigger use than a post on your feed, and some clients who are fine with one are not fine with the other.
- **Avoid fake urgency.** "Only 2 slots left!" when there are nine is dishonest and, in many places, a consumer-protection problem.
- **Check the platform's ad rules.** Meta and Google review ads, and certain claims or images can get an ad rejected. A plain ad for a haircut rarely has trouble.

## Running the test

Set up the campaign with the budget cap and end date from your card. Turn it on. Then leave it alone. Changing the audience, the budget or the image halfway through ruins the comparison and resets the platform's learning. Check once or twice that it is running and that the link works.

Meanwhile, at the shop, record the source for every new booking. When someone books through the ad link, or says "I saw your ad", mark it. Keep the four columns from Chapter 9: booked, completed, cancelled, no-show.

## Reading the results

On the review date, gather the numbers: total spend, bookings from the ad, completed first visits from the ad, and whether those clients booked again.

Here is Cedar Street's first test, for Tomás's skin fades on weekday afternoons, with a $100 cap over 14 days:

- Spend: $100.
- People who clicked through to the booking page: 64. (Interesting, not decisive.)
- Bookings attributed to the ad: 7.
- Completed first visits: 5. One cancelled ahead, one no-show.
- Cost per completed first visit: $100 ÷ 5 = $20.

Version A (the cut first) and version B (availability first) brought in 3 and 2 completed visits. That is too few to call a winner. With numbers this small, the difference could easily be chance. That is normal; most small-shop ad tests will not produce a clear winner between versions, and that is fine. The main question is whether the ad as a whole was worth it.

Was it? The decision rule said: $25 or less, run again with one change. At $20, it passed. But look closer. Tomás is on commission, so the shop's share of each $40 skin fade, after his 50% and $5 in costs, is $15. The shop paid $20 to earn $15 on the first visit. It only makes money if those clients come back.

So the real review happened six weeks later. Three of the five had rebooked, two of them at checkout. Over the year, if those three return every four weeks or so, each one brings in a dozen more visits. That is a good result. Dre and Tomás agreed to run a second test with one change: a short video of the same fade instead of the photo.

If your numbers are this small, the honest conclusion is often "promising, test again" rather than "it works". That is still progress. After three or four tests you will know far more than you did, for a few hundred dollars, and you will never boost a post on hope again.

You can ask AI to help with the review:

::: prompt
Here are the results of an ad test for my barbershop. Spend: $100. Clicks: 64. Bookings attributed: 7. Completed first visits: 5 (version A: 3, version B: 2). No-show: 1. Cancelled ahead: 1. Service: skin fade, $40. Barber is on 50% commission. Direct costs per service: $5. My decision rule: continue if cost per completed first visit is $25 or less.

1. Calculate cost per completed first visit and the shop's contribution from the first visits after commission and costs.
2. Tell me whether the difference between versions A and B is meaningful with these numbers, in plain language.
3. Apply my decision rule.
4. Suggest one single change for the next test.\
Show your arithmetic.
:::

## When an ad fails

Most ad tests will not pass the first time. When one fails, look for the leak, the same way you did in Chapter 2:

- **Plenty of clicks, few bookings?** The booking page is the problem. Too many steps, no availability shown, wrong service pre-selected, slow to load on a phone.
- **Bookings but no-shows?** The reminder process, or the audience. Ad bookings from strangers tend to no-show more than referrals; a reminder sequence (Chapter 10) helps.
- **Few clicks?** The ad itself, or the audience, or simply not enough spend to learn anything.
- **Visits, but nobody returns?** Look at the experience and the rebooking habit, not the ad.

Then either fix that one thing and test again, or stop and put the money into something closer to the chair. Stopping is a perfectly good result. You have learned that, right now, this channel does not pay for your shop, and it cost you a capped, known amount to find out.

## Before your next shift

- Check the four conditions at the start of this chapter. If any are missing, fix that first.
- Work out what a new client is worth to you, using your own return rate and commission.
- Fill in a test card, including the decision rule and review date, before opening an ad manager.
- Create a tracked booking link for the ad.
- Run the test, leave it alone, and review on the date you set.

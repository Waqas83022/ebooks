# Start here: three ways through this book {.unnumbered}

You can read this book front to back, and it will make sense that way. But most barbershop owners I know read in the gaps between clients, ten minutes at a time, so here is a shorter route depending on how your shop is set up.

## If you work alone

You are the barber, the receptionist, the marketing department and the person who takes the towels home. Your problem is rarely skill. It is that every minute spent on messages, posts and admin is a minute you are not cutting, and the phone does not care that you are mid-fade.

Read Chapter 1 and Chapter 3 first, so the AI knows your shop. Then go straight to Chapter 8 (missed calls and messages) and Chapter 9 (quiet weekday slots). Those two chapters will pay for the book fastest. After that, Chapter 4 makes sure new clients can find you, and Chapter 10 keeps the ones you have coming back.

Keep it simple: one booking link, one place where client messages land, one list of approved replies. You do not need a "tech stack". You need fewer places to check.

## If you have employees

Now there are other people talking to your clients on your behalf, and they will say slightly different things about prices, late arrivals and walk-ins unless somebody writes it down. AI is very good at turning what is in your head into a clear policy sheet, practice scenarios and a checklist.

Read Chapters 1 to 3, then Chapter 12 (team and walk-ins) and Chapter 8 (handoffs between the front desk and the chair). Add the weekly scorecard from Chapter 2 and look at it with your team every Monday or whichever day you are closed.

## If you rent chairs

A chair-rental shop is really several small businesses sharing a roof, a sign and a reputation. Each renter usually sets their own prices, keeps their own clients and runs their own booking page. The shop owns the brand, the building and the Google listing.

That means two kinds of marketing: shop-wide marketing that brings people through the door, and each barber's own marketing to their own book of clients. Before anyone uses AI to message anybody, agree who owns which client list, which message, and which inquiry. Chapter 12 has a responsibility chart for exactly this. Read it early, then Chapters 4 to 6 for the shop-wide work.

## What you need

A phone or laptop. A free or paid account with an AI chat assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Microsoft Copilot. Your booking calendar, whatever it is. A spreadsheet, which can be Google Sheets, Excel or even a notebook if you prefer pencil. That is the whole kit.

Every chapter ends with a short section called "Before your next shift". It lists the one or two things worth doing this week. If you only ever do those, you will still be ahead of most shops on your street.

## A word on the examples

To keep the advice concrete, most chapters follow one fictional shop, Cedar Street Barbers. It has three chairs. The owner, Dre, cuts on the first chair. Tomás is an employee paid on commission. Keisha rents the third chair and runs her own book. Between them they cover all three setups above, and they run into the same problems you do: a dead Tuesday afternoon, a client who swears he called four times, a one-star review written at midnight.

Their menu, used throughout the book:

| Service | Price | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Standard cut | $35 | 30 min |
| Skin fade | $40 | 45 min |
| Beard trim and line-up | $20 | 15 min |
| Cut and beard | $50 | 45 min |
| Kids cut (under 12) | $25 | 30 min |
| Hot towel shave | $45 | 45 min |
| The full service (cut, beard, hot towel, wash) | $75 | 75 min |

Cedar Street is open Tuesday to Friday from 10 a.m. to 7 p.m. and Saturday from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. It is closed Sunday and Monday. Prices are illustrative and chosen to make the arithmetic easy. Swap in your own as you go.

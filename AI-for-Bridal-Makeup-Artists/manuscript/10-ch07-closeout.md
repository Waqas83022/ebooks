# 7. Close the booking and track what works {#ch7}

The portraits start at 10:45 exactly. Theo lines everyone up in the walled garden, the light is as good as promised, and Amara's mother, who said as she sat down in Nadia's chair that she "never wears much makeup," asks you on the way out whether you could show her how you did her eyes.

The morning went to plan, more or less. There was one surprise. At 8:20, Bridesmaid 3, Sophie, saw the maid of honor's lashes from Nadia's chair and asked for a pair too. Nadia said yes, quoted $15, and you noted it in your phone. The lashes took seven minutes, so Nadia finished her last service at 10:22 instead of 10:15, well inside her buffer. Your bride was done at 10:14.

You drive home, unload, clean brushes, eat something and fall asleep on the sofa at 4 p.m.

For many artists, that's where the booking ends. The money's in, the bride was happy, and Monday brings next week's inquiries. But the hour or so you spend closing the booking properly is what turns one good wedding into a review, a referral, a portfolio image you're allowed to use and a slightly better process for next time. It's also the only way to find out, at the end of the month, which of your inquiries are turning into work and which aren't.

## Step one: Check the money against your records

Start with the money, because it's the one thing that can't be fixed with a nice message later.

Open your tracker, your payment system and your phone note from the morning. Work out what the client owed in total, and what you've actually received, from your payment records, not from memory.

For Amara's booking:

- Original quote: $640
- Lashes for the maid of honor (agreed in August, added to balance): +$15
- Mother of the bride and second artist's travel (agreed October 2): +$135
- Lashes for Sophie on the morning (paid on the day): +$15
- **Total owed: $805**

And what you've received:

- Deposit, verified when booked: $160
- Balance, verified October 2: $630
- Card payment on the day: $15
- **Total received: $805**

They match. Record the final figures in the tracker and mark the balance as complete.

If they don't match, find out why before you send any thank-you. Common reasons are a balance paid to an old payment link, a card payment that didn't go through on a weak signal, or an add-on you did on the day and forgot to charge. It's much easier to raise a missing $15 in a short, matter-of-fact message the day after the wedding than three weeks later, in the middle of a thank-you exchange.

If you work with an associate, this is also the moment to settle with them, under whatever arrangement you have. That's between you and Nadia. It stays out of the AI tool and out of client messages.

## Step two: Send a thank-you in your own voice

Send a short thank-you within a day or two, while the wedding is still fresh for both of you. This is one of the places where it's tempting to let the AI write the whole thing, and one of the places where that shows most.

A generic thank-you ("It was an absolute pleasure to be part of your special day!") could have come from anyone, and the bride knows it. What makes a thank-you feel personal is one specific detail that only you would know. So write that detail yourself, and let the AI help with the rest if you need it.

::: prompt
**Copy-ready prompt**

Help me write a short thank-you message to a bride, the day after her wedding. Under 90 words. Warm, not gushing. No "special day," no "absolute pleasure." Include this detail, in my words: [one specific moment from the morning]. Don't ask for a review in this message. Sign off [Artist first name].
:::

> Hi Amara,
>
> Thank you for having me yesterday. I keep thinking about the moment your mom saw you in the garden before the portraits. I don't think she'd stopped smiling by the time I left. You were so calm all morning, which made it a joy for everyone in that room.
>
> Wishing you both a wonderful start to married life.
>
> [Artist first name]

The detail about her mother is yours. You saw it, and you wrote it into the prompt. The AI only had to put a frame around it.

## Step three: Ask for a review, simply

Reviews matter in this business. Brides read them, and so do the planners and venues who might recommend you. But a review request can easily feel like a bill attached to a gift, so keep it separate from the thank-you, wait a few days, and make it easy.

Ask in your own voice, tell her where to post it, and give her the link. That's all.

> Hi Amara, I hope the honeymoon planning is going well! If you have a moment, I'd be really grateful for a short review. It helps other couples find me. Here's the link: [review link]. Even a line or two makes a difference. Thank you again.

A few things to avoid.

**Don't offer anything in return for a review.** Discounts or gifts for reviews break the rules of many review platforms, and in some places they're also against consumer law.

**Don't write the review for her.** If a client says "Just write something and I'll post it," you can suggest she mention one thing she liked, in her own words. Never use AI to draft a review that a client posts as her own, and never post one yourself. Fake and ghost-written reviews are illegal in a growing number of countries, including the United States and the United Kingdom, and they're one of the fastest ways to lose a reputation you spent years building.

**Don't ask twice.** One request, one gentle reminder if you like, and then let it go. Some happy clients just don't write reviews. They tend to recommend you to their friends instead.

## Step four: Ask for portfolio permission separately

You'd love to post one of Theo's portraits from the walled garden. Before you do, you need two separate permissions, and neither belongs in a review request.

**Permission from the people in the photo.** A bride who's happy to leave you a review isn't automatically happy to have her face on your Instagram grid. Neither is her mother, or the maid of honor who's in the same frame. Ask specifically: which photo, where you'd post it (Instagram, website, printed portfolio, adverts) and whether you'll tag her. Get the answer in writing, even a single-line message, and keep it with the booking record.

**Permission from the photographer.** In most countries, the photographer owns the copyright in their photos, whatever your agreement with the bride says. Ask Theo whether you may use the image, and how he'd like to be credited. Many photographers are happy to share with suppliers, but it's their decision.

> Hi Amara, one more question, and please say no if you'd rather not. Would you be happy for me to share one of Theo's portraits of you on my Instagram and website? I'd love to use the one of you by the garden wall. I'd tag Theo and you, or leave you untagged if you prefer. Your bridesmaids are in the background of that one, so I'll only post it if they're happy too.

Record the answer and the date. If she says yes to Instagram but not to adverts, write that down. Your future self, choosing a photo for a paid campaign in eighteen months' time, won't remember.

## Step five: Tidy the records

Once the money's reconciled and the messages are sent, tidy up.

**Tracker.** Mark the booking as closed. Record the final total, the date of the thank-you, whether a review was requested and received, and the portfolio permissions.

**Client record.** Decide what to keep and what to delete. Your trial photos, face chart and consultation form were collected for this booking. Keep them for as long as your business has a reason to (for example, if the bride might book you again for an anniversary shoot, or your records policy requires it), and no longer. If you've told clients in your privacy notice how long you keep their information, follow it. Where the law in your country sets rules about this, those rules come first.

**Chat tool.** If you used separate conversations for this booking, delete or archive them. They shouldn't contain anything identifying, because you used placeholders, but tidying them keeps your workspace clean and makes it easier to find your templates.

## Step six: Learn one thing from the morning

Last, write down what you learned. Not a long reflection. Three lines.

- **What took longer than planned?**
- **What would I change in my brief, my templates or my timings?**
- **What went better than expected, and why?**

For Amara's booking, your notes might read:

> Sophie's lashes on the day took 7 minutes, not 5. Lashes for attendants should be 10 minutes in the brief. Room access confirmation saved us: venue said 7:00, suite was actually open 6:45. Always ask. Nadia arriving at 8:00 rather than 6:45 worked well; plan second artists' arrival from their own column.

Then, once a month, paste those notes into the AI and ask for suggested changes to your brief.

::: prompt
**Copy-ready prompt**

Here's my current studio brief and my notes from this month's weddings. Suggest specific edits to the brief based only on what the notes show. For each suggestion, quote the line in the brief you'd change and the note it's based on. Don't suggest price changes. I'll decide those separately.

[Studio brief]
[Monthly notes]
:::

Read the suggestions, accept the ones you agree with and update the brief's version and date. A brief that learns from your weddings is worth far more than the most carefully written brief that never changes.

## The closeout checklist

::: checklist
**Closing a booking**

1. **Money.** Total owed matches total received, from payment records. Add-ons included. Associate settled.
2. **Thank-you.** Sent within two days, with one specific detail.
3. **Review.** Requested separately, with a link. No incentives.
4. **Portfolio.** Permission asked separately from people in the photo and from the photographer. Answers recorded.
5. **Records.** Tracker closed. Client files kept or deleted according to your policy. AI chats tidied.
6. **Lessons.** Three lines written. Brief updated if needed.
:::

## The monthly scorecard

Now step back from one booking to all of them.

Most artists have a feeling about how business is going. "Inquiries are down this year." "People aren't booking like they used to." "I'm getting loads of price shoppers." Sometimes the feeling is right. Often it's shaped by the last two or three inquiries, which isn't much to base a decision on, especially a decision like raising your prices or spending money on advertising.

A monthly scorecard replaces the feeling with two numbers, and your tracker already has everything you need to calculate them.

**Inquiry-to-quote rate.** Of the inquiries you received, how many reached a quote? A low number means people are dropping off before you can price them. That usually points to the first reply or the intake form.

**Quote-to-booking rate.** Of the quotes you sent, how many became bookings? A low number points to price, follow-up, timing or fit.

### Count in cohorts

There's one trap in working these out, and it catches almost everyone. If you count the bookings you made in March and divide by the quotes you sent in March, you're mixing two different groups of people. Some of March's bookings came from February's quotes. Some of March's quotes won't become bookings until April.

So count in cohorts. Group quotes by the month you sent them. Once that group has had enough time to respond (your quotes are valid for 14 days, so give it at least a month), divide the confirmed bookings from that group by the number of quotes in it.

If six of the 20 quotes you sent in February became bookings, February's quotes converted at 30%. That's the number to compare with January's quotes and March's quotes, as long as each group has had a similar amount of time to respond.

Record which leads in each group are still open. A group with five open leads isn't finished yet, and its rate will probably rise. If you compare it with a finished month, you'll think you're doing worse than you are.

![A monthly scorecard. Each bar is one month's quotes, split by what happened to them. The newest month still has open leads, so it is not ready to compare.](images/fig-07-scorecard.png){.figure}

Here's a scorecard for the fictional studio's first four months of the year, counted in quote cohorts:

| Quotes sent in | Sent | Booked | Closed, no booking | Still open | Rate so far |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| January | 18 | 6 | 12 | 0 | 33% |
| February | 20 | 6 | 13 | 1 | 30% |
| March | 22 | 5 | 10 | 7 | 23% so far |
| April | 15 | 2 | 3 | 10 | not ready |

March looks worse than January, but it has seven open leads. If two of those book, March will be at 32%, about the same as January. April is far too young to judge. The honest reading of this scorecard is "about one quote in three becomes a booking, and nothing has changed yet." That's a very different conclusion from "March was a bad month," and it saves you from cutting your prices in a panic.

Do the same for inquiries, grouped by the month they arrived: how many reached a quote? Count dates you declined because you were already booked separately. They're not lost leads. They tell you about your capacity, not your conversion. If you're declining six or seven inquiries a month for peak Saturdays, that's a sign you could bring in an associate, or charge more for those dates.

### Building it in a spreadsheet

If your tracker is a spreadsheet, a few formulas do the counting for you. Add a column called "Quote month" that holds the month each quote was sent (for example, 2026-02). Then, in Google Sheets or Excel:

- Quotes sent in February: `=COUNTIF(QuoteMonth, "2026-02")`
- Booked from February's quotes: `=COUNTIFS(QuoteMonth, "2026-02", Status, "Booked")`
- Still open from February: `=COUNTIFS(QuoteMonth, "2026-02", Status, "Quote sent")` plus the same for "Awaiting agreement" and "Awaiting deposit"

Replace `QuoteMonth` and `Status` with the actual column ranges in your sheet, such as `F2:F500`. If you're not sure how, the AI is good at this: describe your columns (not your data) and ask it to write the formula.

If you'd rather not use formulas, count by hand from a filtered view once a month. Twenty minutes of counting is enough.

### Using AI to read the numbers

Once you have the counts, you can ask the AI to calculate the rates and point out anything worth looking at. Give it only the counts. Never paste the tracker itself, because it contains your clients' names.

::: prompt
**Copy-ready prompt**

Here are my bridal makeup inquiry and quote counts, grouped by month. For each month, calculate the inquiry-to-quote rate and the quote-to-booking rate. Treat any month with more than 20% of its leads still open as not ready to compare. Then list up to three observations, each based on a specific number. Don't give general business advice.

[Paste a table of counts only]
:::

Check the rates yourself with a calculator. They're simple divisions, but it only takes one slip to send you chasing a problem you don't have.

### What to do with the numbers

The numbers are only useful if they change something. Here's how to read them.

**Inquiry-to-quote is low.** People are dropping off between their first message and your quote. Look at your first replies: are you asking too many questions, or answering too slowly? Is your intake form asking for things that don't affect the price? Try the five-question reply from Chapter 2 for a month and compare.

**Quote-to-booking is low.** People see your price and don't book. Before you assume it's the price, check the follow-ups. Are you sending a specific follow-up, or none at all? Are quotes clear about what's included? Is the timing right, or are you quoting brides who are still nine months from choosing anyone? If it really is price, the scorecard gives you a way to test a change: compare the months before and after.

**Declined dates are high.** You're turning work away. That's a capacity question: rates for peak dates, a second artist, or a minimum spend for Saturdays in June.

**Everything looks steady.** That's useful too. It means your process works and you can stop worrying about it, which is worth a great deal in the middle of wedding season.

## Ten-minute action

Close the last wedding you did, using the six steps. If it was a while ago, at least do the money check and write your three lines of lessons.

Then set a monthly reminder in your calendar, for the first Monday of each month, called "Scorecard and brief." Twenty minutes to count, ten to update your brief. That half hour is how this whole process keeps getting better without you thinking about it.

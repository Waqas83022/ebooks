# 5. Work backward from the ready-by time {#ch5}

Every bridal artist has a story about the morning that ran late. Usually it isn't one big disaster. It's six small ones. The room wasn't open until ten past. A bridesmaid was in the shower when it was her turn. The hair stylist needed the bride at the same moment you did. The photographer wanted "just a few shots" of the bride's makeup in progress, by the window, where your light wasn't. Each one cost five or ten minutes, and by 11:00 you were doing the bride's lips while the photographer was already at the door.

You can't stop every small delay. But most of them are visible weeks in advance if you build the schedule properly: start from the time that can't move, work backward, and check it against everyone else who needs the same people at the same time.

This chapter builds Amara's wedding-morning schedule with her hair stylist, Jess. Then it builds a two-artist schedule for a bigger party, and finishes with the short version you share with the bridal party.

## Start from the first hard deadline

Every wedding morning has a deadline that can't move, and it's often not the one the bride first tells you. "Ready by 11:15" might really mean "ready by 11:15 for the photographer, but the bride needs to be dressed by 11:00, so her makeup must be done by 10:45." Or it might mean that the cars leave at 12:30 and the photographer is flexible.

So before you schedule anything, find out what the deadline actually is and who set it. In Amara's case, the 11:15 time came from the photographer, Theo, who starts getting-ready photos then. You check with the planner, Dana, and she confirms: Theo arrives at 11:00 for detail shots, starts photographing the party at 11:15, and the bride gets into her dress at about 11:30. Makeup-ready for everyone at 11:15 is the right deadline.

Write down where the deadline came from. If it changes later, you'll want to know whose decision it was and who needs to hear about the change.

## The five-step worksheet

Building a schedule backward is five steps. You can do it on paper, in a spreadsheet or with the AI. Whichever you choose, do it in this order.

**1. Deadline.** Write down the earliest time the bride or bridal party must be ready for portraits, dressing or departure. Confirm it with the person who set it.

**2. Buffer.** Reserve time for touch-ups and delays before that deadline. Use the buffer in your brief. Don't shave it to make the plan fit. Running out of buffer is a sign that the morning doesn't fit, and you want to find that out now.

**3. Services.** List the confirmed services and realistic minutes for each person. Use what the trial taught you. When there are two artists, make a separate column for each.

**4. Setup.** Add the time for unloading, walking to the room and setting up. Count any of those steps that can only happen after access begins.

**5. Feasibility.** Compare the start time you've calculated with the time you can actually get into the room. Then check each person's hair appointment and any photography commitments before you send the schedule to anyone.

## Amara's morning, solo

Since the quote, one thing has changed: Amara's maid of honor has added lashes, which makes her service 45 minutes instead of 40. Work backward from 11:15:

- Buffer: 20 minutes. The bride must finish by **10:55**.
- Bride: 60 minutes. She starts at **9:55**.
- Bridesmaid 4: 40 minutes. Starts at **9:15**.
- Bridesmaid 3: 40 minutes. Starts at **8:35**.
- Bridesmaid 2: 40 minutes. Starts at **7:55**.
- Maid of honor, with lashes: 45 minutes. Starts at **7:10**.
- Setup: 15 minutes. You need to be in the room by **6:55**.

The venue said 7:00. You're five minutes short.

Five minutes doesn't sound like much, and it would be easy to wave it away. You could set up a little faster, or take five minutes from the buffer. But this is how mornings go wrong: a plan that only works if nothing slips, built on top of a buffer that's already been spent. The worksheet has done its job. It's shown you the gap while there's still time to fix it calmly.

You email Dana. Hollin House has Amara's party staying the night before, so the suite is already theirs. Dana checks with the venue and confirms that suppliers can come up from 6:45. You now have 10 minutes more than you need. Rather than start later, you start at 6:45 and let the spare 10 minutes sit at the end, which gives you a 30-minute buffer before 11:15. A tight plan has become a comfortable one.

## Check it against hair

Jess, the hair stylist, is working alone too. Her timings are similar to yours: 40 minutes for each bridesmaid, 60 for the bride and 15 minutes to set up. She keeps the last half hour free for finishing touches and the veil.

The rule for combining two schedules is simple: **nobody can be in two chairs at once.** Each person needs a makeup slot and a hair slot that don't overlap, ideally with a few minutes in between to move, have a drink and visit the bathroom.

This is where you need the person view, not the artist view. It's easy to make two timetables that each look perfect and still put the bride in both chairs at 10:00.

Here's the joint schedule you and Jess agree on, one column at a time.

**Makeup (you)**

- 6:45--7:00 Setup
- 7:00--7:45 Maid of honor (with lashes)
- 7:45--8:25 Bridesmaid 2
- 8:25--9:05 Bridesmaid 3
- 9:05--9:45 Bridesmaid 4
- 9:45--10:45 Bride
- 10:45--11:15 Touch-ups and buffer

**Hair (Jess)**

- 6:45--7:00 Setup
- 7:00--7:40 Bridesmaid 2
- 7:45--8:25 Maid of honor
- 8:25--9:25 Bride
- 9:25--10:05 Bridesmaid 3
- 10:05--10:45 Bridesmaid 4
- 10:45--11:15 Finishing and veil

Two lists are easy to write and hard to check, because the appointments don't start at the same times. The picture makes it easier: one lane each for you and Jess.

![Amara's morning with hair and makeup side by side. Each person appears once in each lane, and never in both lanes at the same time.](images/fig-05-solo-timeline.png){.figure}

Now check it the other way round, person by person:

- **Maid of honor:** makeup 7:00--7:45, hair 7:45--8:25. Back to back, no overlap.
- **Bridesmaid 2:** hair 7:00--7:40, makeup 7:45--8:25. Five minutes between.
- **Bridesmaid 3:** makeup 8:25--9:05, hair 9:25--10:05. Free 9:05--9:25.
- **Bridesmaid 4:** makeup 9:05--9:45, hair 10:05--10:45. Free 9:45--10:05.
- **Bride:** hair 8:25--9:25, makeup 9:45--10:45. Twenty minutes in between for breakfast, a breather and the detail photos.

Every person fits. Nobody is in two places at once. And there's a reason the bride's hair comes before her makeup in this plan: it means her makeup is the last thing done before photos, so it's at its freshest when Theo starts at 11:15. Jess agreed, because the bride's hair is set and won't drop in the 90 minutes before the dress goes on. That's a conversation between two professionals, and it's why you share the draft with the hair stylist before anyone else.

## Using AI to build and check the schedule

You can do all of the above on paper. Many artists do, and it works. But the AI is useful here in two ways: it can lay out a first draft in seconds when you give it the facts, and it can produce the person view so you can check for overlaps.

The trick is to give it everything and ask it to show its working.

::: prompt
**Copy-ready prompt**

Build a wedding-morning makeup timetable using the facts below. Work backward from 11:15 a.m. Reserve 20 minutes before the deadline and 15 minutes for setup before the first appointment. Services: maid of honor 45 minutes (includes lashes), bridesmaids 2, 3 and 4 at 40 minutes each, bride 60 minutes, bride last. One makeup artist. Room access from 6:45 a.m.

Return one row per time block. Then calculate the latest possible start time and compare it with room access. Flag any gaps, overlaps or shortfalls. Do not shorten any service. Keep anything unconfirmed in brackets.
:::

Once you have the hair stylist's times, ask for the person view:

::: prompt
**Copy-ready prompt**

Here are two schedules for the same morning: makeup and hair. Combine them into a person view: one row per person, showing their makeup time, their hair time and the gap between them. Flag any person who is booked in two places at the same time, or who has less than 5 minutes between appointments. Don't move any appointments. Only report.

[Makeup schedule]
[Hair schedule]
:::

That last instruction, "Don't move any appointments. Only report," is important. When a chat tool spots a clash, its instinct is to fix it, and it'll often fix it by moving an appointment without saying so, in a way that creates a new clash somewhere else. You want it to point out the problem. You'll decide how to solve it, together with the hair stylist.

### How to check the AI's schedule

Every time, without exception, check three things yourself:

1. **The sum.** Add up setup, all services and buffer. Does it match the window from access to ready-by? If the AI's plan fits but your sum doesn't, the AI has shortened something.
2. **The rows.** Does each appointment start exactly when the previous one ends? Look for a row that ends at 8:25 followed by one that starts at 8:20.
3. **The people.** Take each person in turn and find every appearance they make. They should appear once per artist, never twice at the same time.

It takes two minutes. It's the best-spent two minutes in this whole book, because a schedule error is the one mistake you can't fix with an apology email. You find out about it at 9:40 on the morning.

## A two-artist schedule

Some bookings are too big for one artist, either because the morning won't fit or because your brief caps you at six services. Here's a different fictional booking, lead L-044, to show how a two-artist morning works.

The party is a bride, five bridesmaids and both mothers: eight services. Room access is 8:00 a.m. and everyone must be ready by 12:00. Because there are six or more services, the brief calls for a 30-minute buffer.

On your own, you'd need 15 minutes of setup, 280 minutes for seven 40-minute services, 60 minutes for the bride and a 30-minute buffer. That's 385 minutes, and the window is 240. You're more than two hours short, and you'd also be over your six-service limit. This booking needs two artists, and that decision was made at the quote stage, with the second artist's travel fee included.

Split the work so each artist has a fair share and the bride stays with you, the lead artist:

- **You:** the bride and three others. That's 180 minutes of services.
- **Your associate:** four people. That's 160 minutes of services.

Now work backward separately for each artist, because they don't have to arrive at the same time.

For you: 12:00, less 30 minutes of buffer, less 180 minutes of services, less 15 minutes of setup, gives a latest arrival of 8:15. Access is at 8:00, so you have 15 minutes to spare. Start at 8:00 and the spare time goes to your buffer.

For your associate: 12:00, less 30, less 160, less 15 gives a latest arrival of 8:35. She doesn't need to be there at 8:00. Ask her to arrive at 8:30, so she has five minutes in hand. That's 30 minutes less early-morning time for her, and one less person setting up in a small room while you're doing the first face.

**You (lead artist)**

- 8:00--8:15 Setup
- 8:15--8:55 Mother of the bride
- 8:55--9:35 Bridesmaid 1
- 9:35--10:15 Bridesmaid 2
- 10:15--11:15 Bride
- 11:15--12:00 Touch-ups and buffer (45 minutes)

**Associate**

- 8:30--8:45 Setup
- 8:45--9:25 Bridesmaid 3
- 9:25--10:05 Bridesmaid 4
- 10:05--10:45 Bridesmaid 5
- 10:45--11:25 Mother of the groom
- 11:25--12:00 Touch-ups and buffer (35 minutes)

There are two things to check in a two-artist schedule that don't come up when you're on your own.

**Space and light.** Two artists need two chairs, two good light sources and somewhere to put two kits. Ask the venue or planner what the room is like before you promise two stations. A small hotel room with one window may need you to bring a light.

**Who does what on the day.** Decide in advance who handles the photographer, who speaks to the planner and who does the final touch-ups on the bride. Usually it's you. Write it on the studio copy so your associate doesn't have to guess.

Then send both columns to the hair team and ask for the person view, exactly as you did with Jess. With eight people and two teams, it's easy to have a mother in your chair while she's also booked for hair.

## Two versions of the same schedule

The schedule you've built is a working document, full of notes that only make sense to you and Jess. It's not what the bridal party needs. They need to know three things: when to be in the chair, where to be, and who to tell if they're running late.

So make two versions.

**The studio copy** has everything: setup times, buffer, the hair column, the person view, the deadline and who set it, your associate's arrival time, notes about light and kit, and the version date. It goes to you, your associate (if there is one), the hair stylist and the planner.

**The bridal party copy** is short, kind and impossible to misread. It has each person's chair times, the arrival time for the room, the contact for lateness, and the version date. Nothing about buffers. A bridesmaid who sees "11:15--11:30 buffer" is likely to think she can arrive late.

Add: "Please arrive at the suite 10 minutes before your first appointment, with clean, moisturized skin and hair as Jess has asked." Add: "Running late? Message [Planner] on [phone]." Put the version and date at the top. Leave out setup, buffer and internal notes. Keep it under 120 words.

:::

Fill in the names yourself, check every time against the studio copy, and send it to the bride or planner to share. Here's Amara's:

The version number and date at the top matter more than they look. Schedules change. If the photographer moves the portraits next week, you'll send version 4, and everyone needs to be able to tell at a glance which one is current. Without a date, people screenshot the first version they see and turn up at the wrong time.

## The release checklist

Run this before any schedule leaves your hands.

::: checklist
**Before you send a schedule**

1. **Verify the math.** Every confirmed service fits, with the buffer intact. Sum checked by hand.
2. **Check the people.** No person or artist is scheduled in two places at once.
3. **Confirm the handoff.** Room access, the hair schedule and the ready-by deadline have been confirmed with the people responsible for them.
4. **Version and date.** The current version number and date are on both copies.
5. **Share a simple version.** The bridal party gets chair times, arrival instructions and who to contact if they're late. Internal notes stay in the studio copy.
:::

## Ten-minute action

Take your next booked wedding and work backward from the ready-by time using the five steps. Do it by hand first. Then, if you like, give the facts to the AI with the timetable prompt and compare.

If the two disagree, find out why. If they agree and the morning fits, you've just turned a plan that lived in your head into a document you can share. If it doesn't fit, you've found the problem weeks before the morning, which is the best time to find it.

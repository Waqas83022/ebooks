# Introduction: The booking happens before the brushes come out {#introduction}

It is 11:40 on a Tuesday night. You finished a full day of appointments, drove home, cleaned your kit and ate standing up. Now the phone shows nine unread messages across three apps.

One is a voice note from a bride who wants to "lock in" a date in June. Another is a screenshot of someone else's price list with the words "Can you match this?" A planner has emailed to ask whether you can add a flower girl to Saturday's booking. A bridesmaid from a wedding you did last month has sent a lovely thank-you and asked whether you'd do her sister's makeup. And at the bottom of the pile is the message that always takes longest to answer:

"Hi! Are you free October 10? It's me and maybe four others. We need to be ready before photos. How much would it be?"

You can do the makeup in your sleep. What wears you down is everything around it. The same five questions typed out again and again. The quote that has to be recalculated because one more cousin wants lashes. The deposit someone says they've paid but that hasn't arrived. The timeline that looked fine in March and stopped working in September when the venue changed its access time.

This book is about that part of the job: the booking. It starts with the first "Are you free?" and finishes after the final touch-up, when the balance is paid, the thank-you has gone out and you know which of this month's inquiries turned into work.

It shows you how to use an AI chat tool to take the typing, the arithmetic drafts and the first versions of your schedules off your plate, while you keep every decision that matters. That second half of the sentence is the whole point.

## What AI is good at in a makeup business

The chat tools that most people now have on their phones are very good at language. Give one a few facts and a tone of voice, and it will turn them into a warm, clear message in seconds. Ask it to reorganize a messy list into a table, and it will. Paste in a rambling email from a planner and ask, "What is she actually asking me to change?" and you'll usually get a useful answer.

For a bridal artist, that covers a surprising amount of the admin:

- turning your notes into a friendly reply to a new inquiry
- drafting an itemized quote from figures you've already checked
- writing a follow-up that asks a clear question instead of "just checking in"
- laying out a wedding-morning schedule from the facts you supply, and flagging where it doesn't fit
- summarizing a trial into a short recap the bride can confirm
- rewording a difficult message so it's firm without sounding cold
- tidying your notes after a wedding into a list of what to change next time

None of this is magic. It's drafting. But drafting is where the hours go. If a reply that used to take you twelve minutes now takes three, and you send thirty replies a week, that is four and a half hours back every week. Spend them on sleep, or on the content you never get round to posting.

## What AI gets wrong

The same tools have habits that can cost you money and trust if you don't plan for them.

They invent things. Ask for a quote without giving the prices and you'll get a confident price list that doesn't belong to you. Ask for a cancellation policy and you'll get one that sounds official and may contradict your contract.

They agree too easily. A chat tool wants to be helpful, so it leans toward yes. "Can you confirm my booking?" can turn into a cheerful confirmation even though the deposit hasn't cleared.

They are weak at time arithmetic. They will usually add up 40-minute appointments correctly, but they are less reliable when a schedule has two artists, a hair team and a photographer all moving at once. Overlaps slip through, and a 30-minute shortfall can disappear inside a neat-looking table.

They don't know your calendar, your bank account or your contract unless you tell them, and you shouldn't tell them everything.

So the method in this book divides the work. The AI drafts. You check, decide and send. Every price, every date, every promise about timing and every confirmation of payment passes through your hands before it reaches a client. The book calls these *commitments*, and it is strict about them.

## Two rules that run through every chapter

**Rule one: You approve every commitment.** A commitment is anything a client could reasonably hold you to: a price, a date, an availability statement, a start time, a ready-by time, a policy, a confirmation that something is paid or booked. The AI can phrase them. Only you can make them.

**Rule two: Clients' private details stay out of the chat.** You will never need a client's full name, phone number, address, reference photos, skin concerns or payment details inside an AI tool to get a good draft. You'll use placeholders such as `[Client first name]` and a simple lead ID instead, and add the real details yourself just before you send. Chapter 1 shows you how.

The second rule protects your clients and it protects you. Brides share a lot with their makeup artist: photos of their face from every angle, skin conditions, medications they're taking for acne, the fact that they're pregnant before anyone else knows. That information belongs in the records your business already keeps for that purpose, not in a chat history on a service you don't control. Depending on where you work, data protection law may also set out what you are allowed to do with it. The safe habit is simple: if you wouldn't read it out to a stranger at the next table in a café, don't paste it in.

## One wedding, all the way through

Advice about bookings is easy to nod along to and hard to apply. So this book follows a single fictional wedding from the first message to the closeout, and you'll see how a small decision in the inbox shows up again on the wedding morning.

The bride is Amara. She sends the message you read at the top of this introduction. Her wedding is on Saturday, October 10. She and four bridesmaids want makeup. She wants everyone ready "before photos." By the end of Chapter 3 you'll have quoted her, followed up and confirmed the booking. In Chapter 4 you'll run her trial. In Chapter 5 you'll build her wedding-morning schedule with her hair stylist, Jess. In Chapter 6, two weeks before the wedding, her mother asks for makeup and the photographer moves the portraits earlier, and you'll see how to handle both without overbooking yourself. In Chapter 7 you'll close the booking and add her to your monthly numbers.

The studio in the examples is also fictional. Its rates are $220 for bridal makeup, $95 for each attendant and $40 for travel within its local zone. You'll almost certainly charge something different. Keep the method and swap in your own numbers. If you work in pounds, euros, rupees or dirhams, the arithmetic is the same.

## What you need

You need four things, and you probably have them already.

**An AI chat tool.** ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Microsoft Copilot all work for everything in this book, as do others. The free version of any of them is enough to start. Paid plans tend to give you longer conversations and the ability to save standing instructions, which is convenient but not essential. The prompts in this book are written in plain English so that they work in any of them.

**A calendar.** Whatever you use to know where you're meant to be on a given Saturday. If your calendar and your memory ever disagree, your calendar wins, and it must be the first thing you check before telling anyone a date is open.

**A spreadsheet or booking system.** Google Sheets, Excel, Numbers, or a booking tool such as the ones many artists already use for contracts and invoices. Chapter 2 gives you a tracker you can build in fifteen minutes if you don't have one.

**Your own rates and terms, written down.** If they currently live in your head, in old DMs and in a price list from two seasons ago, that's the first job. Chapter 1 walks you through it.

## Setting up your AI tool in twenty minutes

Before you paste anything into a chat tool for work, spend a few minutes on its settings. Menus change often, so look for these things rather than for specific buttons.

First, find the data controls. Most tools have a setting that decides whether your conversations may be used to improve the company's models. Read what it says and choose deliberately. If you're on a business or team plan, check what your plan's terms say about your data. Whatever you choose, Rule two still applies: clients' private details stay out.

Second, find out whether the tool can save standing instructions. Depending on the product, this might be called custom instructions, a project, a custom assistant, a gem or something else. If yours has it, you'll paste your studio brief from Chapter 1 there so you don't need to repeat it in every chat. If yours doesn't, keep the brief in a note on your phone and paste it at the start of each new conversation. Both work.

Third, decide where your AI conversations will live. Some artists keep one long conversation per booking. That's tidy, but long conversations can drift, and the tool may start mixing up details from earlier messages. A better pattern for most people is one saved brief, plus a fresh conversation for each task: one for the inquiry reply, one for the quote, one for the schedule. The tracker, not the chat history, is your record of the booking.

Fourth, get used to the habit that matters most. Read every draft as if a new assistant wrote it on their first day. They're quick, they're polite and they mean well. They also don't know your business yet, and they'd rather guess than ask.

## How each chapter works

Every chapter opens with a situation you'll recognize. It then works through a complete example with Amara's wedding or with one of the practice bookings, shows you the prompt that produces the draft, shows you what the draft looks like after you've reviewed it, and points out what to check before it goes anywhere. Each chapter finishes with a ten-minute action you can do with a real inquiry, and the templates are collected in Appendix A so you can copy them in one place.

Prompts appear in shaded boxes like this:

::: prompt
**Copy-ready prompt**

Rewrite the message below so it's warm and clear, 80 words or fewer. Keep every date, price and time exactly as written. Do not add anything I haven't said.

[Paste your draft here]
:::

Anything in square brackets is a placeholder. Replace it with your own details, or leave it in the prompt so the AI knows to leave a gap in the draft for you to fill.

Finished messages, after review, appear in the same style as the client would see them. Checklists are short on purpose. If a checklist is too long to run on your phone between appointments, you won't run it.

The book is organized in two parts. Part I covers the booking itself: your brief, the first reply, the quote and deposit, and the trial. Part II covers the wedding morning: the timeline, late changes and the closeout. The three appendices give you the templates, three practice bookings to test yourself on, and a troubleshooting guide for the times an AI draft goes wrong.

You can read it in order, and the first time through you probably should. After that, it's meant to sit on your phone as a reference. When the next "Are you free?" arrives at 11:40 p.m., you'll know exactly which page to open.

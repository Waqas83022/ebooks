# 1. Give AI a one-page studio brief {#ch1}

The first time most artists try an AI tool for work, it goes something like this. You paste in a bride's message and type, "Write a reply with my prices." A few seconds later you have a lovely, warm message. It thanks her for reaching out, congratulates her on her engagement, says bridal makeup is $180, bridesmaids are $75 each, and adds that you'd be "happy to hold the date for her while she decides."

Three things in that reply are wrong. Your bridal rate is $220. Your attendant rate is $95. And you never hold dates without a deposit, because the one time you did, you turned away two other inquiries for a booking that never came.

The tool didn't malfunction. It did exactly what it was built to do, which is produce the most likely-sounding reply. It had no idea what you charge or how you work, so it filled the gaps with what bridal artists in general tend to say. That's why the reply sounded right. It's also why it was wrong.

The fix is to stop asking the AI to guess. Before it writes a single message for you, give it a one-page description of your business: what you offer, what you charge, how long things take, how far you travel, what your terms are and how you sound. This chapter calls that page your *studio brief*. You'll write it once, update it each season and paste it in (or save it in the tool) whenever you work on a booking.

## What goes in the brief

A good brief is short enough to read in two minutes and specific enough that a new assistant could answer a basic inquiry from it without calling you. It has eight sections.

**1. About the studio.** Who you are, where you're based, whether you work alone or with other artists, and who you serve. Two or three sentences.

**2. Services and approved rates.** Every service you sell, with its price and what's included. If something isn't on this list, the AI shouldn't offer it.

**3. Timing.** How long each service takes on a wedding morning, how long you need to set up, how much buffer you keep before the ready-by time, how many services one artist can do in a morning, and your earliest start.

**4. Travel.** Your zones or mileage rules and the point beyond which you decide case by case.

**5. Booking terms.** Your minimum spend, deposit rule, what makes a date "booked," how long a quote stays valid, when the balance is due, and where the official terms live (your agreement, not the chat).

**6. Tone of voice.** How you write: the length of your messages, how formal you are, words you use and words you'd never use.

**7. What the AI may do and what it must hand back to you.** The most important section, and the one most people skip.

**8. Privacy and placeholders.** What never goes into the tool, and the placeholders you use instead.

Here's the brief for the fictional studio used throughout this book. Read it once for the content, then once more for the style: short lines, no padding, nothing the AI has to interpret.

::: template
**STUDIO BRIEF: Linden Bridal Makeup (fictional example)**

*Version: Spring season. Last updated: [date]*

**About**
Solo bridal makeup artist. One associate artist available for larger parties at my discretion. I travel to getting-ready venues. Bridal parties, elopements and small family groups. No hair services.

**Services and approved rates**
Bridal makeup, wedding day: $220. Includes skin prep, lashes and a small touch-up kit. 60 minutes.
Attendant or family makeup (age 13 and over): $95. 40 minutes.
Junior makeup (under 13): $50. 20 minutes.
Strip lashes for an attendant: $15. Adds 5 minutes.
Bridal trial at my studio: $110. 90 minutes. Billed separately and paid when the trial is booked.

**Timing**
Setup: 15 minutes, starting when I have access to the actual makeup room.
Buffer before ready-by time: 20 minutes for up to 5 services; 30 minutes for 6 or more.
Maximum per artist per morning: 6 services.
Earliest start: 6:00 a.m. Anything earlier needs my approval and carries a $60 early-start fee per artist.

**Travel (per artist)**
Zone 1, up to 25 miles from the studio: $40.
Zone 2, 25 to 50 miles: $75.
Over 50 miles: I decide and quote personally.

**Booking terms**
Wedding-day minimum: $300 before travel.
Deposit: 25% of the wedding-day total, rounded up to the nearest $10. It is applied to the total.
A date is booked only when the agreement is accepted AND the deposit is verified in my payment system. Until then the date is not held.
Quotes are valid for 14 days.
Balance due 7 days before the wedding. Changes agreed before then are added to the balance. Services added after the balance is paid are paid on the day.
Cancellations, refunds and changes are covered by the agreement. Do not summarize or explain them. Refer the client to the agreement.

**Tone of voice**
Warm, calm, brief. First person ("I"). Use the client's first name. Plain words. At most one exclamation mark per message. No "hun," "babe" or "lovely" as a name. No emojis unless the client used them first. Messages under 150 words unless I ask for more. Sign off: [Artist first name].

**The AI may**
Use the rates and timings above. Calculate totals from them. Describe what's included. Ask the intake questions. Draft a schedule and label it "proposed." Write follow-ups. Reword my drafts.

**Hand back to me (do not decide)**
Whether a date is available. Any discount, package or price not listed. Travel over 50 miles. Starts before 6:00 a.m. Adding a second artist. Anything about refunds, cancellations or exceptions. Confirming that a booking is made or a payment received. Anything a client asks that isn't covered here.

**Privacy**
I will use a lead ID (for example, L-031) and placeholders such as [Client first name]. Never ask me for, and never include, full names, phone numbers, email addresses, venue addresses, photos, health or skin details, or payment details. If you need one of these to finish a draft, leave a bracketed gap.

**When information is missing or contradictory**
Leave a bracketed gap and list the problem under the draft. Never guess a number, date, time, link or policy.
:::

## Rules the AI may use, decisions only you make

Look again at the last four sections of the brief. They do something that most AI advice for small businesses misses: they tell the tool where its job ends.

Without that line, a chat tool will cheerfully do your thinking for you. Ask it to reply to a bride who wants a discount because her budget is tight, and it'll offer 10% off, because that's what a "helpful" reply often looks like. Ask it to answer someone who says they've paid, and it'll thank her for the payment and confirm her date.

So split your business rules into two kinds.

**Rules** are things you'd apply the same way for every client. Your attendant rate is $95. Your setup is 15 minutes. Travel within 25 miles is $40. The AI can use rules all day long, as long as it uses the exact figures you gave it.

**Decisions** are things where the answer depends on the client, the date, your diary, your energy or your judgment. Whether to take a 5:00 a.m. start for a wedding two hours away. Whether to offer a second artist. Whether to make an exception for a friend of a past bride. Whether this person, who has sent four messages without telling you her date, is worth chasing. The AI should never make these. What it can do is notice that a decision is needed and put it in front of you.

A useful test: if you'd want a new assistant to ring you before answering, it's a decision. Write it in the "Hand back to me" list.

One category deserves its own warning. Availability looks like a rule ("I'm free on the 10th") but it's always a decision, because your calendar is the only source of truth and the AI can't see it. Even if you've connected a calendar to a tool that can read it, you still look before you say yes. A date that was free when you drafted the reply might not be free when you send it, especially in peak season when two inquiries for the same Saturday can arrive within an hour.

## Writing your tone of voice

Most artists find this section the hardest, because they've never had to describe how they write. There's a quick way to do it.

Find three messages you've sent to clients that you were happy with. Choose a range: a reply to a new inquiry, a message about timing and one that handled something awkward. Remove names and details. Then paste them into the chat tool with this prompt:

::: prompt
**Copy-ready prompt**

Here are three messages I've written to clients. Describe my writing style in no more than eight short lines that another writer could follow. Cover length, formality, how I open and close, punctuation habits, and any phrases I use often. Don't flatter me. Just describe.

[Message 1]
[Message 2]
[Message 3]
:::

You'll get something like: "Short paragraphs, rarely more than three sentences. Opens with thanks and the client's name. Uses 'I'd love to' rather than 'I would be delighted.' Few exclamation marks. Closes with a clear next step." Edit it until it sounds like you, and paste it into the tone section.

Then add a short list of things you never say. Every artist has them. Some can't stand "hun." Some hate the word "flawless." Some never use "packages" because it makes their work sound like a phone contract. The AI will default to the most common phrasing in the industry, and the most common phrasing is often the one you've spent years avoiding.

## Placeholders and lead IDs

Rule two from the introduction says clients' private details stay out of the chat. Placeholders make that easy.

Give every new inquiry a lead ID as soon as it arrives. Keep it simple: L-001, L-002 and so on, or the wedding date plus a number, like 1010-1 for the first inquiry for October 10. Write the lead ID in your tracker next to the client's real name. When you talk to the AI about that booking, use only the ID.

Use a small, fixed set of placeholders so that you always know what to fill in:

- `[Client first name]` for the greeting
- `[Venue]` for the getting-ready location
- `[Secure booking link]` for your agreement and deposit link
- `[Artist first name]` for your sign-off
- `[Hair stylist]` and `[Planner]` when other suppliers are involved
- `[date]` for anything you need to look up

The habit is: draft with placeholders, review, then fill the placeholders in your email or messaging app, not in the chat tool. It takes ten seconds and it means the tool never sees anything that identifies the bride.

What if the bride's own message contains her details? Don't paste the whole message. Retell it. "Lead L-031. Bride plus four attendants, maybe. October 10. Wants everyone ready before photos, no time given. Asked for price." That's all the AI needs, and writing it takes less time than redacting a long message line by line. As a bonus, summarizing it yourself makes you notice what's missing.

## Testing the brief before you trust it

Before you use the brief on a real inquiry, test it with three awkward requests. These are the situations where a chat tool is most likely to overstep, so they show you quickly whether your "Hand back to me" list is working.

Start a new conversation, paste the brief (or open your saved project), and try each one.

**Test 1: The discount.** "Lead L-T1. Bride says her budget is $500 for herself and four bridesmaids including travel. Draft a reply." A good draft gives the correct total ($640), doesn't offer a discount, and flags that a discount is your decision. If the AI offers a lower price, tighten the wording in your brief: "Never offer or suggest a discount."

**Test 2: The early confirmation.** "Lead L-T2. Bride says 'I've just sent the deposit, so we're all booked!' Draft a reply." A good draft thanks her and says you'll confirm once you've checked the agreement and the payment. It doesn't say the date is booked. If it does, add a line to the brief: "Never state that a booking is confirmed or a payment received. I will tell you when it is."

**Test 3: The far-away wedding.** "Lead L-T3. Bride is getting ready 70 miles away and needs everyone ready by 9:00. Quote for bride and two attendants." A good draft calculates the services ($220 plus $190), leaves travel as a bracketed gap, and flags both the distance and the possible early start for you to decide. It should not invent a travel fee.

If all three pass, your brief is ready. If one fails, you've just found out on a test lead instead of on a bride's phone.

## The pre-send checklist

Even with a good brief, every draft gets a final look before it leaves your hands. Keep the check short enough to run on your phone in a minute. Six questions cover almost every mistake.

::: checklist
**Before you send anything AI helped write**

1. **Date.** Is it the right date, and have I checked my calendar today?
2. **Numbers.** Does every price, total and deposit match my rate sheet and my own arithmetic?
3. **Promises.** Does it promise anything I haven't decided, such as a hold, a discount, a time or a confirmation?
4. **Placeholders.** Have I filled every bracket, and is the client's name spelled correctly?
5. **Voice.** Does it sound like me? Have I cut anything I'd never say?
6. **Thread.** Am I replying to the right person, in the right conversation?
:::

That third question is the one that saves you. Read the draft once looking only for promises. Each time you find one, ask yourself whether you'd stand behind it if the bride screenshotted it and sent it back to you in eight months' time. Because some of them will.

## Keeping the brief up to date

The brief is a working document. Put a version label and a date at the top and change it whenever your business changes: a new rate for next season, a new travel zone because you've moved, a longer attendant time because you've added lashes as standard.

When you update rates, keep the old brief for bookings already quoted. A bride who was quoted $95 per attendant in January shouldn't be sent a draft at $105 in March because you updated the brief in between. A simple way to handle this is to note the brief version in your tracker when you send a quote, so you always know which prices apply.

It's also worth looking at the brief after every few weddings with one question: what did I have to correct in a draft this month? If you keep changing the same thing, such as removing the word "flawless," adding the balance due date or fixing a timing, put it in the brief so you never have to fix it again.

## Ten-minute action

Open a blank note and write the first draft of your own brief using the eight headings above. Don't polish it. Put in your current rates, your real service times (not the ones you wish were true) and your deposit rule. Then write your "Hand back to me" list, with at least five decisions on it.

Save it somewhere you can reach from your phone. In Chapter 2 you'll use it on a real inquiry.

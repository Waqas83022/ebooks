# 12. The discovery call and the one-page proposal

When an introduction or an outreach message works, you get a conversation. This chapter is about turning that conversation into a signed agreement, quickly and without giving away your evenings.

## Keep the call short and useful

The first conversation with a potential client is usually called a discovery call. For an evening business, it should be 20 to 30 minutes on video or the phone, booked at a time outside your working hours. Many small business owners are happy with an early evening or a Saturday morning. Use a scheduling link that only shows your business hours, so nobody books you for Tuesday at 2 p.m.

The call has one purpose: to find out whether your offer fits their problem, and if it does, to agree on the next step. It is not a free consultation, and it is not a sales pitch. It is closer to a doctor's first appointment. You ask questions, you listen, and at the end you either say "I think I can help, and here's how" or "I don't think I'm the right fit, but here's what I'd suggest."

## Ten questions that do most of the work

Adapt these to your service. You won't need all ten every time.

1. What made you get in touch now? (The trigger tells you the real problem and the urgency.)
2. How do you handle this today? Walk me through it.
3. What goes wrong? Can you give me a recent example?
4. What does that cost you, in time, money or stress?
5. What have you tried already?
6. If this were fixed, what would be different in three months?
7. Who else is involved in the decision, or in the work?
8. What would you need from me to feel confident going ahead?
9. Is there a deadline or an event this needs to fit around?
10. Have you set aside a budget for this, or would it help if I explained how I price it?

Take notes. Write down their words, especially their description of the problem and the outcome. You will use their language in the proposal, and nothing persuades a buyer like hearing their own words reflected back.

Listen more than you talk. A good rule is that the client should speak for at least two-thirds of the call. When you do talk, describe the outcome and the process in plain terms and show the sample. Don't talk about AI unless they ask, and if they ask, answer honestly using your process note from Chapter 6.

## Qualify, kindly

Not every conversation should become a project. During the call, check four things:

- Fit. Is their problem the one your offer solves? If they need something different, say so. Offering a referral to someone better suited builds goodwill.
- Scope. Can it be done within your fixed scope, or close to it? If they want ten procedures and your offer is three, propose the three first.
- Timing. Can you deliver within your capacity cap? If not, offer a start date.
- Budget. Can they pay your price? If the budget is far below it, explain what a smaller scope would look like, or part on good terms.

Watch for warning signs: a client who wants it "yesterday," who has had problems with "every freelancer" before, who wants a detailed plan before paying anything, or who hopes you'll do the work "for exposure." You will not have enough evenings to spend them on difficult clients.

## End with a clear next step

If the fit is good, close the call by saying what happens next: "Based on what you've told me, I think the Starter Process Manual fits well. I'll send you a one-page proposal by Thursday evening. If it looks right, you can approve it by email, and we'll schedule the interview."

Then do it. Sending the proposal when you said you would is the first proof that you are reliable.

## The one-page proposal

Long proposals are a waste of evening hours and rarely read. For a fixed-scope offer, one page is enough. It confirms what you heard, what you will deliver and what it costs, and it gives the client an easy way to say yes.

::: box
### One-page proposal template

**Prepared for:** client name, business, date.

**What you told me:** two or three sentences in the client's words describing the problem and what they want to be different.

**What I'll deliver:** the deliverables from your offer card, adjusted to this client (for example, "procedures for annual boiler service, drain unblocking and radiator valve replacement").

**What I need from you:** the inputs and dates.

**Timeline:** start date and delivery date.

**Revisions:** what's included.

**Not included:** the exclusions.

**How I work and handle your information:** your process and data note, two or three sentences.

**Price and payment:** the price, the payment schedule, payment method, and when invoices are due.

**To go ahead:** "Reply to this email to confirm, and I'll send the first invoice and book the interview."

**Terms:** a link to or attachment of your short terms of business.
:::

Dana wrote her proposals in about twenty minutes using this template. Ravi took slightly longer because he restated the client's must-haves precisely, which the clients found reassuring. Neither of them ever wrote more than a page.

## Terms of business

Your proposal should point to a short set of standard terms. These protect both sides and save you from inventing the rules in the middle of a disagreement. They usually cover:

- payment terms (deposit, final payment, when invoices are due, any late-payment fee);
- what happens if the client doesn't provide inputs on time (for example, the timeline moves, and after 30 days of delay the project may be closed with the deposit retained);
- revisions and changes (what's included and how additional work is priced);
- ownership (the client owns the final deliverables once paid in full; you keep the right to reuse your general methods, templates and know-how);
- confidentiality (you keep their information confidential and handle it as described in your data note);
- AI and tools (you use AI tools in the way described; client content is not used to train models where you can control that);
- liability (you are responsible for delivering what was agreed with reasonable skill and care; you are not responsible for decisions the client makes based on the deliverables, and the total liability is limited to the fee paid);
- cancellation (how either side can end the project and what is owed).

Write your terms once. A lawyer or a template from a reputable freelancers' organization is a good starting point; a one-hour lawyer review of your finished terms is money well spent. Some places also have laws protecting freelancers. New York State's Freelance Isn't Free Act, which took effect statewide in 2024, requires a written contract for freelance work worth $800 or more and requires payment within 30 days of completion unless the contract says otherwise. Illinois passed a similar law in 2023, in force since July 2024. If you work with clients in such places, your terms should meet those rules.

## Handling the price conversation

Some clients will accept the price in the proposal without comment. Others will ask for a discount. Decide in advance how you'll respond.

The best response to a discount request is to change the scope, not the price. "I can't reduce the price for the full scope, but I could do two procedures instead of three for $320." This keeps your pricing consistent and treats the client as a reasonable person with a budget, which most are.

If you are offering a pilot price in exchange for feedback and a testimonial (as described in Chapter 11), say so in the proposal, so it is clear that the lower price is temporary and has a reason.

## When they say yes

When the client confirms, move quickly. Within a day:

1. Send the deposit invoice.
2. Confirm the dates for inputs, any calls and delivery.
3. Create their project folder.
4. Add the project to your time log and capacity plan.
5. Send a short welcome note telling them what to expect and how to reach you.

Start work only when the deposit has arrived. This is normal practice for small service businesses and protects your limited hours. A client who won't pay a deposit for a small fixed-scope project is telling you something about how payment at the end will go.

## When they say no, or nothing

Many proposals get no reply. Follow up once after three or four days and once more a week later. Keep it short and friendly: "Just checking you received the proposal. Happy to answer any questions, or to adjust the scope if that would help."

If the answer is no, ask one question: "Thanks for letting me know. Would you mind telling me what made it not quite right?" The answers are some of the most useful market research you will get. Ravi learned from two polite refusals that small charities often needed a brief within a week, not two, for an upcoming board meeting. He created a faster version with a narrower scope.

# 5. Twelve services that fit an evening schedule

This chapter is a catalogue. Each entry describes a service that suits an employed person with five hours a week: what you deliver, who tends to buy it, what the AI tools do, what you must do yourself, roughly how long a first project takes, and what goes wrong.

The pilot prices are starting points for a first, deliberately limited project, the kind you sell to your first two or three clients while you learn. They are in US dollars and assume a small business buyer in a high-cost English-speaking market. Prices vary widely by country, niche and experience, so treat them as a sense of scale and test them against your own market in Chapter 13.

Read the list with your top two ideas from Chapter 4 beside you. The goal is to sharpen one of them, or to notice a better fit you had overlooked.

## 1. Process manuals and standard operating procedures

You deliver a short set of written procedures (typically three to ten) for tasks a small business does repeatedly: opening the shop, onboarding a new client, servicing a boiler, closing the month. Each one has steps, checks, owners and a simple flowchart or checklist.

Small business owners with five to fifty staff buy it, especially when they are hiring, franchising, preparing to sell or tired of training every new person themselves. AI transcribes a recorded walkthrough, drafts steps from the transcript, formats them consistently and suggests checklists. You plan the interview, ask the follow-up questions, catch missing steps and unsafe shortcuts, and make the language fit the people who will use it.

A first project of three procedures takes eight to twelve hours. Pilot price: $400–$900.

Watch for owners who cannot find time for the interview, and procedures that involve legal or safety requirements you are not qualified to judge. Dana's pilot in Chapter 8 follows this service in detail.

## 2. Meeting-to-knowledge packs

You turn a set of recorded meetings, calls or workshops into usable material: decisions and actions, a summary for people who missed it, an FAQ, a project wiki page. Small agencies, nonprofits, community organizations and teams running a one-off event or project are typical buyers.

AI does the transcription, first-pass summary and extraction of decisions and actions. You decide what matters, resolve contradictions between speakers, check names and numbers, and structure the result so people actually use it.

First project: four to eight hours. Pilot price: $250–$600.

Recording consent is the big issue. Everyone recorded must know and agree, and some jurisdictions require all-party consent. Confidential discussions should not go into tools whose data terms you have not checked.

## 3. Public research and comparison briefs

You deliver a dated, sourced brief comparing options from public information: three accounting tools for a charity, four venues for a conference, five competitors' pricing and positioning, the regulations a small business must follow to open in a new city (summarized with links, not legal advice).

Small business owners, nonprofit managers and consultants who need a researcher for a week buy these. AI searches, gathers and sorts information, drafts comparison tables and summarizes long documents. You frame the question, decide which criteria matter, verify every factual claim against a primary source, record where each fact came from and write a recommendation you can defend.

First project: six to ten hours. Pilot price: $300–$800.

AI tools state wrong facts confidently, and that is the main hazard; Ravi's pilot in Chapter 9 is built around a verification routine. Keep away from anything that would amount to regulated advice (investment, legal, medical).

## 4. Spreadsheet and reporting cleanup

You take a messy spreadsheet or set of exports and deliver a clean, structured file with formulas that work, a simple monthly report or dashboard and a one-page guide to updating it.

Owners of shops, studios, clinics, trades businesses and small online stores buy this, especially before meeting an accountant, a bank or an investor. AI can suggest formulas, explain errors, write cleanup scripts, draft the guide and spot anomalies. You understand what the numbers mean, decide what the owner needs to see, test every formula against known totals and protect the client's financial data.

First project: six to twelve hours. Pilot price: $300–$900.

Watch the data: financial records are sensitive, so use the client's own accounts or files where possible and minimize what you upload anywhere. Do not drift into bookkeeping or tax advice unless you are qualified.

## 5. Small workflow automations with a person in the loop

You set up a small automated workflow using no-code tools: a form that feeds a spreadsheet and drafts a reply, an invoice that creates a task, a new client that triggers a welcome sequence. You include a manual fallback and a one-page guide.

Independent professionals and small teams who lose time or business to slow manual handling buy it: photographers, tutors, consultants, trades, small agencies. AI can draft messages, classify enquiries, summarize forms and help you write and debug automation steps. You map the real process, decide where a person must approve, test the failure cases, set permissions carefully and hand over something the client can run without you.

First project: eight to fifteen hours. Pilot price: $500–$1,200, sometimes with a small monthly support fee.

Automations break when an app changes, and clients expect you to fix them, sometimes during working hours. Price support separately and keep a human approval step for anything sent to customers. Teresa's pilot in Chapter 10 follows this service.

## 6. Help-center and FAQ builds

You turn a business's scattered customer emails, chat logs and policies into a clear set of FAQ pages or help articles, often with suggested canned replies for common questions.

Small online shops, software startups, membership organizations and service businesses that answer the same questions every day are the buyers. AI clusters questions by topic, drafts answers from the business's own policy documents and keeps the tone consistent. You check that every answer matches actual policy, remove anything that promises more than the business delivers and get the owner's sign-off on each article.

First project: six to ten hours for fifteen to twenty-five articles. Pilot price: $350–$800.

Customer messages contain personal data; ask for exports with names and contact details removed, or remove them yourself before using any AI tool.

## 7. Onboarding and training packs

You turn existing material (old slides, a handbook, recorded explanations) into a short training pack for new staff or volunteers: a welcome guide, a first-week checklist, short quizzes, and perhaps a slide deck.

Small businesses that hire regularly, franchise owners, nonprofits with rotating volunteers and clubs buy these. AI drafts quiz questions, simplifies dense text, restructures material into lessons and suggests checklists. You decide what a new person actually needs in week one, cut what they don't, and make sure everything is accurate and matches how the organization works now.

First project: eight to twelve hours. Pilot price: $400–$1,000.

Old material is often out of date, so budget a review call with someone who knows current practice.

## 8. Product listing and catalogue cleanup

For small e-commerce sellers, you rewrite and standardize product titles, descriptions and attributes for a set of products, fix inconsistencies, and deliver a simple style guide for future listings.

Small online shops with 30 to 500 products, often written by different people over the years, are the buyers. AI drafts consistent descriptions from specifications, standardizes formats and checks for missing attributes. You verify every specification against the product, avoid claims the seller cannot support, and follow the selling platform's rules.

First project: six to ten hours for a batch of 30–50 products. Pilot price: $250–$600.

This one has a commodity risk. Generic AI listing writing is cheap and widely offered, so your value has to come from accuracy, structure and a particular niche you know (craft supplies, auto parts, specialist foods).

## 9. Proposal, tender and grant first drafts

You help a small business or nonprofit prepare a proposal, tender response or grant application: organize the requirements, draft sections from the client's own material, build a compliance checklist and produce a clean draft for the client to finalize.

Small nonprofits applying for grants and small firms bidding for contracts are the buyers. AI pulls requirements out of long tender documents, drafts sections from notes and checks the draft against the criteria. You make sure every claim is true and evidenced, keep the client's voice, and manage the checklist so nothing required is missing.

First project: eight to fifteen hours. Pilot price: $500–$1,500.

Deadlines are fixed and often tight, which is hard on a five-hour week, so take work only with a comfortable lead time. Some funders now ask whether AI was used; answer honestly and make sure the client knows. Never invent results, numbers or testimonials.

## 10. Policy and document plain-language rewrites

You rewrite dense documents (a staff handbook, terms for a membership, a parent guide, a volunteer policy) into clear language while keeping the meaning. You deliver a tracked-changes version plus a clean one.

Small organizations, schools, clubs, landlords and community groups buy it. AI drafts plain-language versions, suggests structure, flags jargon and long sentences. You check every rewritten sentence against the original, because a policy that says something different from what the organization meant is worse than a hard-to-read one, and you mark anything with legal weight for the client's lawyer to review.

First project: five to ten hours. Pilot price: $300–$700.

Do not present yourself as giving legal advice. Rewrite for clarity; leave legal judgment to lawyers.

## 11. Slide and report redesign

You take an existing deck or report and deliver a clear, consistent, readable version: restructured story, cleaner slides, consistent charts, and a template for next time.

Consultants, small agencies, founders preparing to pitch, and nonprofits preparing board or funder reports are the usual buyers. AI suggests structure and headlines, rewrites dense text for slides and generates simple diagrams or icon ideas. You decide what the audience needs to see, check every chart against its data and keep the client's brand.

First project: five to ten hours. Pilot price: $300–$800.

Scope creep is common ("while you're at it, could you also..."). Define the number of slides or pages.

## 12. Translation and localization review

If you are fluent in two languages, you review and correct AI-translated material (websites, product information, customer emails, training content) so it reads naturally and correctly for the target audience.

Small businesses entering a new language market, nonprofits serving multilingual communities, and online sellers are the buyers. AI produces the first-pass translation and keeps terminology consistent. You fix errors in meaning, tone and cultural fit, and flag anything that could cause offense or legal trouble.

First project: four to eight hours, depending on length. Pilot price: $200–$600.

Certified or legal translations require qualifications in many countries. Stick to uncertified business content unless you hold them.

## Services to avoid

Some ideas are popular with AI side-hustle courses and should be avoided by an employed person who wants a business with a future.

- Regulated advice without a license. Legal advice, tax preparation and advice, investment advice, medical or mental-health advice, immigration advice. AI makes these look easy to imitate. The rules on who may provide them exist for good reasons, and the liability is real.
- Academic ghostwriting. Writing essays or theses for students is cheating, and the student and you both carry the risk.
- Fake reviews, fake engagement or fake personas. Writing reviews for products you haven't used, or running accounts pretending to be real customers, is deceptive and, in many places, illegal. In the US, a Federal Trade Commission rule in force since 2024 specifically bans fake reviews, including AI-generated ones.
- Mass content for search rankings. Pumping out hundreds of AI pages to game search engines is a race to the bottom, and search platforms penalize it.
- Anything using personal data you do not have the right to use. Scraping contact details to sell lead lists, building profiles of people from social media, or reselling data.
- Voice or image cloning of real people without their explicit, informed consent.
- Vague "AI consulting." Promising to "transform a business with AI" without a defined deliverable leads to unhappy clients and unpaid invoices. If you want to help businesses adopt AI, package it as a specific outcome: "a set of five tested prompts and a one-page usage policy for your front-desk team," not "AI strategy."

## Choosing your one

Look at your two ideas from Chapter 4 and the catalogue above. Choose one service to pilot. If you are torn, pick the one where:

1. you are most confident you could spot a bad AI draft;
2. you can name the most potential buyers;
3. the first project fits most easily in two weeks of evening sessions.

You are not marrying this offer. You are running a ten-week test. If it fails, you will know why, and the second idea will still be there.

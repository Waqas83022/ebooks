# Questions readers ask

These are the questions that come up most often from employed people thinking about an AI-assisted service business. Each answer points back to the chapter that covers it in more depth.

## Do I need a website before I start?

No. For the first ten weeks, your sample deliverable and a one-page offer card do more than a website would, because most of your first clients will come from introductions and direct conversations (Chapter 11). A simple page with your offer, your sample and a contact form is worth building once you have two or three testimonials to put on it. Until then, a PDF of your offer card attached to an email works.

## I'm not technical. Can I still do this?

Yes, if you choose the right service. Of the twelve services in Chapter 5, only automation work (number 5) needs real technical comfort. Process manuals, research briefs, training packs, plain-language rewrites and meeting packs need you to be good at your craft and comfortable with a chat-based AI assistant, which takes a few evenings to learn. The six-part brief in Chapter 7 is the main skill.

## What if my employer finds out?

Plan for them to find out, because they may. That is why Chapter 3 asks you to check your contract, get any required approval, keep a clean line and consider telling your manager yourself. If you have done all that, your employer finding out is a non-event. If you haven't, fix it before you take your first client.

## Should I tell clients I have a full-time job?

You don't need to lead with it, but don't hide it, and don't pretend to be available when you aren't. Telling clients that you work on their project in scheduled sessions and reply within one working day sets honest expectations. Most small business owners respect it. Many started out exactly the same way.

## Can I use AI tools my employer pays for?

No. Use only tools you pay for yourself, on your own devices and accounts (Chapter 7). Using an employer's license for outside work usually breaks its IT policy and the software's license terms, and it puts your client's data inside your employer's systems.

## Will clients pay less if they know I use AI?

Some may ask. In practice, clients pay for the outcome, and a clear account of your process (the tools do the mechanical work; you check, correct and stand behind the result) usually builds trust. Chapter 14 has wording you can adapt. If a client wants AI-free work, price it for the extra hours or decline.

## What if a client asks me to do something outside my skills?

Say so, and suggest someone who can help if you know one. The fastest way to damage an evening business is to accept work you can't judge, as Jordan did in Chapter 1. Turning work down is not a failure. It protects the reputation that brings you the next client.

## How do I handle a client who is unhappy with the work?

Listen first, then sort the complaint into one of three kinds (Chapter 14). If you got something wrong, fix it promptly, whatever the revision terms say. If they want something different within the scope, use the revision round. If they want new work, price it. If the relationship breaks down entirely, offer a fair partial refund for undelivered work, collect what is owed for delivered work, and learn from it. A short, calm email helps more than a long one.

## How soon can I quit my job?

Probably not soon, and maybe never, which is fine. Chapter 18 lists six things to check before leaving: consistent demand over six to twelve months, proven economics at full-time scale, a spread of clients, a financial runway of six months or more, a clear sense of what you want, and a clean exit from your contract. Most readers of this book will be better served by a steady, well-paid side business than by a leap.

## What if I try everything and nobody buys?

Then the pilot has told you something useful. Look at the evidence (Chapter 17). If people liked your sample but didn't buy, the problem is usually the buyer, the price or the timing. If nobody responded at all, the problem is usually reach (too few personal conversations) or the offer (unclear, or solving a problem people don't feel). Change one thing, and run another six weeks. If the second pilot also fails, return to your skill inventory and choose a different idea.

## Do I need to register a company?

Usually not to begin with. In most countries you can start as an individual running a business in your own name and register with the tax authority when your income passes the relevant threshold. Chapter 15 covers the basics for the US and the UK. Check local licensing rules, and speak to an accountant once you can see your first year's numbers.

## Will AI tools make my service obsolete?

Some parts of it, yes. The parts of any service that are pure drafting and formatting will keep getting cheaper. That is why every offer in this book is built around the work AI does badly: understanding a particular client, gathering the right inputs, checking facts and fitting the result into a real business. Review your offer once a year with that in mind, and move your service toward judgment and away from typing. In the research in Chapter 1, the most experienced workers gained least from AI, because the tools mostly helped others reach the standard the experts already had. Aim to be one of the experts.
